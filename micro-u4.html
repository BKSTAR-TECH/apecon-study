4.1 Imperfectly competitive markets

Unit 3’s firm read the price off a screen. Every firm in Unit 4 writes it, and pays for writing a high one in units it does not sell. That is the only structural change, and everything below is downstream of it.

The sentence the whole unit unwinds from To sell one more unit, you have to lower the price — and lower it on every unit you were already selling. Hold that and the rest of the unit is derivable. Marginal revenue falls below price. The profit-maximizing quantity therefore lands to the left of where price would equal marginal cost. The price ends up above marginal cost, which is this unit’s definition of an inefficient market. And the gap between the quantity produced and the efficient quantity is the deadweight loss. Four results, one premise.

What counts as imperfectly competitive

Imperfectly competitive market
A market where at least one side is big enough to move the price.In real lifeThe airline flying the only direct route out of your city. It does not read a price off a screen the way a wheat farmer does; it decides one, and it can decide differently next Tuesday.Any market in which a firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve rather than a horizontal one, so its own output decision moves the price. The CED names four: monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition in product markets, and monopsony in factor markets. Notice that the list crosses the aisle deliberately. Three of the four are about a seller with power; the fourth is about a buyer with power, and the price-setting logic is the same one run backwards. Unit 5 draws the monopsony graph; what belongs here is knowing it is on the list.
Market poweralso called pricing power, price-setting power or monopoly power“Monopoly power” is the loosest of the three and the most misleading: a firm can have market power without being a monopoly, and every firm in this unit has some.
The ability to raise your price and not lose every customer.In real lifeA stadium charges $9 for a bottle of water that costs $1 outside. Nobody is forced to buy it, and plenty still do, because inside the gates there is nowhere else.The degree to which a firm can set price above marginal cost without its sales going to zero. A perfectly competitive firm has none: raise the price by a cent and it sells nothing, because a perfect substitute is one stall along. Market power is a matter of degree, not a yes-or-no - a corner shop, a branded soft drink and a patented drug all have some, in increasing order, and the source is always the same thing, an absence of close substitutes.
On the exam

A city has one bridge across its river and charges a toll. A second bridge opens two kilometres downstream, and drivers can use either. What happens to the first bridge’s market power, and why?

  1. AIt rises, because total traffic across the river has increased
  2. BIt falls, because drivers now have a close substitute for the crossing
  3. CIt is unchanged, because the first bridge still owns its own crossing
  4. DIt falls, because the toll is now regulated by the number of bridges
Answer

B. Market power comes from the absence of close substitutes, and a second crossing two kilometres away is a close substitute. The first bridge can still charge a toll, but raising it now sends drivers downstream, so the demand curve it faces has become flatter - more elastic - which is exactly what less market power looks like.

Price makeralso called price setter or price searcherThe opposite is the price TAKER of Unit 3. A price maker still cannot choose price and quantity independently - demand fixes one once it has chosen the other.
A firm that chooses its price, and pays for choosing a high one in units sold.In real lifeThe only cinema in a small town prints the ticket price on its own poster. It could print $20 instead of $12 - and then watch the Tuesday screenings empty.A firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve, so it picks a point on that curve rather than accepting a price given to it. The trap in the name is that it sounds like freedom. It is not: the firm chooses either the price or the quantity, never both, because demand fixes the other one. That is why the monopoly graph reads quantity off MR = MC and then goes up to demand for the price. The mirror term is a price taker, the perfectly competitive firm in Unit 3, whose demand curve is horizontal at the market price.
Monopsony
One buyer, many sellers - market power on the purchasing side.In real lifeA remote mining town where one company is the only employer. Nobody is stopping a worker from asking for more; there is simply nobody else in town to ask.A factor market with a single buyer. It is the fourth structure on the CED’s list of imperfectly competitive markets and the only one that is not about sellers, which is exactly why it gets dropped from revision. The logic is the monopoly’s in a mirror: to hire one more worker the monopsonist has to raise the wage, and it has to raise it for everyone already hired, so the marginal cost of labour lies above the labour supply curve just as marginal revenue lies below demand. The graph belongs to Unit 5’s factor markets; the idea belongs here, at the head of the unit, because it is the same idea.
Price above marginal cost
The test this unit uses for inefficiency, in five words.In real lifeA dose of a patented drug costs $4 to make and sells for $300. Everybody in the queue who would gladly pay $50 goes without, and making their dose would have cost $4.In an imperfectly competitive market consumers and producers respond to prices that sit above the marginal cost of production - that is, price is greater than marginal cost in an inefficient market. The parenthesis is the CED’s own and it is worth reading as a definition rather than as a remark, because it is the exact mirror of the perfectly competitive result in Unit 3, where price equals marginal cost. Every unit between the quantity produced and the quantity where P = MC is a unit somebody values above what it would cost to make and which is not made. Saying “monopolies are inefficient” without saying this is a verdict with no test attached to it.
Allocative inefficiency
The wrong quantity is produced - too little, at a price above marginal cost.In real lifeEmpty seats on a half-full flight while people who would have paid $80 stayed home, because the airline’s price was $250 and the cost of carrying one more passenger was about $30.Society’s resources are not allocated to the combination of goods people value most, because output stops where P > MC instead of where P = MC. It is a claim about which quantity is produced, not about whether the firm is wasteful in producing it - that second claim is productive inefficiency, and the two are separate charges that both happen to stick to the monopolist. The loss it causes has a name and an area on the graph: the deadweight loss.
Deadweight lossalso called welfare loss, efficiency loss or the lost gains from tradeIt is not a transfer. What the monopolist takes from buyers shows up as profit; the deadweight loss goes to nobody at all.
The gains from trade that nobody gets, because the trades never happen.In real lifeA concert that sells out at $200 while a thousand people who would have paid $70 stay home, in a hall with a thousand empty seats. Nobody captured that value; it simply is not there.The value of the mutually beneficial trades that a market failure prevents - here, every unit between the quantity produced and the quantity where price equals marginal cost. It is not a transfer: the surplus a monopolist takes from buyers by charging a high price is a transfer and shows up as profit, whereas the deadweight loss goes to nobody. On the graph it is the triangle bounded by demand, marginal cost and the vertical line at the quantity actually produced, and it is the single most-drawn area in this unit.
The fourth structure, and why it is always the one that gets dropped The CED’s list is monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition in product markets, and monopsony in factor markets. Three sellers and a buyer. A page about imperfect competition writes itself as a page about sellers, and revision written that way is missing a quarter of the opening statement. Monopsony’s graph belongs to Unit 5; what belongs here is that a single buyer has market power for the same reason a single seller does, and pays for using it the same way — by having to raise the wage for everyone already hired, not just for the next person through the door.
Price taker against price setter
Demand curve it facesRelationship of P and MRWhat it choosesEfficiency at its own optimum
Price taker (Unit 3)Horizontal at the market priceP = MRQuantity only; the price is givenP = MC, allocatively efficient
Price makerDownward-slopingP > MRA point on the curve: price OR quantity, not bothP > MC, allocatively inefficient
The line that separates themThe slope of the demand curve the individual firm faces, and nothing else. Make it horizontal and P = MR, so MR = MC is the same equation as P = MC and the efficient outcome comes out of pure self-interest. Tilt it downward and marginal revenue drops away from price, MR = MC now sits to the left of P = MC, and the same self-interested rule produces too little output at too high a price. Every other difference between Units 3 and 4 is downstream of that one tilt.

The line that separates themTwo questions sort all four, in this order. Is entry blocked? If yes, long-run profit can survive and you are in the bottom half of the table. If no, entry competes profit away to zero and you are in the top half. Then: is the product identical? Identical with free entry is perfect competition; differentiated with free entry is monopolistic competition. Everything else in the row follows from those two answers, including the shape of the demand curve - and the demand curve is what decides whether P = MR or P > MR, which decides everything about the graph.

Barriers to entry

Barriers are what let a structure survive. Without them, profit calls in entrants, entrants compete the profit away, and any market power a firm had is temporary — which is exactly the story Unit 3 tells and exactly the story 4.4 tells again.

Barriers to entryalso called entry barriers or obstacles to entry
Whatever stops a profitable market from attracting new firms.In real lifeYou cannot answer a shortage of a patented medicine by making it in your kitchen, however profitable it looks. The law will stop you before chemistry does.Incentives to enter an industry may be mitigated by barriers to entry, and the CED names three kinds: high fixed or start-up costs, legal barriers to entry, and exclusive ownership of key resources. Three, not one - a page that offers patents and stops has covered a third of the statement while reading as though it covered the lot. What barriers do is not raise the price directly; it is to keep economic profit from being competed away, which is why the monopolist in this unit can still be earning profit in the long run while the firm in Unit 3 cannot.
On the exam

Four industries are described. In which is the barrier to entry an example of exclusive ownership of a key resource rather than a legal or a cost barrier?

  1. AA pharmaceutical firm holding a 20-year patent on a drug
  2. BA firm that has bought the only deposit of a rare mineral in the country
  3. CA rail operator whose track cost billions to lay
  4. DA taxi company operating under a licence the city issues to only 200 vehicles
Answer

B. Owning the only source of the input is exclusive ownership of a key resource. A patent (A) and a capped licence (D) are legal barriers - the state creates them and could remove them. The track (C) is a high fixed cost barrier: entry is not forbidden, just ruinously expensive.

High fixed or start-up costs
Entry is legal and nobody can afford it.In real lifeAnyone may lay a second set of water pipes under your city. Nobody does, because the digging costs more than the water will ever be worth.The first of the CED’s three barriers, and the one that produces a natural monopoly rather than a granted one. When a huge fixed cost is spread over output, average total cost keeps falling as the firm grows, so whoever is already large can undercut anyone who starts small. Nothing forbids entry; the arithmetic does the forbidding.
Legal barriers to entry
The state grants one firm the right and refuses it to others.In real lifeA patent on a new battery chemistry. For twenty years, copying it is not competition, it is an infringement.Patents, copyrights, exclusive licences, franchises granted by a government, and quotas on the number of operators. The mark of a legal barrier is that it is created and removable by policy - which is why a very common exam stem is a patent expiring, and the answer is that generics enter, the demand curve the original firm faces flattens and its economic profit falls toward zero.
Exclusive ownership of a key resource
One firm owns the only source of what everyone would need.In real lifeA single spring supplies the mineral water a whole region’s bottlers want, and one company owns the land it rises on.Control of an input nobody can produce or substitute for: an ore body, a spring, a wavelength, the one usable harbour. It is the rarest of the three barriers in the real world and the commonest one for students to miss, because the other two have obvious names. The test is whether the obstacle sits in the supply chain rather than in the law or in the balance sheet.
Concentration ratio
The share of an industry’s output that its largest few firms produce.In real lifeIf the four biggest carriers fly 88 percent of a country’s passengers, the remaining dozen airlines are splitting the last eighth between them.Usually the four-firm ratio: the percentage of industry sales made by its four largest firms. The CED does not ask you to compute one, but stems use it as shorthand for a market structure, and reading it is free marks. A high ratio - the four largest producing most of the output - describes an oligopoly. A low one, with the largest firm at a couple of percent, describes perfect competition or monopolistic competition, and what separates those two is not the ratio at all but whether the product is differentiated.

The line that separates themAsk where the obstacle sits: in the balance sheet, in the statute book, or in the supply chain. The CED names all three and an answer that offers only patents has stated a third of the point. The one that behaves differently is the first - a cost barrier is the barrier that creates a natural monopoly, and it is the only one of the three that would still be there if every law were repealed and every resource were common property.
Trap — “the only firm” is not the definition Being the only seller today makes a firm a monopoly only if something stops a second one appearing tomorrow. The CED states the cause rather than the count: a monopoly exists because of barriers to entry. This is why the exam’s monopoly stems always plant a barrier in the first sentence — a patent, an exclusive licence, the only deposit of an ore, a utility’s network — and why a stem that describes one seller with no barrier at all is describing a firm about to have company.

4.1a Marginal revenue, and why it falls below price

This is a one-idea section, and the idea is worth more marks than any single graph in the unit. Learn it here and the monopoly, the discriminating monopolist and the monopolistically competitive firm all draw themselves.

The demand curve a price setter faces
Downward-sloping, so selling more means charging less.In real lifeA food truck with a unique menu can fill its lunch queue at $9 or run half empty at $14. Both are available to it; neither can be had at the other’s quantity.In an imperfectly competitive market the individual firm’s demand curve is the downward-sloping market demand curve (for a monopoly) or a downward-sloping slice of it (for the other three structures). That is the whole structural difference from Unit 3, where the individual firm’s demand curve is horizontal at the market price. Everything else in this unit is a consequence: because the curve slopes down, price and marginal revenue part company, and because they part company, price ends up above marginal cost.
Marginal revenue (MR)
What one more unit adds to revenue - less than its price, once the curve slopes down.In real lifeA stall selling 20 mugs at $74 drops the price to $58 and shifts 40. It takes $1,160 from the twenty extra mugs and gives up $16 on each of the twenty it was already selling, so those twenty units really brought in $840 - forty-two dollars each, not the fifty-eight on the price tag.The change in total revenue from selling one more unit. In an imperfectly competitive output market, holding everything else constant, a firm must lower price to sell additional units - and it must lower it on every unit, not only on the extra one. So the extra unit’s contribution is its price minus the price cut suffered on all the units already being sold, which makes MR < P at every positive quantity. On a straight-line demand curve MR starts at the same vertical intercept and falls at twice the slope, so it hits the quantity axis at half the quantity demand does. This one sentence is the engine of the unit; the monopoly graph is its picture.

The table shows the demand schedule facing a firm with market power. What is the marginal revenue of the third unit?

  1. A$32
  2. B$26
  3. C$20
  4. D$96
Answer

C. Work in total revenue, never in price. Two units at $38 is $76; three units at $32 is $96. The third unit added $20, not the $32 it sold for, because taking the price from $38 to $32 gave up $6 on each of the two units already being sold: 32 − 12 = 20. Choosing $32 is reading the price and calling it marginal revenue, which is the error this table exists to catch; $96 is the total revenue rather than the change in it.

Total revenue (TR)
Price times quantity - and it does not rise forever as output grows.In real lifeA ferry filling more seats by cutting fares takes more money at first and then less, because at some point the fare is falling faster than the passengers are arriving.P × Q. On a straight-line demand curve total revenue rises while marginal revenue is positive, peaks exactly where MR = 0, and falls once MR is negative. Two exam facts fall straight out of that. First, the demand curve is elastic above the midpoint and inelastic below it, so a price cut raises revenue in the top half and lowers it in the bottom half. Second, a profit-maximising firm never produces where MR is negative: marginal cost is always positive, so MR = MC can never be solved down there. A monopolist therefore always operates on the elastic part of its demand curve.
On the exam

A single-price monopolist finds that it is currently producing on the inelastic portion of its demand curve. What should it do to increase profit?

  1. ARaise the price and sell less, because revenue rises and costs fall
  2. BLower the price and sell more, because revenue rises
  3. CKeep output where it is; the elasticity of demand does not affect profit
  4. DRaise the price and sell more, because demand is inelastic
Answer

A. On the inelastic stretch marginal revenue is negative, so the last units are subtracting from revenue while still costing something to make. Cutting output raises total revenue and lowers total cost at the same time, so profit rises on both sides. The firm keeps cutting until it is back on the elastic stretch, which is why an unregulated monopolist is never found operating in the inelastic range.

Worked example — marginal revenue from a demand schedule Five units, and the price the firm must charge to sell each quantity:
1 unit at $70 — total revenue $70. Marginal revenue of the first unit: $70.
2 units at $60 — total revenue $120. The second unit added $60 − $10 = $50.
3 units at $50 — total revenue $150. The third added $50 − $20 = $30.
4 units at $40 — total revenue $160. The fourth added $40 − $30 = $10.
5 units at $30 — total revenue $150. The fifth added −$10. Revenue went down.
Two habits come out of this. Work in total revenue and subtract; never read the price and call it marginal revenue. And notice how fast MR falls — $70, $50, $30, $10, −$10, dropping $20 each time while price drops $10. That is the “twice the slope” rule, in numbers, before it is a line on a graph.
Two facts that fall straight out, and are examined on their own
MR = 0is where total revenue is at its maximum
MR < 0is the inelastic range, where no firm ever produces
Marginal cost is positive, so MR = MC has no solution below the axis
A profit-maximizing firm with market power is therefore always operating on the elastic portion of its demand curve. A stem that puts one in the inelastic range is telling you the firm is doing the wrong thing, and the answer is that it should raise the price and sell less — which raises revenue and lowers cost at the same time.
Trap — the marginal revenue of the FIRST unit MR and D share the same vertical intercept, and that is not a drawing convention. Selling the first unit discounts nothing, because there are no earlier units to discount. Every unit after it discounts everything before it, which is why the two lines separate immediately and keep separating. If you are ever unsure whether MR should start at the same place as demand, ask what the first unit gives up: nothing.

4.2 Monopoly

The monopoly graph is the most-drawn figure in AP Microeconomics, and almost every mark lost on it is lost in the same two places: reading the price off the wrong curve, and forgetting that the profit-maximizing rule never changed.

Monopoly
One firm is the industry, and something keeps every other firm out.In real lifeThe water utility for your city. There is no second tap you could subscribe to instead, and it is not because rivals never thought of it.A single seller of a product with no close substitutes. The CED states the cause rather than the count: a monopoly exists because of barriers to entry. Being the only firm today is not a monopoly if anyone could open tomorrow - the profit would pull them in and it would be gone. Because entry is blocked, a monopoly can keep positive economic profit in the long run, which is the single sharpest contrast with everything in Unit 3 and with monopolistic competition in 4.4.
Single-price monopolist
A monopolist that must charge everybody the same price.In real lifeA cinema with one price on the board for the evening screening. It cannot quietly charge the enthusiast $30 and the undecided student $8; whatever it prints, both pay.The default assumption behind the monopoly graph, and it is an assumption rather than a fact about monopolies. Because one price has to be charged to all buyers, cutting the price to reach one more buyer costs revenue on everyone already served - which is what pushes marginal revenue below price, and therefore what creates the deadweight loss. Topic 4.3 takes the assumption away and the deadweight loss goes with it, so remembering that the graph says single-price is what keeps 4.2 and 4.3 from contradicting each other.
The profit-maximizing rule (MR = MC)
Same rule as perfect competition: produce where marginal revenue meets marginal cost.In real lifeA print shop keeps accepting orders while each extra job brings in more than it costs to run, and stops on the job where the two are level. It does not matter whether it is the only print shop in town.In a monopoly the equilibrium, profit-maximizing quantity is found by equating marginal revenue to marginal cost. This is the half of the statement students throw away, and it is worth more than the other half: the rule has not changed. What changed is the marginal revenue curve - in Unit 3, P = MR, so MR = MC and P = MC were the same equation; here they are not. Learn the rule once and apply it to whichever MR curve the market structure gives you.
The monopoly price
Found by going UP from the MR = MC quantity to the demand curve.In real lifeA firm works out that 400 units is where the last unit’s revenue matches its cost. It then asks what buyers will pay for 400 units - not what the 400th unit added to revenue.The price charged is read off the demand curve at the profit-maximizing quantity, and it is greater than the marginal cost of that unit. Reading the price off the MR curve instead is the most common wrong answer on this graph in every released paper, and it is why the atlas figure draws the step up explicitly. Two curves, two jobs: MR = MC gives the quantity, demand gives the price.
On the exam

In the figure, a single-price monopolist maximizes profit. Which pair correctly describes how its price and quantity are found?

  1. AQuantity where MR = MC; price read off the MR curve at that quantity
  2. BQuantity where MR = MC; price read off the demand curve at that quantity
  3. CQuantity where demand meets MC; price read off the demand curve there
  4. DQuantity where demand meets MC; price read off the MR curve there
Answer

B. The rule picks the quantity and demand prices it. Option A is the classic error and produces a price equal to marginal cost, which no monopolist would charge. Options C and D find the allocatively efficient quantity, which is where a competitive industry with these costs would end up - larger output and a lower price than the monopolist chooses.

Productive inefficiency
Not producing at the bottom of the average-total-cost curve.In real lifeA factory running one shift in a building sized for two. Nothing is being wasted hour by hour; the building is simply spread over fewer units than it could be.Producing at an output where average total cost is above its minimum - so each unit costs more to make than it needed to. It is a different charge from allocative inefficiency, and this unit convicts the monopolist of both: output sits to the left of minimum ATC (productive) and price sits above marginal cost (allocative). Keeping them apart matters because a question can ask for one and be answered with the other, and because in 4.4 the monopolistically competitive firm in long-run equilibrium is productively inefficient while earning exactly zero profit.
How to read the monopoly graph, in order
  1. Find where MR crosses MC. Drop straight down. That is the quantity, and it is the only thing this crossing tells you.
  2. Go straight up from that crossing to the demand curve. Read across to the price axis. That is the price.
  3. For profit, go up from the same quantity to ATC and read that height too. Profit per unit is the gap between price and ATC; total profit is that gap times the quantity.
  4. For the deadweight loss, find where MC crosses demand — the allocatively efficient quantity — and shade the triangle back to the quantity the firm chose.

The line that separates themThe comparison is only meaningful because the costs are held identical - the exam always says “with the same demand and cost curves”, and the atlas draws both firms from one cost function so the two pictures really are comparable. Given that, the monopoly restricts output to raise price: less output, higher price, every time. Do not add “and higher costs” - nothing in the model says a monopolist is a worse producer, and the whole loss is caused by the quantity it chooses, not by the way it makes things.

The line that separates themOne is about marginal cost and one is about average cost, and a question that asks for one is not answered by the other. Allocative is about how much gets made; productive is about how cheaply each unit is made. A single-price monopoly fails both. A monopolistically competitive firm in long-run equilibrium also fails both, while earning zero profit. A perfectly competitive firm in long-run equilibrium passes both, which is the entire case for that market structure.
Trap — the cost change that does nothing A rise in fixed cost — the rent goes up, the licence fee rises — changes average total cost and does not change marginal cost. MR = MC is therefore unmoved, so output and price do not change and profit falls by exactly the extra cost. A rise in marginal cost — a per-unit tax, a dearer input, a higher wage per unit of output — shifts MC up, moves the MR = MC crossing left, and so lowers output and raises price. Sorting a stem’s cost change into fixed or marginal answers the question before any reasoning about monopoly begins.
Worked example — is this firm doing the right thing? A firm with market power sells baskets at $50 each. At its current output, marginal revenue is $27 and marginal cost is $34.
The last basket brought in $27 and cost $34 to make, so it lost $7. Producing it was a mistake, and so was the one before it if the same is true there.
The firm should reduce output, which moves it back up its demand curve to a higher price, until MR has risen to meet MC.
Notice what the $50 price never entered. The rule compares MR with MC; the price is what you read off demand afterwards, and comparing $50 with $34 and concluding “each basket is profitable” is the trap the numbers are built around.

4.2a Natural monopoly and its regulation

A natural monopoly is not a big firm. It is a market whose cost curve makes one firm cheaper than two — and that single fact is what turns the usual policy advice inside out.

Natural monopoly
One firm can serve the whole market more cheaply than two could.In real lifeThe pipes under your street. Two competing water networks would mean two sets of pipes, twice the digging and the same water.A market in which long-run economies of scale for a single firm exist throughout the entire effective demand of the product: average total cost is still falling where the demand curve runs out. Read that carefully, because the exam tests the boundary. It is not “a very large firm” and it is not “a firm with high fixed costs” on its own - it is that ATC is still declining at every quantity anyone would buy, which means splitting the market between two firms would raise the cost of every unit. On the graph the tell is an ATC curve sloping downward across the whole picture, with marginal cost below it everywhere.
Economies of scale
Bigger output, lower average cost.In real lifeThe first copy of a piece of software costs a year of salaries; the millionth costs a download. The average cost per copy falls the whole way.The falling stretch of the long-run average total cost curve, where spreading a large fixed cost and specialising the work make each unit cheaper. Unit 3 met the idea as one phase of a U-shaped LRATC; here it is the whole picture, because a natural monopoly is defined by scale economies that never run out within the market. Note the direction of the argument: the barrier to entry in this case is not a law and not a resource, it is the cost curve itself.
Fair-return pricealso called average-cost pricing, the break-even price or the normal-profit priceA stem that says the regulator lets the firm earn “only a normal profit” is naming this price without using any of these words.
Regulate the price down to where demand meets ATC: zero economic profit.In real lifeA utility commission that lets the electricity company charge enough to cover its costs and a normal return on capital, and not a cent more.The regulated price set where the demand curve crosses average total cost. Because P = ATC, the firm earns zero economic profit - it covers all its costs, including a normal return, so it stays in business without a subsidy. Output is higher than the unregulated monopoly’s and the deadweight loss is smaller, but price still exceeds marginal cost, so some inefficiency remains. It is the regulator’s workable compromise, and the exam names it by the arithmetic rather than the title: “the price at which the firm earns only a normal profit”.
Socially optimal pricealso called marginal-cost pricing or the allocatively efficient priceAlso called the “efficient” price, which is true and is exactly why it is worth remembering that it puts a natural monopoly out of business.
Price where demand meets marginal cost - efficient, and a loss for the firm.In real lifeCharging what the next litre of water actually costs to pump. The bill would be tiny, and it would not come close to paying for the pipes.The regulated price set where the demand curve crosses marginal cost, so P = MC and allocative efficiency is achieved. This is the one graph in the course where the efficient answer is not the recommended one on its own: in a natural monopoly ATC is falling everywhere, which forces MC to lie below ATC everywhere, so a price equal to marginal cost is below average total cost and the firm loses money on every unit. Without a per-unit subsidy it exits. The exam asks this as “what happens to the firm if the regulator sets the allocatively efficient price”, and the answer is that it makes a loss.
On the exam

A regulator sets a natural monopoly’s price where the demand curve crosses marginal cost. Which of the following describes the outcome?

  1. AThe firm earns zero economic profit and output is allocatively efficient
  2. BThe firm earns a positive economic profit because output has risen
  3. CThe firm incurs a loss, because average total cost exceeds price at that output
  4. DThe firm produces less than it did unregulated, so the deadweight loss grows
Answer

C. A natural monopoly has economies of scale throughout, so average total cost is falling everywhere, and a falling average always has marginal below it. Pricing at marginal cost therefore prices below average total cost, and the firm loses money on every unit. The outcome is allocatively efficient and unsustainable at once, which is why this price comes with a subsidy or does not come at all. Option A describes fair-return pricing, which is a different point on the demand curve.

Why the efficient price bankrupts the firm A falling average always has its marginal below it. If your average is coming down, the next item must be pulling it down, so it must be below the average. A natural monopoly has ATC falling everywhere, so MC lies under ATC everywhere. Setting price equal to marginal cost therefore sets price below average total cost, and a firm charging less per unit than it costs per unit is losing money on every one of them. Efficiency and solvency cannot both be had here without money from outside — a per-unit subsidy — and that is not a quirk of one drawing, it is the arithmetic of averages.

4.3 Price discrimination

This topic contains the result students refuse to believe on first reading: a monopolist that charges every buyer a different price is allocatively efficient. It is also the topic where efficiency and fairness have to be kept in separate hands, because here they point opposite ways.

Price discrimination
Charging different buyers different prices for the same good.In real lifeTwo people in adjacent seats on the same flight, one who booked in March for $180 and one who booked on Thursday for $610. Identical seat, identical journey.A firm with market power charging different prices for the same product where the difference is not a difference in cost. The CED’s wording gives both motives: to increase its profits and to capture additional consumer surplus - and they are the same act seen from the two sides of the transaction, since the surplus the buyer keeps under one price is exactly what the seller takes under many. It works only under certain conditions, which is examined as often as the definition: market power, the ability to identify or separate buyers by willingness to pay, and the ability to stop resale.
Preventing resale
If the cheap buyer can sell to the dear one, the scheme collapses.In real lifeA cinema ticket at the student price is checked against a student card at the door. A bag of rice at a bulk discount is not, which is why rice is not price discriminated.The condition students forget, and the one the exam most often tests as “which of the following is necessary”. If buyers charged the low price can resell to buyers who were to be charged the high one, the arbitrage drags every price back to one price and the firm is a single-price monopolist again. This is why discrimination is common in services - a haircut, a flight, a seat, a night in a ward - which cannot be handed on, and rare in storable, transportable goods.
Separating buyers by willingness to pay
The seller must be able to tell the eager buyer from the reluctant one.In real lifeA railway that sells a cheap ticket only if you book fourteen days ahead. Nobody is asked how much they want the trip; the booking date sorts them.The firm needs some observable signal that correlates with willingness to pay - age, a student card, the date of purchase, a coupon that only the price-sensitive bother to clip, the border between two countries. It follows that the higher price goes to the group with the less elastic demand: those buyers stay when the price rises, so raising it on them adds revenue. That direction is a favourite one-line exam question and it is the direction people guess wrong.
Perfect price discriminationalso called first-degree price discrimination or perfect discriminationTextbooks number the degrees; the AP exam does not, and asks about the first degree only.
Every buyer pays exactly their own maximum, so output is efficient and the surplus is all the seller’s.In real lifeA market trader who haggles individually with every customer and, impossibly well informed, guesses each one’s limit exactly. Everyone who values the good above its cost goes home with one, and nobody feels they got a bargain.With perfect price discrimination a monopolist produces the quantity where price equals marginal cost, just as a competitive market would, but it extracts all economic surplus associated with its product and eliminates all deadweight loss. Read that as two claims, because most students believe only the second. The first is the counter-intuitive one: the demand curve becomes the marginal revenue curve, since selling one more unit no longer means cutting the price on the units before it, so the firm keeps producing all the way to P = MC. The result is allocatively efficient - and consumer surplus is zero. Efficiency and fairness are different questions, and this is the figure that proves it.
On the exam

A single-price monopolist becomes able to practise perfect price discrimination. Compared with before, its output, its consumer surplus and the deadweight loss will be, respectively:

  1. AHigher, zero, and zero
  2. BHigher, higher, and zero
  3. CUnchanged, zero, and unchanged
  4. DLower, zero, and higher
Answer

A. Output rises to the quantity where price equals marginal cost, the same quantity a competitive industry would produce, because the firm no longer gives up revenue on earlier units when it serves one more buyer. Every buyer pays their own maximum, so consumer surplus is zero. And since every unit worth making is made, no gains from trade are left unrealised and the deadweight loss is zero. Efficient allocation, with the entire surplus going to the seller.

Consumer surplus captured
What buyers would have kept, moving across to the seller.In real lifeYou would have paid $90 for the concert and the ticket was $60, so you kept $30. Under personalised pricing the site charges you $90 and that $30 becomes the seller’s.The transfer that price discrimination is for. It is worth naming separately from the deadweight loss, because they behave in opposite directions here: as discrimination becomes more precise, the transfer grows and the deadweight loss shrinks. That is why “price discrimination hurts consumers” and “price discrimination is efficient” are both true and are not in conflict - one is a statement about who holds the surplus, the other about how much of it exists.

Which market gets the higher price The one with the less elastic demand. Those buyers stay when the price rises — that is what inelastic means — so raising the price on them adds revenue. The elastic group would leave, so they get the discount. The tension the CED sets up, and how to hold both halves Topic 4.1 defines an inefficient market as one where price is greater than marginal cost. Topic 4.3 then presents a monopolist who prices at marginal cost and destroys no surplus at all. Read carelessly, the unit says monopoly is inefficient and monopoly is efficient.
Both are right, and the reconciliation is worth stating outright: the deadweight loss is an artefact of the single price, not of market power as such. It exists because reaching one more buyer means cutting the price for everyone. Remove that constraint and the loss goes with it — and what replaces it is not a fairer outcome but a total transfer of the surplus to the seller. Efficient and unfair are answers to different questions, and this figure is where the course proves it.

4.4 Monopolistic competition

Monopolistic competition borrows one property from each of its neighbours: a downward-sloping demand curve from monopoly, free entry from perfect competition. Nearly every exam question about it is a question about which half you are being asked for.

Monopolistic competition
Many firms, easy entry, and a product each one has made slightly its own.In real lifeThe restaurants in any city. Dozens of them, anyone can open one, and yet no two are interchangeable - which is why one can raise its prices a little and keep most of its tables full.A market with many firms selling differentiated products and free entry and exit. The name is the definition: a little bit monopoly, because differentiation gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve of its own; mostly competition, because entry is free. In the short run a firm may earn positive, negative or zero economic profit. In the long run, free entry and exit drive profits to zero. It is the structure that borrows one property from each of the other two, which is exactly why exam questions come in the form “which is true of BOTH monopolistic competition and X”.
Product differentiationalso called differentiated products or non-price competition“Non-price competition” is the wider name for competing on anything except price - branding, service, location, opening hours - and advertising is its commonest form.
Making your version distinguishable, so buyers do not treat it as identical.In real lifeTwo coffee shops on the same street sell the same beans at different prices and both stay busy - one has the sofas, the other the window.Any real or perceived difference between one firm’s product and its rivals’: recipe, branding, location, service, packaging, opening hours. Its consequence is the only thing the exam cares about - a differentiated product means the firm faces a downward-sloping rather than a horizontal demand curve, so it has some market power. The more substitutable the rivals’ products are, the flatter (more elastic) that curve, which is why a question about a firm losing only some of its customers after a price rise is a question about differentiation.
Advertising
How the product gets differentiated, not just how it gets known.In real lifeTwo nearly identical energy drinks, one of which sponsors a sports team. The liquid did not change; the reason someone reaches for that can did.The CED states it as a fact about the structure: firms in monopolistic competition typically use advertising as a means of differentiating their product. It is worth taking that at its word rather than treating advertising as mere publicity, because the exam asks what advertising is for. Successful advertising shifts the firm’s demand curve right and makes it less elastic - more buyers, and buyers less inclined to leave over a price rise - at the cost of raising average total cost. It is also the clearest example of non-price competition, which is what firms do when they would rather not cut prices.
Long-run equilibrium in monopolistic competition
Demand ends up just touching ATC: zero economic profit, and a tangency rather than a crossing.In real lifeA neighbourhood with a queue outside its one good bakery. Two more open within a year, the queue divides three ways, and eventually none of them is doing better than an ordinary living.Free entry and exit drive profits to zero in the long run. Positive profit attracts entrants, each entrant takes customers from the firms already there, and every incumbent’s demand curve shifts left and becomes more elastic until it just touches average total cost at the profit-maximizing quantity. The picture is a tangency, and drawing a demand curve that cuts through ATC is the examined error: a curve that cuts leaves a stretch where P > ATC, which is profit, which contradicts free entry. Same destination as perfect competition - zero economic profit - reached the same way, but the firm arrives at it above minimum ATC rather than at the bottom.
Excess capacityalso called producing below efficient scale or operating below minimum-ATC output
Producing less than the output that would minimise average total cost.In real lifeEvery hairdresser on the street has a chair free most of Tuesday. None of them is failing; there are simply more salons than the street’s hair needs to keep them all full.The output level in monopolistic competition is smaller than the output level needed to minimize average total costs, creating excess capacity. It follows from the tangency: a downward-sloping demand curve can only touch a U-shaped ATC on its falling stretch, which is to the left of the minimum. The firm could produce more cheaply per unit by producing more, and it will not, because selling more would mean cutting the price. This is what society pays for variety, and the exam asks it as “why do monopolistically competitive firms have excess capacity in long-run equilibrium”.
Allocative inefficiency in monopolistic competition
Even at zero profit, price still sits above marginal cost.In real lifeThe restaurant that would happily serve one more table at the price it charges, and does not, because filling that table would mean a cheaper menu for everyone.In long-run equilibrium the price is greater than marginal cost, creating allocative inefficiency. This is the result students find hardest to hold alongside “zero economic profit”, because zero profit sounds like the competitive outcome and in one respect it is. But zero profit is a statement about P against ATC, and efficiency is a statement about P against MC. A monopolistically competitive firm satisfies the first and fails the second, which is precisely what perfect competition does not do: there, P = MC = minimum ATC, and all three coincide.
On the exam

Which of the following is true of a profit-maximizing monopolistically competitive firm in long-run equilibrium?

  1. APrice equals marginal cost and the firm earns zero economic profit
  2. BPrice equals average total cost and exceeds marginal cost
  3. CPrice equals minimum average total cost and marginal revenue equals price
  4. DPrice exceeds average total cost, so a positive economic profit persists
Answer

B. Free entry drives economic profit to zero, so P = ATC - that is the tangency. But the firm still faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so marginal revenue is below price and the MR = MC quantity leaves P above MC. Option C describes perfect competition, where the tangency happens at the bottom of ATC and P = MR. Option D contradicts free entry.

Five claims in one CED statement, and a page can state two and look finished
  1. Short-run economic profit can be positive, negative or zero.
  2. Firms typically use advertising to differentiate the product.
  3. Free entry and exit drive profits to zero in the long run.
  4. Output is smaller than the output that minimises average total cost — excess capacity.
  5. Price is greater than marginal cost — allocative inefficiency.
Drawing the tangency and saying “zero profit” states the third. It is the last two that separate this structure from perfect competition, and they are where the marks are.

Trap — zero profit is not efficiency Zero economic profit is a statement about price against average total cost. Allocative efficiency is a statement about price against marginal cost. A monopolistically competitive firm in long-run equilibrium satisfies the first and fails the second, and holding both at once is what the “which is true of BOTH” stems are testing. Worked example — is this firm in long-run equilibrium? A firm in a monopolistically competitive market produces 100 units, sells them at $50, and its average total cost at 100 units is $50.
Price equals average total cost, so economic profit is zero — consistent with long-run equilibrium.
Does that make it efficient? No. It is profit-maximizing, so MR = MC at 100 units; and because demand slopes down, MR is below $50. So MC is below $50 too, which means P > MC: allocative inefficiency, at zero profit.
And because the tangency is on the falling part of ATC, average total cost would be lower at some output above 100. That gap is the excess capacity. One firm, zero profit, and two efficiency failures.

4.5 Oligopoly, collusion and cartels

Oligopoly is the one structure defined by a relationship rather than by a count. Few firms is the symptom; watching each other is the disease, and it is why this topic needs a table of payoffs instead of a graph.

Oligopoly
A few firms, high barriers, and each one watching the others.In real lifeThe two or three mobile networks in most countries. When one launches an unlimited plan on a Monday, the others have answered it by Friday.An inefficient market structure with high barriers to entry, where there are few firms acting interdependently. The count is the symptom; interdependence is the definition, and it is what nothing else in this course has needed. In every other structure a firm reads the market and optimises against it; here a firm has to anticipate a rival who is anticipating it back, which is why the tool for this topic is a payoff matrix rather than a graph.
Mutual interdependence
What is best for me depends on what you decide to do.In real lifeTwo petrol stations facing each other across a junction. Neither posts a price in the morning without looking at the other’s sign.The defining characteristic of oligopoly: each firm’s best action depends on the actions of the others, and each knows the others are reasoning the same way. A stem asking which market structure features firms that “recognise their mutual interdependence” is asking for oligopoly, every time. It is also the reason oligopoly has no single graph the way monopoly does - the outcome depends on the rivals’ choices, so what the model produces is a set of payoffs rather than a curve.
Collusion
Rivals agreeing to act as one firm instead of competing.In real lifeTwo builders bidding for the same council contract who quietly agree which of them will bid low this time. The council believes it ran an auction.An agreement among firms to restrict output or fix prices, so that between them they reach the monopoly outcome and split the monopoly profit. Firms in an oligopoly have every incentive to collude, because the joint payoff at the cooperative outcome is larger than the joint payoff at the competitive one. It is generally illegal, and the agreement need not be written or even spoken - matching a rival’s announced price increase week after week is tacit collusion, and price leadership is its usual form.
Cartel
A formal collusive agreement - a group of firms behaving as one monopolist.In real lifeAn association of oil-exporting countries that meets to set production quotas, and whose members are regularly accused by each other of pumping over their quota.The organised form of collusion: members agree output quotas or a price and share the profit that results. Firms have an incentive to collude and form cartels - and the same self-interest that makes joining attractive makes cheating attractive the moment the agreement holds, because at the cartel’s high price any one member gains by quietly selling extra. That is why cartels are unstable, and it is the same structure as the prisoner’s dilemma in 4.5’s game theory.
Cheating on a collusive agreement
Undercutting the deal you signed, because at the deal’s price it pays to.In real lifeA quota-bound exporter that ships a few extra cargoes while prices are high. Every member reasons the same way, and the price the deal was protecting falls.Oligopolists have difficulty achieving the monopoly outcome for reasons similar to those that prevent players from reaching a cooperative outcome in the Prisoner’s Dilemma. The cartel price sits above every member’s marginal cost, so each member individually profits by selling more than agreed - and when all of them do it, output rises and the price falls back. The exam pairs this with the honest conclusion the CED draws next: even so, prices are generally higher and quantities lower under oligopoly than under perfect competition. Cartels fail to reach the monopoly outcome; they do not fail all the way back to the competitive one.
Price leadership
One firm moves its price and the others follow, with nothing agreed out loud.In real lifeOne airline raises its baggage fee on a Tuesday and within a fortnight every competitor charges the same. No meeting was held.A pattern in which a dominant firm changes its price and rivals match it, producing something close to the collusive outcome without an agreement anyone could be prosecuted for. It appears in stems alongside collusion and price wars as behaviour “usually observed in” oligopoly, and the answer is oligopoly because both need few enough firms to watch each other. In a market of two hundred sellers there is no leader to follow.

The line that separates themBarriers to entry, which is what produces the small number of firms, which is what produces the interdependence. A restaurant setting its prices does not wonder how the restaurant two streets away will react, because it has two hundred rivals and none of them is watching. A mobile network setting its prices thinks about nothing else. Where a stem describes firms reacting to a named rival’s decision, it has described an oligopoly whatever else it says.

The honest conclusion is the CED’s: oligopolists do not reach the monopoly outcome, and prices are still higher and quantities lower than under perfect competition.

Trap — what makes a cartel likely to break Conditions that make cheating easy and detection hard: many members rather than two, differentiated products so a discount can be hidden inside a change of specification, secret discounts to individual buyers, falling demand that leaves everyone with spare capacity, and no way to punish a defector next period. The mirror is also examinable: cartels are most durable where there are few members, an identical product with a visible price, and repeated dealing in which cheating is spotted quickly.

4.5a Game theory: dominant strategies and Nash equilibrium

Two definitions, one procedure, and one implication that runs in only one direction. Everything the exam asks about game theory is assembled from those.

A game
A situation where your payoff depends on your choice AND on everyone else’s.In real lifeTwo shops deciding whether to open on a public holiday. Opening is worth it if the other stays shut and barely worth it if both open.A situation in which a number of individuals take actions, and the payoff for each individual depends directly on both the individual’s own choice and the choices of others. The second half is what makes it a game rather than a decision: if your payoff turned only on what you did, you would simply pick the biggest number and there would be nothing to model. Because it turns on both, you have to reason about what the other player will do, and they are reasoning about you.
Strategy
A complete plan of actions for playing the game.In real life“Charge the high price” is a strategy for a shop. It is a plan it can carry out whatever the shop across the road does.A complete plan of actions for playing a game - complete in the sense that it says what to do in every situation the game can reach, not just in the one the player expects. In the two-by-two games this course uses, each player has exactly two strategies and they are the two rows or the two columns of the table.
Normal form (the payoff matrix)also called the payoff matrix, the payoff table or strategic formThe CED names the model; every exam stem you will ever read names the table. Same object.
The table showing what each player gets for every combination of strategies.In real lifeThe grid a business-school case prints for two rival bidders, four cells, two numbers in each - what each of them walks away with under each pair of bids.The normal form model of a game shows the payoffs that result from each collection of strategies, one for each player. One player’s strategies are the rows, the other’s the columns, and every cell holds a pair of numbers. The exam’s convention, which it states in the stem rather than assuming: the first number in each cell belongs to the row player. Reading the pair the wrong way round turns every answer inside out, and it is worth ten seconds at the start of every game question.
Payoff
What a player gets from one cell - usually profit, and always for one named player.In real lifeThe $40 a car wash takes in a morning if it prices high and the car wash across the road prices high too.The outcome a player receives from a particular combination of strategies. Two habits are worth building. First, always name the player as you read a number, because half of every cell belongs to somebody else. Second, compare payoffs only down a column or across a row - that is, holding the other player’s choice fixed. Comparing diagonally is comparing two things that could never happen from one player’s decision, and it is where wrong answers to game questions come from.
Dominant strategy
One action that is better for you no matter what the other player does.In real lifeCarrying an umbrella when it is free to carry and the forecast is genuinely unknown. Whatever the sky does, you are no worse off.A player has a dominant strategy when the payoff to a particular action is always higher independent of the action taken by the other player. “Always” is the whole test and it is the word students drop: you must check every column, not the one you looked at first, and find the same action winning each time. If the winner changes from one column to the next, that player has no dominant strategy. Dominated actions can then be eliminated from a player’s action set, and doing so can sometimes lead straight to an equilibrium outcome - which is how the games where only one player has a dominant strategy are solved.
On the exam

The figure shows the payoffs to two firms, with Firm A’s payoff first in each cell. Which statement is correct?

  1. ABoth firms have a dominant strategy, and the equilibrium is Expand / Advertise
  2. BFirm A has a dominant strategy and Firm B does not; the equilibrium is Expand / Wait
  3. CFirm B has a dominant strategy and Firm A does not; the equilibrium is Hold / Advertise
  4. DNeither firm has a dominant strategy, so the game has no Nash equilibrium
Answer

B. Check Firm A down each column: against Advertise, 60 beats 45; against Wait, 80 beats 50. Expand wins both times, so it is dominant. Check Firm B across each row: against Expand it prefers Wait (35 over 30); against Hold it prefers Advertise (50 over 20). The winner changes, so B has no dominant strategy. Firm B knows A will Expand, and its best reply to Expand is Wait - so the equilibrium is Expand / Wait, a Nash equilibrium reached with only one dominant strategy in the game.

Nash equilibrium
A cell nobody can improve on by changing their own move alone.In real lifeEveryone driving on the same side of the road. You cannot do better by switching sides by yourself, however much you might prefer the other convention.A condition describing the set of actions in which no player can increase his or her payoff by unilaterally taking another action, given the other players’ actions. Unilaterally is the entire definition and it is the word the exam turns on: the test asks whether one player, moving alone while the other stays put, can do better. Students instead ask whether both players together could do better, which is a different question, and the answer to it is often yes - that is precisely what makes the prisoner’s dilemma a dilemma. To find one, mark each player’s best reply to each of the other’s actions; a cell where both marks land is a Nash equilibrium.
On the exam

Two firms choose a low or a high price, with the first number in each cell being Firm A’s profit. Using the figure, which cell is the Nash equilibrium, and why is it not the cell with the largest joint profit?

  1. AHigh / high, because 70 + 70 is the largest total available
  2. BLow / low, because each firm gains by cutting price whatever the other does
  3. CLow / high, because the firm that cuts first takes 90
  4. DThere is no Nash equilibrium, because both firms would rather be somewhere else
Answer

B. Hold Firm B at Low: A gets 40 by pricing Low and 20 by pricing High, so Low. Hold B at High: A gets 90 by pricing Low and 70 by pricing High, so Low again. The same is true for B by symmetry, so both play Low and neither can gain by moving on its own. High / high pays both firms more, but getting there needs both to move at once, and the definition allows only one player to move at a time - which is why the equilibrium can be a cell both players would rather escape.

The Prisoner’s Dilemma
Both players follow their own interest and both end up worse off than if they had not.In real lifeTwo neighbours who each water their lawn through a drought. Both would prefer a shared ban to two brown lawns and a fine, and neither will be the only one to stop.The game in which each player’s dominant strategy leads to an outcome both players like less than the cooperative one. It is the model of the cartel: the cooperative cell is the monopoly outcome, defecting is cheating on the quota, and the equilibrium is the cell where everybody has cheated. The economics conclusion the CED draws from it is careful, and worth copying exactly - oligopolists have difficulty achieving the monopoly outcome, and nevertheless prices are generally higher and quantities lower under oligopoly than under perfect competition.
Eliminating a dominated action
Cross out what a player would never do, then solve the smaller game.In real lifeIf you know your rival will never open on Sunday, you can stop planning for Sundays and decide the rest of the week on that basis.Because a rational player never takes a dominated action, that action can be eliminated from the player’s action set, and the game that remains is smaller. Where one player has a dominant strategy and the other does not, this is the fastest route to the answer: fix the dominant strategy, look only at that row or column, and take the other player’s best reply within it. The CED says this can sometimes lead to an equilibrium outcome, and the hedge is honest - in a game where nobody has a dominant strategy there is nothing to eliminate, and the equilibrium has to be found by marking best replies instead.
The procedure, and it never varies
  1. Check the stem for whose payoff is written first in each cell. It is normally the row player’s, and reading it backwards inverts every answer.
  2. For the row player, go down each column in turn, compare the two first-numbers, and mark the bigger. Same row marked in every column means that row is a dominant strategy.
  3. For the column player, go across each row, compare the two second-numbers, and mark the bigger. Same column every time means dominant.
  4. A cell carrying both marks is a Nash equilibrium. There may be one, more than one, or none.
Never compare diagonally. A diagonal comparison holds nothing fixed, and no single player’s decision could ever move you along it.

The line that separates themThe implication runs one way only. If every player has a dominant strategy, the cell where they meet is a Nash equilibrium - so dominance gives you an equilibrium. But a Nash equilibrium needs no dominant strategy anywhere in the matrix: it only asks whether either player could gain by moving unilaterally. Assuming the converse is the single most expensive habit in this topic, because the exam’s favourite matrix is one in which at most one player has a dominant strategy.
Trap — the implication that only runs one way The CED prints the dominant-strategy statement immediately before the Nash statement, and that order invites the reading “a Nash equilibrium is where both players play their dominant strategies”. Only one direction holds. Dominant strategies all round do give a Nash equilibrium. A Nash equilibrium needs no dominant strategy anywhere in the matrix, because its test is only whether one player, moving unilaterally, could do better. The exam’s favourite matrix is one in which at most one player has a dominant strategy — exactly the case this reading gets wrong.
Worked example — why the dilemma is a dilemma Two firms, each choosing a low or a high price. Both high pays 70 each. Both low pays 40 each. If one goes low while the other stays high, the low one takes 90 and the high one takes 20.
Is low dominant? Against the other firm’s low: 40 beats 20, so low. Against its high: 90 beats 70, so low again. Dominant, for both firms by symmetry.
So the outcome is low-low, at 40 each. Both firms would prefer 70 each and neither will move, because moving alone takes you from 40 to 20.
That last sentence is the whole definition of a Nash equilibrium doing its work. The cell is stable not because it is good but because no single player can improve on it by itself. Getting to high-high needs both to move at once, and nothing in the model lets them.
Not on the exam — the CED says so Dominant strategies and Nash equilibrium with more than two players, or with more than two actions per player, are beyond the scope of the course. So are mixed-strategy equilibria, extensive form games (the tree diagrams), and normal form games larger than two by two. That rules out most of what you will find if you go looking for game theory anywhere else — and it is permission to stop reading, not a warning. Everything examinable fits in a two-by-two table with two named actions each, and the procedure in the rule box above solves every one of them.

The card to memorize

The rules

  • Profit-maximizing quantity, every structure: MR = MC
  • Price for a price setter: read UP to demand, never off MR
  • Straight-line demand: MR shares the intercept, twice the slope
  • Marginal revenue: change in TOTAL revenue ÷ change in quantity
  • Profit: (P − ATC) × Q, both read at the chosen quantity
  • Allocatively efficient quantity: where MC crosses demand
  • Fair-return price: where demand crosses ATC. Socially optimal: where demand crosses MC
  • Nash equilibrium: a cell where neither player gains by moving alone

The traps, in one list

  • Never read the monopoly price off the MR curve
  • The rule did not change from Unit 3; the MR curve did
  • A firm with market power never produces where MR is negative
  • A rise in FIXED cost changes neither price nor output
  • Natural monopoly means ATC still falling at the quantity demanded
  • Marginal-cost pricing bankrupts a natural monopoly
  • Perfect price discrimination is efficient, and takes all the surplus
  • Zero profit is P = ATC; efficiency is P = MC. Different claims
  • The long-run picture in 4.4 is a tangency, never a crossing
  • Dominant needs the SAME action to win in every column
  • Nash does not require any dominant strategy at all
