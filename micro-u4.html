Unit 3’s firm read its price off a screen and could do nothing about it. Every firm in Unit 4 writes its own price. It pays for writing a high one in units it does not sell. That is the only change, and the whole unit is downstream of it.

The sentence the whole unit unwinds from To sell one more unit, you have to lower the price — and lower it on every unit you were already selling. Hold that and the rest of the unit is derivable. Marginal revenue falls below price. The profit-maximizing quantity therefore lands to the left of where price would equal marginal cost. The price ends up above marginal cost, which is this unit’s definition of an inefficient market. And the gap between the quantity produced and the efficient quantity is the deadweight loss. Four results, one premise.
The one sentence the whole unit unwinds fromTo sell one more unit you must lower the price. And you must lower it on every unit you were already selling.Do that once with numbers and you never have to hold it in memory. Three cups at $8 brings in $24. Drop the price to $7 to sell a fourth, and all four buyers pay $7. So $28 comes in, and the fourth cup added $4. Not the $7 on the board. On the exam

A city has one bridge across its river and charges a toll. A second bridge opens two kilometres downstream, and drivers can use either. What happens to the first bridge’s market power, and why?

  1. AIt rises, because total traffic across the river has increased
  2. BIt falls, because drivers now have a close substitute for the crossing
  3. CIt is unchanged, because the first bridge still owns its own crossing
  4. DIt falls, because the toll is now regulated by the number of bridges
Answer

B. Market power comes from the absence of close substitutes, and a second crossing two kilometres away is a close substitute. The first bridge can still charge a toll, but raising it now sends drivers downstream, so the demand curve it faces has become flatter - more elastic - which is exactly what less market power looks like.

On the exam

The figure shows the payoffs to two firms, with Firm A’s payoff first in each cell. Which statement is correct?

  1. ABoth firms have a dominant strategy, and the equilibrium is Expand / Advertise
  2. BFirm A has a dominant strategy and Firm B does not; the equilibrium is Expand / Wait
  3. CFirm B has a dominant strategy and Firm A does not; the equilibrium is Hold / Advertise

Answer

B. Check Firm A down each column: against Advertise, 60 beats 45; against Wait, 80 beats 50. Expand wins both times, so it is dominant. Check Firm B across each row: against Expand it prefers Wait (35 over 30); against Hold it prefers Advertise (50 over 20). The winner changes, so B has no dominant strategy. Firm B knows A will Expand, and its best reply to Expand is Wait - so the equilibrium is Expand / Wait, a Nash equilibrium reached with only one dominant strategy in the game.

The procedure, and it never varies
  1. Check the stem for whose payoff is written first in each cell. It is normally the row player’s, and reading it backwards inverts every answer.
  2. For the row player, go down each column in turn, compare the two first-numbers, and mark the bigger. Same row marked in every column means that row is a dominant strategy.
  3. For the column player, go across each row, compare the two second-numbers, and mark the bigger. Same column every time means dominant.
  4. A cell carrying both marks is a Nash equilibrium. There may be one, more than one, or none.
Never compare diagonally. A diagonal comparison holds nothing fixed, and no single player’s decision could ever move you along it.

Worked example — why the dilemma is a dilemma Two firms, each choosing a low or a high price. Both high pays 70 each. Both low pays 40 each. If one goes low while the other stays high, the low one takes 90 and the high one takes 20.
