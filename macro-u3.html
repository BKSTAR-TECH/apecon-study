3.1 Aggregate demand

One curve, four sources of spending, and three separate reasons it slopes down that have nothing to do with the reason a single good’s demand curve slopes down.

MOD-2.A.1, in full AD describes the relationship between the price level and spending by households, firms, government, and the rest of the world. Four sources, added together at every price level: consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports. A page that draws the curve and never names the four has drawn the shape and missed what it is made of - and the multiplier one section later has nothing to attach itself to without them.
Aggregate demand (AD)
The whole schedule: how much of everything buyers, firms, government and the world would take, at every price level.also called total spending, aggregate expenditure or total planned spendingAll four names describe the same curve: total spending in the economy at every price level. “Aggregate expenditure” is also the name of a Unit 3 model some textbooks build in more detail than this course requires; on this exam it is a synonym for AD.In real lifeA finance ministry publishing one forecast of total spending in the economy this year, not four separate forecasts. AD is that one number, read off at every possible price level rather than at just the one the country happens to be at.The relationship between the price level and the quantity of goods and services demanded by households (consumption), firms (investment), government (government spending), and the rest of the world (net exports). Four sources of spending, added together at every price level - not one number but a curve, exactly as demand was a curve in Unit 1. A page that draws AD and never names the four components has drawn the curve and missed what it is made of, which matters two sections later when the multiplier needs something to multiply.
Why AD slopes down
Three effects, none of them the reason a MICRO demand curve slopes down.In real lifeA national price level falling a little makes the same savings account buy more, makes borrowing cheaper, and makes the country’s exports look cheap to foreign buyers - three separate reasons, at the same moment, for total spending to rise.The negative slope of the AD curve is explained by the real wealth effect, the interest rate effect, and the exchange rate effect. A lower price level makes existing savings worth more in real terms (real wealth effect), lowers the interest rate needed to attract the saving households now want to do less of (interest rate effect), and depreciates the currency so exports rise and imports fall (exchange rate effect). All three end the same way - more is bought - which is what makes a change in the price level a movement along AD rather than a reason to shift it. Micro’s substitution and income effects belong to one good’s demand curve and play no part here.
On the exam

Which of the following explains the negative slope of the aggregate demand curve?

  1. AAs the price level falls, consumers substitute toward relatively cheaper goods
  2. BAs the price level falls, the real wealth, interest rate and exchange rate effects all raise the quantity of output demanded
  3. CAs the price level falls, firms find it profitable to produce more output
  4. DAs the price level falls, workers require higher nominal wages to supply the same labor
Answer

B. Option A is the MICRO explanation for a single good’s demand curve and does not apply to the price level for the whole economy. The AD curve slopes down because of the real wealth effect, the interest rate effect and the exchange rate effect - three mechanisms triggered by the price level itself, none of them a substitution between goods.

A shift of the AD curve
Anything that changes C, I, G or NX for a reason OTHER than the price level.In real lifeConsumer confidence surveys move the whole spending forecast up or down before a single price anywhere in the economy has changed. That revision is a shift of AD, not a reading further along it.Any change in the components of aggregate demand - consumption, investment, government spending, or net exports - that is not due to changes in the price level leads to a shift of the AD curve. The test is exactly Unit 1’s test, one level up: did a change in the price level cause this? If yes, it is one of the three effects above and the curve did not move. If no - a change in consumer confidence, a tax cut, a foreign recession, a change in the exchange rate itself - the whole curve moves, at every price level at once.
The quantity of output demanded
One number: total spending at ONE particular price level. Not the same claim as aggregate demand.In real lifeA statistics office reporting that, at this quarter’s price level, total planned spending came to $22 trillion. That single figure is a quantity of output demanded, not the whole AD curve.The counterpart to Unit 1’s quantity demanded, one level up: a single point on the AD curve, read at one price level. It changes only because the price level changed - and when it does, the point moves along an AD curve that has not itself moved. “The quantity of output demanded rose” and “aggregate demand rose” are exactly as different as “quantity demanded” and “demand” were for one good, and the exam relies on students blurring them the same way.

Why the curve slopes down

Three effects, and the CED points at all three by name so that the micro explanation cannot be recycled here: MOD-2.A.2 names the real wealth effect, the interest rate effect, and the exchange rate effect, and explicitly cross-references the micro slope explanation as a DIFFERENT thing.

The AGGREGATE demand curve has no “everything else” to substitute toward - there is only one price level for the whole economy. That is exactly why the CED needs three NEW mechanisms rather than reusing the old one, and an answer that says “substitution effect” on an AD question has answered the wrong unit’s question.

3.2 The multiplier effect

A dollar does not stop moving once it is spent. It becomes somebody’s income, part of which is spent again, and the multiplier is the arithmetic of that chain - checked here twice, because a multiplier computed with the wrong denominator renders exactly as convincingly as one computed with the right one.

Marginal propensity to consume (MPC)
The share of one extra dollar of DISPOSABLE income that gets spent, not saved.In real lifeA household that gets a $100 bonus and spends $80 of it has an MPC of 0.8 on that dollar. The other $20 is what the marginal propensity to save measures.The marginal propensity to consume is the change in consumer spending divided by the change in disposable income - not gross income, and not GDP. The sum of the marginal propensity to consume and the marginal propensity to save is equal to one: every extra dollar of disposable income is either spent or saved, with nothing left over. Both the expenditure multiplier and the tax multiplier are built entirely out of this one number, which is why a stem that hands you a tax rate alongside a gross-income change is testing whether you divide by the right thing.
On the exam

A household’s gross income rises by $500, of which $100 is paid in additional taxes. The household then increases its spending by $320. The household’s marginal propensity to consume is

  1. A0.64
  2. B0.80
  3. C0.36
  4. D0.20
Answer

B. MPC is measured against the change in DISPOSABLE income, not gross income: $500 minus the $100 in extra tax leaves $400 of disposable income, and $320 ÷ $400 = 0.80. Dividing by the $500 gross figure instead gives 0.64, the wrong denominator and the standard way this mark is lost.

Autonomous expenditure
Spending that would happen at zero income - the intercept the multiplier works on.In real lifeA government infrastructure program funded regardless of how the economy is doing this quarter. It does not wait for GDP to move first; GDP moves because of it.A $1 change to autonomous expenditures leads to further changes in total expenditures and total output. Autonomous spending is the part of C, I, G or NX that is not itself a response to the level of income - the intercept of the consumption function is the household half of it. The multiplier is the machine that turns one dollar of autonomous change into a larger change in equilibrium output, because that first dollar becomes somebody’s income, part of which is spent again.
The expenditure multiplier
1 ÷ (1 − MPC). Multiplies a change in ANY of the four AD components.also called the spending multiplier or the government spending multiplier, when applied to GThe CED’s own name is “expenditure multiplier” and it applies to ANY of the four AD components, not only G. “The government spending multiplier” is correct only when the change being multiplied is a change in G.In real lifeA $10 million factory contract does not raise the region’s income by $10 million. It raises it by $10 million paid to workers and suppliers, who then spend part of that, who are paid by others who spend part of that - and the multiplier is the running total of every one of those rounds.The expenditure multiplier quantifies the size of the change in aggregate demand as a result of a change in any of the components of aggregate demand - not only government spending. A change in investment, in consumer confidence, or in exports is multiplied by exactly the same 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) as a change in G. Calling it “the government spending multiplier” and reserving it for G is a narrower rule than the CED states, and the exam applies it to all four.
The tax multiplier
−MPC ÷ (1 − MPC). Smaller in size than the spending multiplier by exactly one.In real lifeA tax cut of $100 does not add $100 of spending on round one. Some of it - MPS of it - is saved before it ever reaches a shop, which is why the tax multiplier starts a round behind the spending multiplier and never catches up.The tax multiplier quantifies the size of the change in aggregate demand as a result of a change in taxes. Its formula is −MPC ÷ (1 − MPC), negative because a tax INCREASE lowers AD and a tax cut raises it, and smaller in absolute value than the expenditure multiplier by exactly one - the round of spending a tax change never gets, because the first dollar of a tax change has to become disposable income and then a household’s decision before any of it is spent.
The balanced-budget multiplier
Raise G and T by the same amount and real GDP rises by THAT amount - not by zero.In real lifeA town that raises property taxes by exactly what it spends on a new school still sees local spending rise, because the school’s builders spend every dollar of the contract while the taxpayers funding it would only have spent a share of theirs.Because the spending multiplier and the tax multiplier differ by exactly one, raising government spending and taxes by the same amount does not cancel out. One full round of spending survives - the round the tax increase does not manage to remove, because it only ever removed the MPC-sized share of what the equal spending increase puts in whole. The balanced-budget multiplier is therefore always exactly one, whatever the MPC is.

The expenditure multiplieralso called the spending multiplier or the government spending multiplier, when applied to GThe CED’s own name is “expenditure multiplier” and it applies to ANY of the four AD components, not only G. “The government spending multiplier” is correct only when the change being multiplied is a change in G. is the engine behind every fiscal-policy calculation later in this unit.

Trap — the denominator MPC is the change in consumer spending divided by the change in disposable income - after tax, not gross income and not GDP. A stem that hands you a gross-income change alongside a tax rate is testing exactly this: divide by the gross figure and the multiplier comes out wrong in a way that looks perfectly confident.

The line that separates themThe gap between the two is not a coincidence of the numbers: it is exactly one whole round of spending, because a dollar of government spending IS round-one spending, while a dollar of tax cut becomes round-one spending only after a household decides to spend the MPC-sized share of it. Equal-sized policies are never equally powerful.
Worked example — both multipliers, MPC = 0.8 Spending multiplier: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5.
Tax multiplier: −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4.
The check. The two differ by exactly one: 5 − 4 = 1, as POL-1.A.4 requires and as the “balanced-budget” result below depends on.
Balanced budget: raise G by $10 billion and T by $10 billion in the same year. Effect of G: $10bn × 5 = +$50bn. Effect of T: $10bn × (−4) = −$40bn. Net: +$10 billion - not zero, and exactly equal to the size of the equal change.
Trap — multiplying a gap instead of dividing it Closing a recessionary gap asks for the MINIMUM change in spending needed, and College Board’s own rubric writes it as ΔG = output gap ÷ spending multiplier. The instinct is to multiply the gap by the multiplier instead, which overshoots by the multiplier squared. A $60 billion gap with a multiplier of 5 needs a $12 billion injection ($60bn ÷ 5), not a $300 billion one ($60bn × 5).

3.3 Short-run aggregate supply

SRAS has a slope for exactly one reason, and that reason is also why a stem can turn an AD-AS shock into an unemployment answer without ever mentioning the labor market.

Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)
The relationship between the price level and total output supplied, WHILE some input prices are stuck.In real lifeA factory whose workers are on a two-year wage contract keeps paying the same wage however the price of what it sells moves this quarter. That stuck input price is the whole reason SRAS has a slope at all.The SRAS curve describes the relationship between the price level and the quantity of goods and services supplied in an economy, over the horizon on which some input prices have not yet adjusted. It is upward-sloping, and the next entry is the reason why.
Why SRAS slopes up
Sticky wages and prices: output prices can rise while input costs have not caught up, so producing more is briefly profitable.In real lifeA bakery that signed a year-long flour contract sees the price of bread rise this month while its biggest cost has not moved at all. Every extra loaf now carries a fatter margin, so it bakes more.The SRAS curve is upward-sloping because of sticky wages and prices: some input costs are fixed over the short run while output prices can move, so a higher price level briefly raises the margin on every unit and firms respond by producing more. This is also the mechanism that stops working in the long run, once every sticky input has caught up - which is exactly the pivot the next topic turns on.
What shifts SRAS
Anything that changes PRODUCTION COSTS, such as a change in inflationary expectations.In real lifeFirms that expect prices to rise 8% next year start signing wage contracts at 8% now, before the inflation itself has happened. Their costs just rose because of a belief, and SRAS shifts left on the strength of it.Any factor that causes production costs to change, such as a change in inflationary expectations, will cause the SRAS curve to shift. Higher expected inflation raises wage demands and input costs before the inflation arrives, shifting SRAS left; a fall in input prices - a commodity price drop, a productivity gain - shifts it right. The CED names inflationary expectations as its own worked example, which is the version the exam reaches for most.
Moving along SRAS and unemployment
Higher output along SRAS means more workers hired, and - with the labor force UNCHANGED - lower unemployment.In real lifeA factory town where output climbs through a boom rehires everyone who was laid off in the last downturn, without a single new person moving to town. The labor force is the same; how many of it are working is not.Moving along the SRAS curve, an increase in the price level is associated with an increase in output, which means employment must correspondingly rise. With the labor force held constant, unemployment will fall. So there is a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment - the same relationship the Phillips curve draws directly, and the “labor force held constant” clause is the step a summary usually drops, even though it is what makes more output translate into fewer unemployed rather than into a larger workforce.
SRAS against LRAS: one economy, two horizons

The line that separates themRead as two separate facts about supply, these look like a contradiction. They are one fact about the same economy at two horizons: stickiness is exactly what the long run is defined as the absence of. A shock moves the economy along SRAS and around LRAS; only growth moves LRAS itself.

The line that separates themExactly the AD test, run on the supply side. A price-level-caused move is a movement along SRAS and lands on the demand-shock side of the next table; a cost-caused move is a shift of SRAS and lands on the supply-shock side.
The chain MOD-2.D.1 states Price level up → output up → employment up → (labor force held constant) → unemployment down. Every link matters, and the one in parentheses is the one a summary drops. Without “labor force held constant,” more people finding work could just as easily mean the labor force grew - which says nothing about unemployment falling. With it, the chain is airtight: the same people wanting work, more of them working, means a lower unemployment RATE.
Trap — the Phillips curve is not a different topic A stem that gives you an AD-AS diagram and asks about inflation and unemployment together is testing the SAME relationship as one that hands you a Phillips curve directly. Moving along SRAS to the right is moving along SRPC up and to the left. If your two panels disagree about which way unemployment moved, one of them is drawn wrong.

3.4 Long-run aggregate supply

The short run and the long run are not two amounts of calendar time. They are two states of the same prices - and the payoff of getting that right is a vertical LRAS line that never moves for a shock, however large.

The short run and the long run, defined
Not calendar time: a claim about whether prices and wages have finished adjusting.In real lifeA country is still in the short run on the wages set by contracts signed last year, and already in the long run on the price of a vegetable that repriced this morning. Same calendar date, two different clocks.In the long run all prices and wages are fully flexible, while in the short run some input prices are fixed. A consequence of flexible long-run prices and wages is no long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment: once every price has adjusted, a change in AD or SRAS can no longer buy real output by surprising anyone, and the economy settles back to full employment at a new price level instead. This is the same stickiness fact as SRAS’s slope, read from the other end - the short run is defined as the horizon on which that stickiness has not yet gone away.
LRAS and the production possibilities curve
Same idea as the PPC from Unit 1: the ceiling on what the economy can sustainably produce.In real lifeA country reporting output above what its factories, workforce and land can sustainably deliver is like a runner reporting a pace faster than their own top speed - it can happen for a moment, and it cannot last.The LRAS curve corresponds to the production possibilities curve (PPC) because they both represent maximum sustainable capacity. Maximum sustainable capacity is the total output an economic system will produce over a set period of time if all resources are fully employed. This is the bridge Unit 1’s PPC was built for: a point inside the PPC is a recessionary gap on this diagram, and the PPC shifting outward is LRAS shifting right - the same two events, the same axes turned into a price level and an output level instead of two goods.
Why LRAS is vertical
At full employment, however high the price level goes, output cannot exceed capacity for long.also called potential output, potential GDP or the natural level of outputAll four name the same vertical line on the AD-AS diagram - the output the economy can sustainably produce with its current factors and technology. “Potential GDP” is the phrase most textbooks and most news coverage use.In real lifeA stadium sells out at 40,000 seats whatever price the tickets carry. Charging more does not create an extra seat; it only changes who is willing to pay for the ones that exist.The LRAS curve is vertical at the full-employment level of output because in the long run wages and prices fully adjust: no matter how high or low the price level goes, the economy’s sustainable capacity is what it is, set by its factors of production and its technology - the same two things that shift the PPC. A stem asking what happens to output as the price level rises in the long run has one answer, and it is “nothing.”
MOD-2.F.1, in full The LRAS curve corresponds to the PPC because both represent maximum sustainable capacity. Maximum sustainable capacity is the total output the economic system will produce over a set period if all resources are fully employed - the exact same ceiling Unit 1’s PPC drew with two goods on two axes. LRAS draws it again with real GDP on one axis and the price level on the other, and it changes for the SAME two reasons the PPC shifted: more factors of production, or better technology.
The inflation-unemployment trade-off, short run against long run

The line that separates themBoth rows are true, and they do not contradict each other, because they describe two different amounts of TIME for prices to catch up. A stem that gives you a short-run scenario and asks for the long-run answer is testing whether you will keep the trade-off past the point it stops holding.
Trap — shifting LRAS to close a gap LRAS never moves in response to an AD or SRAS shock. A student who shifts LRAS to close a recessionary or inflationary gap has claimed the economy’s CAPACITY changed - a different and almost always wrong claim. The gap closes by AD or SRAS moving, or by self-correction moving SRAS, never by LRAS moving to meet wherever the economy happens to be.

3.5 Equilibrium in the AD-AS model

Two definitions of equilibrium that look alike until you ask how many curves have to agree.

Short-run equilibrium
Where AD meets SRAS. Can sit anywhere relative to full employment.In real lifeA thermometer reading is wherever it is right now, not wherever it is supposed to settle. Short-run equilibrium is that immediate reading; it says nothing yet about full employment.Short-run equilibrium occurs when the aggregate quantity of output demanded and the aggregate quantity of output supplied are equal - at the intersection of the AD and SRAS curves. Nothing in that intersection requires it to sit on LRAS: AD and SRAS can cross to the left of full employment, to the right of it, or exactly on it, and all three are short-run equilibria.
Long-run equilibrium
All three curves crossing at ONE point - AD and SRAS have to meet ON the LRAS.In real lifeThree separate measurements of the same bridge - by the architect, the surveyor, and the safety inspector - all agreeing on one length. Any two agreeing is not the same claim as all three agreeing.Long-run equilibrium occurs when the AD and SRAS curves intersect on the LRAS - at the full-employment level of real output. A page that says “where AD meets SRAS” for both short-run and long-run equilibrium has erased the one condition that makes the long run special: it is not enough for AD and SRAS to cross somewhere, they have to cross exactly where LRAS stands.
Output gaps
Positive = inflationary, above full employment. Negative = recessionary, below it.also called a GDP gap or the gap between actual and potential outputEvery one of these names the SAME horizontal distance: short-run output measured against the vertical LRAS line. “GDP gap” is the more common textbook term for the identical idea the CED calls an output gap.In real lifeA factory running triple overtime shifts it cannot sustain is a positive gap; the same factory running one shift with two lines dark is a negative one. Both are departures from the pace it can keep up indefinitely.The short-run equilibrium output can be at the full-employment level of output, above it, or below it, creating positive (i.e., inflationary) or negative (i.e., recessionary) output gaps. The naming is backwards from what a first read suggests: MORE output than the economy can sustain is called inflationary, and LESS is called recessionary. Both are measured horizontally, as the distance from short-run output to LRAS.
On the exam

In the figure, short-run equilibrium output exceeds the full-employment level. This output gap is best described as

  1. Aa recessionary gap, because output cannot be sustained
  2. Ban inflationary gap, a positive output gap
  3. Ca recessionary gap, a negative output gap
  4. Dneither, because the economy is still at equilibrium
Answer

B. Short-run output above the full-employment level is a POSITIVE output gap, which the CED calls inflationary - the economy is producing more than it can sustain, which is what eventually pulls the price level up further as inputs catch up. Being “at equilibrium” and having an output gap are not mutually exclusive; a short-run equilibrium can sit anywhere relative to full employment.

3.6 Changes in the AD-AS model in the short run

Two kinds of shock, and the exam’s favourite question is which one you are looking at - answered entirely by whether output and the price level move together or apart.

A demand shock
AD moves. Output, employment and the price level all move the SAME way as the shock.In real lifeA sudden surge of foreign tourism raises spending in the economy overnight. Output rises, more people are hired to meet it, and prices creep up too - all three moving together.A positive (negative) shock in AD causes output, employment, and the price level to rise (fall) in the short run. All three variables move in the same direction as each other, because a demand shock slides the economy along an unmoved SRAS curve - which is the signature that separates it from a supply shock.
A supply shock
SRAS moves. Output and employment move ONE way; the price level moves the OTHER way.In real lifeA spike in the cost of a widely used input raises prices across the economy at the very moment factories are cutting shifts because that same cost squeeze makes production less profitable. Prices up, output down - together.A positive (negative) shock in SRAS causes output and employment to rise (fall) and the price level to fall (rise) in the short run. This is the reason supply shocks get a topic of their own: no demand shock can move the price level and output in opposite directions, and a negative supply shock - output down, price level up at once - is a combination that has no demand-side explanation at all.
Demand-pull and cost-push inflation
Two different curves moving, both raising the price level.In real lifeA boom fuelled by easy credit that bids prices up everywhere is demand-pull. A harvest failure that raises the price of a staple crop and drags every downstream price with it is cost-push. Both show up as inflation in the same headline number.Inflation can be caused by changes in aggregate demand (demand-pull) or aggregate supply (cost-push). The two are told apart by what happens to output, not by the fact that prices rose: demand-pull inflation comes with output rising too, because AD moved right along an unmoved SRAS; cost-push inflation comes with output falling, because SRAS moved left. A stem describing rising prices AND falling output has already told you which of the two it means.

Memorise the sign, not the mood.

3.7 Long-run self-adjustment

The sentence this topic states is the one that makes fiscal policy sound optional, and the hedge buried in its middle is the reason it is not.

Long-run self-correction
Left alone, wages and prices adjust and the economy returns to the natural rate of unemployment.In real lifeA thermostat with no one touching it still finds room temperature eventually, because heat keeps moving until it stops having anywhere left to go. The economy’s wages and prices are the thing doing the moving here.In the long run, in the absence of government policy actions, flexible wages and prices will adjust to restore full employment and unemployment will revert to its natural rate after a shock to aggregate demand or short-run aggregate supply. The hedge in the middle of the sentence is the one this unit’s tension records: this is what happens if nobody intervenes, and topic 3.8 is what happens when somebody does. Self-correction is not an argument against fiscal policy; it is a description of what fills the gap while nobody is doing anything else.
Shifts in LRAS and economic growth
LRAS moving is the same event as the PPC moving: a change in the economy’s CAPACITY.In real lifeA country finishing a decade of new ports and a much larger, better-trained workforce can sustain a higher output every year afterward - not a one-off boost, a new ceiling.Shifts in the long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve indicate changes in the full-employment level of output and economic growth. This is the PPC connection from 3.4 completed: what shifted the PPC in Unit 1 - more factors of production, better technology - shifts LRAS here, for the same reason and by the same two causes. A demand shock or a supply shock moves the economy around a fixed LRAS; growth is the rarer event of LRAS itself moving.

MOD-2.I.1’s hedge In the long run, in the absence of government policy actions, the economy returns to the natural rate on its own. That clause sits in the middle of the sentence, which is exactly where it is easiest to read past. Drop it and the statement says the economy always fixes itself - which is the reading topic 3.8 exists to correct. Self-correction is real; it is also slow, and “slow” is the entire case for a faster tool.

3.8 Fiscal policy

A dollar of policy is not one thing - it is two different-length routes to AD, and the length of the route is the entire reason one policy tool is more powerful than the other.

Fiscal policy
Government spending and tax decisions used to steer the macroeconomy.In real lifeA government voting a stimulus package during a downturn, or voting to trim spending during a boom, is doing fiscal policy either way - the direction changes, not the tool.Governments implement fiscal policies to achieve macroeconomic goals, such as full employment. It is one of the two policy levers this course covers - the other, monetary policy, is Unit 4’s - and it operates through the government’s own budget rather than through the money supply.
The two tools of fiscal policy
Government spending, and taxes/transfers.In real lifeA budget bill that both funds a new highway and adjusts the income tax brackets is using both tools in the same piece of legislation.The tools of fiscal policy are government spending and taxes/transfers. The CED writes taxes and transfers as one item on purpose: a transfer payment - unemployment benefit, a subsidy paid to households - travels through the SAME channel as a tax change, household disposable income, rather than through the government-purchases channel. That is why a transfer payment is multiplied by the tax multiplier and not the spending multiplier.
Directly against indirectly
G IS a component of AD. A tax change has to become a household spending decision first.In real lifeA city funding road repairs adds that spending to AD the moment the contract is signed. A tax rebate mailed to the same households only adds to AD once each household decides how much of it to spend.Changes in government spending affect aggregate demand directly, and changes in taxes/transfers affect aggregate demand indirectly. Government spending is literally one of AD’s four components, so a dollar of it is a dollar of AD before anyone has decided anything. A dollar of tax cut becomes a dollar of disposable income, and only the MPC-sized share of THAT gets spent - the mechanism behind the next entry.
Why the spending multiplier beats the tax multiplier
The government spending multiplier is greater than the tax multiplier, because G skips the first round T has to spend.In real lifeTwo ways to inject $100 million: build a bridge with it, or cut taxes by that amount. The bridge money is all spending on day one; the tax cut money is only 80 cents on the dollar spending on day one, if the MPC is 0.8 - the rest is saved before it ever enters the multiplier chain.The government spending multiplier is greater than the tax multiplier, in absolute value, by exactly one - because government spending starts the round of spending at 100% while a tax change starts it at the MPC-sized share only. Equal-sized policies are therefore NOT equally powerful, and a stem offering “either a $50 billion spending increase or a $50 billion tax cut, whichever raises AD by more” always answers spending.
Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy
Expansionary closes a recessionary gap; contractionary closes an inflationary one.In real lifeA stimulus bill passed during a downturn is expansionary; a spending freeze or a tax rise passed to cool an overheating boom is contractionary. Same government, opposite direction, matched to opposite gaps.Expansionary or contractionary fiscal policies are used to restore full employment when the economy is in a negative (i.e., recessionary) or positive (i.e., inflationary) output gap. Expansionary policy - more G, or a tax cut - shifts AD right to close a recessionary gap; contractionary policy - less G, or a tax rise - shifts AD left to close an inflationary one. The direction of the policy always matches the sign the gap needs corrected.
What fiscal policy can move
Aggregate demand, real output, and the price level - all three, through the one shift.In real lifeA stimulus package is reported on the news by three different numbers - the boost to GDP, the change in employment, and the tick up in prices - all three effects of the same AD shift.Fiscal policy can influence aggregate demand, real output, and the price level. All three follow from the single AD shift a fiscal action produces; the exam sometimes asks for only one of the three, and a correct diagram already shows all of them at once.
The model fiscal policy is drawn on
The AD-AS diagram, always - the same one topic 3.5 and 3.6 already built.In real lifeAn FRQ asking to show the short-run effect of a tax cut wants one picture: AD shifting on the same axes as every other shock in this unit, not a new diagram invented for policy.The AD-AS model is used to demonstrate the short-run effects of fiscal policy. Nothing new is drawn - an expansionary policy is an AD shift exactly like MOD-2.H.1’s demand shock, and a contractionary one is its mirror. The only difference from an ordinary demand shock is what caused the shift: a legislated decision rather than a change in confidence or a foreign event.
Lags to discretionary fiscal policy
The economy on paper moves instantly. Real fiscal policy has to survive recognition, decision, and implementation first.In real lifeA recession that started in January is often not officially recognised until well into the year, then needs a bill drafted and passed, then needs the spending to actually begin. Each step is a delay the diagram does not show.In reality, there are lags to discretionary fiscal policy because of factors such as the time it takes to decide on and implement a policy action. “In reality” is the CED conceding that the AD-AS model treats a shift as instantaneous, and nothing in the world is. This is the bridge to 3.9: those same lags are the reason a policy that acts WITHOUT anyone deciding anything - an automatic stabilizer - is worth having.

The line that separates themA dollar is not a dollar of the same size once it has to pass through a household’s spending decision. That single difference in channel is the entire reason POL-1.A.4 says the spending multiplier is larger.
POL-1.A.3, and why POL-1.A.4 follows from it Government spending affects AD directly. Taxes/transfers affect it indirectly. Spending IS a component of AD - a dollar of it is a dollar of AD before anyone decides anything. A dollar of tax cut has to become a household’s disposable income and then a household’s decision, and only the MPC-sized share of it is spent. That one difference in channel is the entire reason the government spending multiplier is greater than the tax multiplier - not a separate fact to memorise, a consequence of this one.
Worked example — closing a $60 billion recessionary gap Full-employment output is $1,000 billion; current output is $940 billion. Recessionary gap: $1,000bn − $940bn = $60 billion.
MPC = 0.8, so the spending multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5.
Minimum change in government spending = output gap ÷ spending multiplier = $60bn ÷ 5 = $12 billion.
The check. $12 billion injected, multiplied by 5, raises AD by exactly $60 billion - closing the gap precisely, not overshooting it.

In reality, none of this happens instantly - which is exactly the argument for the tool the next topic covers.

3.9 Automatic stabilizers

The last topic in the unit is the one where the mechanism from 3.8’s lags pays off: a policy that acts WITHOUT anyone deciding anything, because it was written into law before the shock happened.

Automatic stabilizers
Built into the tax and transfer system, so they support a downturn and restrain a boom WITHOUT a new law.also called built-in stabilizers or non-discretionary fiscal policy“Non-discretionary” is the pointed one: it names exactly what these are NOT, in contrast with the discretionary fiscal policy of 3.8, and a stem that pairs the two words is testing whether you know which is faster.In real lifeNobody has to pass a bill for a worker who is laid off to start collecting unemployment benefit, or for a household whose income falls to owe less tax this year. Both happen automatically, on the existing rules.Automatic stabilizers support the economy during recessions and help prevent the economy from being overheated during expansionary periods. Both directions are stated in the same sentence on purpose: a stabilizer is not a recession tool with an incidental side effect in a boom, it restrains the boom on exactly the same mechanism that supports the recession. No new legislation, no recognition lag, no decision lag - the tax and transfer rules were written in advance and simply respond to whatever income turns out to be.
Why tax revenue falls in a recession
Falling GDP means falling income means falling tax revenue - automatically, and that softens the fall in consumption.In real lifeA household whose income drops from a layoff also drops into a lower tax bracket the same year, so its after-tax income falls by less than its pre-tax income did. That gap is the stabilizer working.Tax revenues decrease automatically as GDP falls, preventing consumption and the economy from falling further. Because taxes are levied on income, a fall in GDP is a fall in the tax base before anyone changes a rate - households keep a larger share of a smaller income than the old budget assumed, which cushions the fall in disposable income and therefore in consumption.
Why tax revenue rises in a boom
Rising GDP means rising tax revenue - automatically, and that restrains the overheating.In real lifeA worker who gets a large raise in a boom year also moves into a higher tax bracket, so take-home pay rises by less than gross pay did. That gap slows the very spending that pushed the economy into the boom.Tax revenues increase automatically as GDP rises, slowing consumption and preventing the economy from overheating. This is POL-1.C.2 run in reverse and it is the half of the mechanism a page in a hurry leaves out, because the recession story reads first and the reader stops paying attention by the boom story - which is precisely the gap POL-1.C.1 exists to close.
Transfer payments as a second stabilizer
Unemployment benefit and similar programs are the SPENDING-side stabilizer, and the one that gets left out.In real lifeA social-service program that pays more people when the economy weakens and fewer when it strengthens is stabilising output on the spending side, without a single tax rate having changed.Government policies, institutions, or agencies may also have social service programs whose transfer payments act as automatic stabilizers. Tax revenue is the half of the mechanism most pages remember; transfer spending is the half that gets left out, because the two statements just before it are both about tax revenue and the reader has stopped by the time this one arrives. Outlays including transfers rise automatically in a recession and fall automatically in a boom - the mirror image of tax revenue, working on the spending side of the same budget.

A page that only ever draws the recession half has covered half a statement while looking finished.
