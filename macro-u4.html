4.1 Financial assets, and the price of a bond

Money is not the only thing a household can hold, and the whole unit starts from the choice between holding it and holding something that pays. Two assets, one price relationship, and one opportunity cost that turns out to be the foundation of every graph that follows.

Liquidity
How quickly an asset can be turned into spending without losing value.In real lifeThe notes in a wallet buy lunch immediately; a flat takes months to sell and costs a fee to sell at all.How readily an asset can be used to pay for something, at short notice and without a loss. The most liquid forms of money are cash and demand deposits - a note is already a means of payment and a checking balance is one keystroke away from being one. Everything else a household can hold sits somewhere further down the same scale, and the further down it sits the more of a step it is to spend.
A financial asset
A claim on somebody else’s money, held instead of holding the money.In real lifeA savings certificate, a government bond, or a share in a company: none of them buys groceries as they stand, and all of them are worth something.A piece of paper (or a database entry) that entitles its holder to future payments from whoever issued it. Financial assets are held in place of the most liquid forms of money precisely because they pay something, and giving that payment up is what makes holding money costly at all.
A bond
A loan you can hold and resell: fixed interest, and the principal back at the end.In real lifeA government that needs to borrow for a new road sells bonds; a pension fund buys them and collects the interest for twenty years.Bonds are interest-bearing assets. The issuer borrows a sum, promises a fixed payment each period - the coupon - and repays the face value at maturity. The buyer of a bond is a lender, not an owner: a bondholder has no say in how the borrower runs anything, and gets paid whether or not the borrower does well.
A stock
A slice of ownership in a company, with no promised payment at all.In real lifeSomeone who buys a share in a supermarket chain owns a fraction of it, and receives nothing unless the board decides to pay a dividend.Stocks are equity: a share of ownership in a firm. There is no fixed payment and no maturity date. A shareholder’s return comes from dividends the firm chooses to pay and from selling the share for more than it cost, and a shareholder is last in line if the firm fails - behind every bondholder. That is the whole distinction the CED’s two parentheses draw: a bond is a debt, a stock is a stake.
On the exam

A saver buys a corporate bond and a share of stock in the same company on the same day. Which of the following correctly describes a difference between the two purchases?

  1. AThe bond makes the saver a part owner of the company; the stock makes the saver a lender to it
  2. BThe bond promises a fixed interest payment; the stock represents ownership and promises nothing
  3. CBoth promise a fixed payment, but only the stock can be resold
  4. DNeither can be resold once it has been bought
Answer

B. A bond is an interest-bearing asset - a debt the company owes, carrying a fixed payment. A stock is equity - a share of ownership, with no promised payment of any kind. Option A has the two roles the wrong way round, which is the reversal the item is testing.

The price of a previously issued bond
Rates up, bond prices down. Always, and it is arithmetic rather than a rule.In real lifeSomeone holding a bond bought last year at 3% finds it worth less the moment new bonds start paying 5% - nobody will pay the old price for the smaller payment.The price of previously issued bonds and interest rates on bonds are inversely related. The reason is that a bond already in circulation has a coupon fixed at issue, which cannot change: if the market rate rises, the only number left that CAN move to bring the bond’s yield up to the market is its price, and it moves down. The word “previously issued” is in the statement for exactly this reason, and it is the sentence that scores the point.
On the exam

Interest rates in an economy rise sharply over one month. What happens to the price of bonds that were issued last year, and why?

  1. AThe price rises, because the bonds now pay more interest
  2. BThe price rises, because bonds become more attractive when rates are high
  3. CThe price falls, because the coupon on an existing bond is fixed and only its price can adjust
  4. DThe price is unchanged, because the terms of an issued bond cannot be altered
Answer

C. An existing bond’s coupon was set when it was issued and never changes. For its yield to match a higher market rate, a buyer has to be able to get that same fixed coupon for less money - so the price falls. Option D confuses the terms of the bond, which really are fixed, with its market price, which is not.

The opportunity cost of holding money
The interest you gave up by keeping wealth as cash instead of as bonds.In real lifeA household that keeps $3,000 in a checking account paying nothing, while a one-year bond pays 5%, has given up about $150 for the convenience.The opportunity cost of holding money is the interest that could have been earned by holding other financial assets such as bonds instead. Nothing is charged for holding cash; the cost is entirely a foregone return, which is why it rises and falls with the interest rate. That single fact is the microfoundation of the next graph in this unit: money demand slopes downward because a higher nominal interest rate makes the same cash balance more expensive to hold.
On the exam

The nominal interest rate on short-term bonds falls from 6 percent to 2 percent. What happens to the opportunity cost of holding money, and to the quantity of money people want to hold?

  1. AOpportunity cost rises; quantity of money held falls
  2. BOpportunity cost falls; quantity of money held rises
  3. COpportunity cost falls; quantity of money held falls
  4. DOpportunity cost is unchanged; quantity of money held rises
Answer

B. The opportunity cost of holding money IS the interest given up, so when the rate falls the cost of holding cash falls with it. Holding money is now cheaper, so people hold more of it - which is the downward slope of the money demand curve, read one point at a time.

The line that separates themA bond is a debt and a stock is a stake. The CED puts the whole distinction in two parentheses - bonds are interest-bearing assets, stocks are equity - and a page that lists “bonds and stocks” together has dropped the only two words that tell them apart.
MEA-3.A.3, and the two words the rubric looks for The price of previously issued bonds and interest rates on bonds are inversely related. “Previously issued” is not decoration. A bond already in somebody’s hands has a coupon that was fixed the day it was sold and can never change again, so when the market rate moves, the price is the only number left free. That makes the inverse relation a derivation rather than a rule to memorize - and a rule memorized is a rule reversed under time pressure.

Worked example — a bond repriced by a rate change A perpetual bond has a face value of $1,000 and pays a fixed coupon of $50 a year. When it was issued, the market rate was 5%, so it sold for exactly its face value: $50 ÷ 0.05 = $1,000.
Market rates now rise to 10%. A buyer will only accept this bond if its $50 buys them a 10% return.
New price = $50 ÷ 0.10 = $500.
Check: $50 ÷ $500 = 10%, which is the market rate. The bond still pays $50 and always will; the price halved to make that fixed payment competitive.
The reverse case. If rates fell to 4% instead: $50 ÷ 0.04 = $1,250. Price up, yield down - the same arithmetic run the other way.
Trap — “the bond pays more when rates rise” It does not. The coupon on an issued bond is a term of the contract and cannot be revised, any more than a fixed-rate mortgage’s payment can. What changes is what somebody will pay to acquire that coupon. Every question in this topic is answered by asking which number is fixed and which is free, and on a previously issued bond the answer is always the same: the coupon is fixed, the price is free.

4.2 Nominal versus real interest rates

Two rates, three numbers, and one equation the CED deliberately states twice - once looking forward and once looking back. The algebra is trivial. Choosing which inflation figure the question meant is not, and that is where the marks are.

The nominal interest rate
The rate printed in the contract, with no adjustment for inflation.In real lifeA car loan advertised at 7% a year. That 7% is what the lender charges in dollars, whatever those dollars turn out to be worth.The rate of interest paid for a loan, unadjusted for inflation. It is the number a bank quotes, the number written into the agreement, and the only one of the two rates that is observable at the moment the loan is signed. It is also the rate on the vertical axis of the money market graph, which is why that axis says nominal and the loanable funds axis does not.
The expected real interest rate
The real return the two sides were aiming for when they set the nominal rate.In real lifeA bank that wants a genuine 3% return and expects prices to rise 2% quotes 5%, and finds out afterwards whether that was enough.Lenders and borrowers establish nominal interest rates as the sum of their expected real interest rate and expected inflation. This is the forward-looking half of the relation: it happens before anybody knows what inflation will be, and the word “expected” belongs on both of its terms. Rearranged, it says the expected real rate is the nominal rate minus expected inflation - which is the calculation a question sets when it gives you a forecast rather than an outturn.
The actual real interest rate
What the loan really earned once you know what inflation actually was.In real lifeA saver who earned 4% in a year when prices rose 6% has more dollars and less shopping than they started with.A real interest rate can be calculated in hindsight by subtracting the actual inflation rate from the nominal interest rate. Because the nominal rate is fixed in the contract and cannot move, the whole of any inflation surprise lands here. Higher-than-expected inflation pushes the actual real rate below the expected one, which moves real purchasing power from the lender to the borrower; lower-than-expected inflation does the reverse.
On the exam

A bank lends at a fixed nominal interest rate of 8 percent, expecting inflation of 3 percent. Inflation over the year turns out to be 7 percent. What is the actual real interest rate on the loan, and who benefits from the surprise?

  1. A5 percent; the lender benefits
  2. B1 percent; the borrower benefits
  3. C1 percent; the lender benefits
  4. D11 percent; neither party is affected
Answer

B. In hindsight the real rate is the nominal rate minus ACTUAL inflation: 8% − 7% = 1%. The lender expected 8% − 3% = 5% and got 1%, so the lender lost four percentage points of real return and the borrower gained them. Option A does the subtraction with the expected figure, which is the calculation for a rate that has not happened yet.

The Fisher relation
One equation joining three numbers: nominal, real, and inflation.In real lifeAny news report that quotes a savings rate and an inflation rate in the same sentence is inviting the reader to do this subtraction.Nominal rate = real rate + inflation rate, which the course uses in two directions and never in more than one at once. Forward, with expectations, it sets the nominal rate. Backward, with the outturn, it reads off the real rate. The examined error is not the algebra - it is using the expected inflation figure in a question that has handed you the actual one, or the reverse.
An inflation surprise
Inflation coming in away from what the contract assumed, moving wealth between the parties.In real lifeA tenant on a five-year fixed rent during an unexpected burst of inflation is paying less in real terms every month, and the landlord is receiving less.The gap between actual and expected inflation. It cannot change the nominal rate, which was fixed before it happened, so it changes the real rate instead - by exactly the size of the surprise. This is why fixed nominal contracts redistribute: an unexpected rise in inflation transfers real purchasing power from lenders to borrowers, and an unexpected fall transfers it back.

One equation, two moments — and the nominal rate is the number that cannot move in between
The nominal rate is fixed the day the contract is signed. So an inflation figure that comes in away from what was expected cannot change it, and has nowhere to go except into the real rate. That single asymmetry is the whole of this topic.

Worked example — one loan, both calculations A bank lends $10,000 for one year. It wants a real return of 4% and expects inflation of 2%.
Nominal rate agreed = 4% + 2% = 6%, so the borrower will repay $10,000 plus $600 of interest.
Over the year, inflation actually turns out to be 5%.
Actual real interest rate = 6% − 5% = 1%.
What that cost the lender. The expected real return was 4% of $10,000 = $400. The actual real return is 1% of $10,000 = $100. The lender is $300 of purchasing power worse off than planned, and the borrower is $300 better off - the same $600 changed hands either way.
The check. Both real rates use the SAME 6% nominal rate, because that number was fixed before anything was known. If your two answers used two different nominal rates, one of them has drifted.
Trap — using the forecast where the outturn belongs A stem that says inflation “was expected to be” is setting the forward calculation; a stem that says inflation “turned out to be” or “was actually” is setting the hindsight one. Both are one subtraction. Reading which figure the question handed you is the entire skill, and an answer that is off by exactly the size of the surprise has almost always used the wrong one.

4.3 What money is, and how much of it there is

The definition of money on this course is about acceptance, not about what a thing is made of - which is wider than most students expect and is exactly why a checking balance counts and a share certificate does not.

MEA-3.C.1, and how wide it is Money is any asset that is accepted as a means of payment. Not “issued by a government”, not “printed on paper”. Whether something is money is a question about what shopkeepers will take, and the answer can change: a demand deposit is money because a card payment settles a purchase, and a gold bar is not, in this economy, whatever it is worth.
Money
Any asset that is accepted as a means of payment.In real lifeA shop that takes a debit card and not a gold bar is telling you which of the two is money in that economy.Money is any asset that is accepted as a means of payment. Acceptance, not government issue, is what makes something money - the definition is deliberately wider than currency, which is how a demand deposit counts and why a stock certificate does not, however valuable it is. Whether a thing is money is a question about what shopkeepers will take, not about what it is made of.
Medium of exchange
The thing you hand over, so a trade does not need two matching wants.In real lifeA dentist who wants bread pays the baker in cash rather than filling a cavity in exchange for a loaf.Money serves as a medium of exchange: it is what changes hands to settle a transaction. Without it every trade needs a double coincidence of wants - each party wanting exactly what the other has - and the number of trades an economy can manage collapses. This is the function that makes the other two matter.
Unit of account
The common measuring stick every price is quoted in.In real lifeA supermarket that prints every shelf label in dollars is using money to make two unrelated goods comparable at a glance.Money serves as a unit of account: the standard in which prices, debts and accounts are expressed. It does not have to change hands to do this job - a contract can be written in a currency and settled in something else - which is what makes this a separate function rather than a restatement of the first.
Store of value
Purchasing power you can hold onto and spend later.In real lifeWages paid on the last day of the month and spent across the following four weeks are being stored, not immediately exchanged.Money serves as a store of value: it carries purchasing power forward in time, so income earned now can be spent later. It is a poor store of value in a high-inflation economy, which is the one function of the three that inflation attacks directly - and the reason people in such economies switch to holding something else while still pricing everything in the local currency.
Fiat money
Money with no commodity behind it, accepted because everyone accepts it.In real lifeA ten-dollar note is a piece of paper worth ten dollars because the next shop will also treat it as ten dollars.Money that has no intrinsic value and is not backed by a commodity. Its value rests on general acceptance and on confidence that the issuer will not print without limit. Fiat money performs all three functions above; what it does not have is a fallback use, which is the difference between it and a gold coin.
Barter
Trading goods directly, with no money in between.In real lifeA farmer swapping a sack of potatoes for a neighbour’s help with a fence has completed a trade with no money involved at all.Exchange of one good or service directly for another. It requires a double coincidence of wants - the potato farmer has to find someone who both wants potatoes and can mend a fence - and the search cost of finding that match is the whole reason economies with money trade far more than economies without. A barter economy is not a poorer version of a money economy; it is one missing the medium of exchange.
M1
The narrow money supply: currency in circulation plus checkable deposits.also called the money stock, narrow money or the quantity of moneyA stem that says “the quantity of money” on the horizontal axis of the money market is naming the same total this aggregate measures.In real lifeThe notes in every wallet in the country plus every checking-account balance, added together.The money supply is measured using monetary aggregates designated as M1 and M2. M1 is the narrow one: currency held by the public plus demand deposits and other checkable deposits - assets that are already a means of payment. It deliberately leaves out anything that has to be moved first, which is why a savings balance is not in it.
On the exam

A household withdraws $2,000 in cash from its savings account and keeps the cash. Assuming nothing else changes, what happens to M1 and to M2 immediately?

  1. AM1 rises by $2,000; M2 rises by $2,000
  2. BM1 rises by $2,000; M2 is unchanged
  3. CM1 is unchanged; M2 falls by $2,000
  4. DM1 falls by $2,000; M2 rises by $2,000
Answer

B. Cash is in M1 and a savings balance is not, so moving one into the other raises M1. Both cash and savings are inside M2, so the withdrawal shuffles the composition of M2 without changing its total. The trick is that the two aggregates are nested, not parallel.

M2
M1 plus the near-money that takes one step to spend.In real lifeA savings account that has to be transferred into checking before the rent can be paid - real purchasing power, one step away.The broader aggregate: all of M1 plus savings deposits, small-denomination time deposits and retail money market fund shares. M1 sits inside M2, so an asset counted in M1 is automatically counted in M2 - and moving money between the two categories can change M1 while leaving M2 exactly where it was.
The monetary base
Currency in circulation plus bank reserves - what the central bank actually controls.also called M0, MB, high-powered money or base money“High-powered” is the pointed one: a dollar of base can support several dollars of money supply, which is exactly what the money multiplier measures.In real lifeEvery note in public hands plus every dollar the commercial banks are holding rather than lending.The monetary base (often labeled as M0 or MB) includes currency in circulation and bank reserves. It is NOT the money supply, and the difference is the point: deposits are in the money supply and not in the base, while reserves are in the base and in no monetary aggregate. The base is the quantity a central bank can change directly, which is why an open-market operation is described as moving the base first and the money supply only afterwards.

Trap — treating the monetary base as a bigger version of the money supply They overlap and neither contains the other. Bank reserves are in the base and in no monetary aggregate; demand deposits are in M1 and M2 and not in the base; currency in public hands is in all three. So a policy that changes the base by $50 billion does not change the money supply by $50 billion, and the ratio between the two changes is what the next topic gives a name to.
Worked example — sorting one household’s wealth A household holds $300 in cash, $2,000 in a checking account, $5,000 in a savings account and $8,000 of corporate stock.
Counted in M1: $300 + $2,000 = $2,300 - cash and the checkable deposit.
Counted in M2: $2,300 + $5,000 = $7,300 - all of M1, plus the savings balance.
Counted in neither: the $8,000 of stock. It is wealth; it is not a means of payment.
The check. M2 is larger than M1 and contains every dollar of it. If your M2 figure is smaller than your M1 figure, or the two do not share the same $2,300, one of them was built the wrong way.

4.4 Banks, balance sheets and the two kinds of reserves

Every question in this topic is a T-account, and every T-account question is really one subtraction done correctly. The whole difficulty is that three plausible numbers sit next to each other and only one of them is lendable.

A depository institution
A bank: it takes deposits and lends most of them out again.In real lifeThe high-street bank that holds a household’s current account and has also lent that household’s neighbour a mortgage.A commercial bank, credit union or similar firm whose business is accepting deposits and making loans. Depository institutions organize their assets and liabilities on balance sheets, which is how every exam question about them is presented: as a T-account with two sides that have to agree.
A balance sheet
Assets on the left, liabilities and owner’s equity on the right, and the two sides match.In real lifeA bank’s published accounts: what it owns and is owed on one page, what it owes on the other.A statement of what a bank owns (reserves, loans, securities, property) against what it owes (deposits) and what its owners have put in (equity). The identity is assets = liabilities + owner’s equity, not simply assets = liabilities, and forgetting the third term is what makes a T-account question look as if it does not balance. A deposit creates an asset and a liability of the same size and leaves equity untouched.
A demand deposit
Money in a checking account, payable to the depositor on demand.also called checkable deposits, checking accounts or current accountsA T-account may print any of these on its liability side, and every one of them is the same thing: money the bank owes you and you can take out today.In real lifeThe balance a card payment draws on. The bank does not have it in a drawer with your name on it - it has lent most of it out.A deposit the customer can withdraw at any time without notice. On the bank’s balance sheet it is a liability - the bank owes it - even though the same dollars appear as an asset in the depositor’s own accounting. Demand deposits are counted in M1, which is what makes bank lending capable of changing the money supply at all.
Fractional reserve banking
Banks keep a fraction of deposits and lend the rest.In real lifeEvery commercial bank in the world: if every depositor asked for their money on the same morning, no bank could pay them all.Depository institutions operate using fractional reserve banking: they hold only part of their deposits as reserves and lend out the remainder. This is not a flaw in the system, it is the system - it is what allows banks to make loans at all, and it is the mechanism by which the banking system as a whole can expand the money supply beyond the monetary base it started with.
Required reserves
The share of deposits a bank must hold and cannot lend.In real lifeA bank with $100 million of deposits and a 10 percent requirement has $10 million it is not allowed to touch.Banks’ reserves are divided into required reserves and excess reserves. Required reserves are the deposits multiplied by the required reserve ratio - the legal minimum the bank must keep. They are an asset of the bank and they are unavailable for lending, which is exactly why they are named separately from the reserves that are.
Excess reserves
Reserves above the requirement - the only part a bank can lend.In real lifeA cautious bank in a downturn sitting on far more cash than the rules demand, because it does not like the loans on offer.Total reserves minus required reserves. Excess reserves are the basis of expansion of the money supply by the banking system: a single bank can lend its excess reserves and no more, and every dollar that gets lent becomes somebody else’s deposit and starts the process again. Applying a multiplier to the whole deposit rather than to the excess reserves is the single most common arithmetic error in this unit.
On the exam

A bank holds $600 million in demand deposits and $90 million in total reserves. The required reserve ratio is 10 percent. What is the maximum amount this single bank can lend?

  1. A$90 million
  2. B$60 million
  3. C$30 million
  4. D$540 million
Answer

C. Required reserves are 10% of $600 million = $60 million. Excess reserves are $90m − $60m = $30 million, and a single bank can lend its excess reserves and nothing more. $90 million is the total reserves including the part the bank is not allowed to touch; $540 million is 90% of the deposits, which is what the bank could have lent if it had held no reserves at all.

The required reserve ratio
The fraction of deposits the law says a bank must keep as reserves.also called the reserve requirement, the legal reserve ratio or rrA stem saying “banks are required to hold 10 percent of deposits” has given you this ratio without using any of its names.In real lifeA central bank announcement that banks must hold 8 percent rather than 10 percent of deposits - one sentence that frees up cash across the whole system.The minimum ratio of reserves to deposits a bank is obliged to hold. It does two jobs on this course: it fixes required reserves for a given deposit base, and its reciprocal is the maximum value of the money multiplier. It is also, on the CED’s own list, one of the tools of monetary policy - though not one the Federal Reserve now leans on.

Excess reserves = $70,000 − $40,000 = $30,000.
Maximum this single bank can lend = $30,000, the excess reserves and no more.
The check. Three numbers were available - $400,000, $70,000 and $40,000 - and the answer is none of them. If your answer to “how much can this bank lend” is a figure that was printed in the question, it is almost certainly wrong.
Trap — a customer withdrawal A withdrawal takes cash out of reserves AND takes the same amount off deposits, so it moves both sides of the balance sheet at once. Required reserves fall (because deposits fell) but total reserves fall by the whole withdrawal, so excess reserves fall by more than the requirement did. A bank with no excess reserves to start with is left short and has to call in loans or borrow - which is why the question is worth setting.

4.4a The money multiplier, and the two numbers it is not

The CED gives the money multiplier two different lives one line apart: a ratio you measure, and a maximum you compute. Reading them as one idea makes the third statement in the sequence look like a contradiction, so they are kept apart here from the start.

The money multiplier
The money supply divided by the monetary base - a ratio you can look up.also called the simple money multiplier, the deposit expansion multiplier or the credit multiplier“Simple” is a warning label rather than a compliment: the simple version is the one POL-2.A.8 says overstates the answer, because it ignores both leakages.In real lifeA country whose money supply is four times the notes and reserves the central bank has issued has a money multiplier of four, whatever any formula predicts.The money multiplier is the ratio of the money supply to the monetary base. Note what that is: a measurement of two quantities that already exist, not a prediction. It is the number that tells you how far the banking system has stretched the base the central bank put in - and it is not the same object as the maximum value below, which is where students lose this topic.
Expansion of the money supply
How far a new dollar of reserves travels depends on the multiplier.In real lifeThe same $1 million of new reserves adds far more to the money supply in an economy where banks lend eagerly than in one where they sit on cash.The size of expansion of the money supply depends on the money multiplier. A larger multiplier means each dollar of new excess reserves supports more lending, more deposits and therefore more money before the process runs out. Because the multiplier depends on how much currency the public keeps and how much banks choose not to lend, the same central bank action can have very different effects in two economies.
The maximum money multiplier
One divided by the required reserve ratio - the ceiling, not the answer.In real lifeA 20 percent requirement gives a ceiling of 5: at the very most, each new dollar of excess reserves can support five dollars of money.The maximum value of the money multiplier can be calculated as the reciprocal of the required reserve ratio. It is what the multiplier would be if every dollar lent came straight back to a bank as a deposit and every bank lent every dollar it was permitted to. Exam questions signal that they want this number with the word “maximum”, or by stating that banks hold no excess reserves and there are no cash leakages - and a question without those words is usually not asking for it.
On the exam

The required reserve ratio is 20 percent. A central bank purchases $40 million of government bonds from commercial banks. Assuming banks lend all excess reserves and the public deposits every loan, what is the maximum possible increase in the money supply?

  1. A$40 million
  2. B$8 million
  3. C$200 million
  4. D$800 million
Answer

C. The purchase creates $40 million of new reserves, all of it excess because no new deposits came with it. The maximum multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5, so the ceiling is $40m × 5 = $200 million. $800 million is the answer you get from using 1 ÷ 0.05, and $8 million from multiplying by the ratio rather than by its reciprocal.

Why the simple multiplier overstates
Cash under mattresses and cautious banks both shrink the real multiplier.In real lifeA nervous saver who takes $500 out of the bank and keeps it at home has removed that $500 from every future round of lending.The amount predicted by the simple money multiplier may be overstated because it does not take into account a bank’s desire to hold excess reserves or the public holding more currency. Both are leakages out of the lending chain, and both are ordinary behaviour rather than malfunctions: a bank with nowhere safe to lend keeps the money, and a household that prefers cash keeps that too. Two named leakages, and a page that mentions only the first leaves a reader unable to explain a currency drain.

4.5 The money market

Two curves, one crossing, and one axis label that has to be read every single time. This is the graph a limited-reserves question draws - and topic 4.6 is where you find out that a great many questions are no longer set in that world.

The demand for money
How much money people want to hold, falling as the interest rate rises.In real lifeA saver who shifts spare cash into a bond fund when rates jump is moving down their own money demand curve.The demand for money shows the inverse relationship between the nominal interest rate and the quantity of money people want to hold. The words matter: money demand is a stock people choose to HOLD, not a flow they borrow. Its slope comes straight from the opportunity cost of holding money - the higher the nominal rate, the more interest a cash balance costs its holder, so the less of it they keep.
Transaction demand for money
The money people hold simply to buy things with.In real lifeThe float a household keeps in its checking account between one payday and the next, sized by the shopping it expects to do.The part of money demand that comes from money’s use as a medium of exchange: holding enough to pay for planned purchases. It depends on how much spending is going on rather than on the interest rate, which is why a higher price level or a higher real GDP shifts the whole money demand curve rather than moving along it.
The supply of money
A vertical line: the central bank sets the quantity, and the rate does not change it.In real lifeA central bank that announces a target for the money stock and hits it is drawing that vertical line itself.Given a monetary base determined by a country’s central bank, money supply is independent of the nominal interest rate. That independence is the REASON the curve is drawn vertical - the CED never uses the word “vertical”, and a page that gives the shape without the reason has taught a drawing convention rather than an idea. Nothing about a higher interest rate makes the central bank issue more money.
Equilibrium in the money market
The nominal rate at which the money people want to hold is the money there is.In real lifeThe overnight rate a financial press report quotes each morning is the market having settled at exactly this crossing.In the money market, equilibrium is achieved when the nominal interest rate is such that the quantities demanded and supplied of money are equal - the crossing of the downward-sloping money demand curve and the vertical money supply line. The variable that adjusts is the NOMINAL interest rate, because that is what this graph’s vertical axis measures.
On the exam

  2. BThe nominal interest rate falls and the quantity of money rises

B. The vertical money supply line moves right, so it meets the unchanged downward-sloping money demand curve further down: a lower nominal rate and a larger quantity of money. Worked example — why the curve slopes down, one household at a time A household holds $4,000 of wealth and can keep any of it in cash or in bonds.
At a nominal rate of 2%: keeping $3,000 in cash costs $60 a year in foregone interest. That is cheap, so the household keeps a large float for convenience.
At a nominal rate of 9%: the same $3,000 of cash now costs $270 a year. The household shifts $2,000 into bonds and keeps $1,000.
The result. Higher rate, smaller quantity of money held. Nothing about the household changed - only the price of the choice - so this is a movement ALONG money demand, and repeating it across every household in the economy is what draws the curve.

4.5a Adjustment and shifts in the money market

Two shifters, one on each side, and the CED names exactly one example of each. Everything else in this topic is the same rule every market on this course obeys: a change in the graph’s own axis variable cannot shift either curve.

Disequilibrium in the money market
A rate off equilibrium leaves a gap, and the gap pushes the rate back.In real lifeBanks holding more cash than they want to at the going rate start buying bonds with it, and in doing so bid the rate down.Disequilibrium nominal interest rates create surpluses and shortages in the money market. Market forces drive nominal interest rates toward equilibrium. Above the equilibrium rate people hold more money than they want and buy bonds with the surplus, pushing bond prices up and the rate down; below it they are short of money and sell bonds, pushing prices down and the rate up. The bond market is the mechanism by which the money market clears.
What shifts the money market
The price level shifts money demand; monetary policy shifts money supply.In real lifeA year of high inflation leaves every household needing a bigger cash float for the same weekly shop, at every interest rate.Factors that shift the demand for money, such as changes in the price level, and supply of money, such as monetary policy, change the equilibrium nominal interest rate. These are the CED’s own two examples and it names no others - one determinant on each side. A rise in the price level, or a rise in real GDP, means more spending to finance and so more money wanted at every rate: money demand shifts right and the equilibrium nominal rate rises, with the central bank having done nothing at all.
On the exam

  2. BMoney demand shifts left and the nominal interest rate falls

  4. DThe economy moves down along an unchanged money demand curve
Answer

A. A higher price level means the same basket of transactions needs more money to finance, at every interest rate - so the whole money demand curve moves right. Against a vertical money supply that has not moved, the crossing is at a higher nominal rate. Option C describes an action by the central bank, and the stem says it took none.

Worked example — a price level rise, step by step An economy’s price level rises by 10%. The central bank does nothing at all.

Step 2. Money supply is unchanged - a vertical line in the same place.

Step 4. The new equilibrium is at the SAME quantity of money and a HIGHER nominal rate.

A stem saying “the price level rises” or “real GDP rises” is describing a genuine shift, because both change how much money is wanted at every rate.

4.6 Monetary policy: the tools, and the two regimes

The 2026 CED rewrote this topic, and the rewrite is not cosmetic: it decides which tool moves the interest rate, and it means the graph in topic 4.5 is a picture of one regime rather than of monetary policy in general.

Monetary policy
A central bank acting on interest rates to hit a macroeconomic goal.In real lifeA central bank raising rates in response to a run of high inflation figures, and saying so at a press conference.Central banks implement monetary policies to achieve macroeconomic goals, such as price stability. Price stability is the goal the CED names, and full employment is the other one every rate decision is weighed against. Unlike fiscal policy, monetary policy needs no legislature: a committee decides and the change takes effect, which is why its lags are shorter and different in kind.
The discount rate
The rate a central bank charges banks that borrow directly from it.In real lifeA bank that finds itself short of reserves overnight and borrows from the central bank rather than from another bank.An administered rate the central bank sets for its own lending to commercial banks. It is on the CED’s list of tools and it is the oldest of them, but it is not the tool that moves the policy rate in an ample-reserves system - it sits above the policy rate as a ceiling rather than setting it.
Interest on reserves
What the central bank pays banks for holding reserves - and the key US tool.also called IORB, interest on reserve balances or the administered rateThis is the Federal Reserve’s key policy tool by the CED’s own parenthesis, so a question that moves it is moving the main lever, not a secondary one.In real lifeA commercial bank deciding not to lend overnight at 4.6 percent because the central bank will pay it 4.7 percent for doing nothing.An administered rate paid on the reserves banks hold at the central bank. It is the Federal Reserve’s key policy tool, because it sets a floor under what any bank will accept for lending its reserves elsewhere: no bank lends at less than it can earn risk-free. Raise it and the whole market rate follows it up, without a single bond changing hands.
Open market operations
The central bank buying or selling government bonds to change reserves.also called OMOs, open-market purchases and sales or buying and selling government securitiesA question that says the central bank “bought bonds on the open market” and one that says it “conducted an expansionary open market operation” are describing the identical action.In real lifeA central bank trading desk buying government securities from banks all morning, paying for them by crediting those banks’ reserve accounts.One of the tools of monetary policy: buying or selling government securities in the market. When the central bank conducts an open-market purchase (sale), reserves increase (decrease), thereby increasing (decreasing) the monetary base. That claim holds in every economy and under every regime. What it then does to the MONEY SUPPLY, and to the interest rate, depends on the regime - which is the next two entries.
On the exam

A central bank conducts an open-market purchase of government bonds. Which of the following is certain to happen, regardless of whether the banking system has limited or ample reserves?

  1. AThe money supply rises by a multiple of the purchase
  2. BBank reserves and the monetary base both rise
  3. CThe policy rate falls

Answer

B. The purchase is paid for by crediting bank reserve accounts, so reserves and the monetary base rise by the amount purchased - always. The multiplied rise in the money supply in option A requires limited reserves AND banks that lend; the fall in the policy rate in option C is a limited-reserves result. Only B is unconditional.

A limited-reserves system
Reserves are scarce, so adding them pushes the interest rate down.In real lifeThe pre-2008 United States, and the textbook money market picture that comes from it.A banking system holding barely more reserves than it is required to. It is also the regime in which changing the quantity of money is what moves the nominal interest rate, which is what the money market graph in topic 4.5 is drawing.
An ample-reserves system
Reserves are abundant, so the rate is set by what the central bank pays on them.In real lifeThe United States today: banks hold vastly more reserves than required, and the Federal Reserve moves rates by announcing a number.A banking system holding far more reserves than it needs. The banking system in the United States has ample reserves, and the Federal Reserve’s key policy tool is interest on reserves. With reserves already abundant, adding more changes nothing - no bank was short - so the quantity tool stops working and the administered rate does the whole job. This is the 2026 CED’s substantive rewrite of the topic, and it is where the recent exam points sit.

The policy rate
The overnight rate banks lend to each other at, and the one the central bank targets.also called the federal funds rate, the overnight interbank lending rate or the target rateThe CED gives the general name and then the American one in a bracket. In the United States, this is the federal funds rate - the rate at which banks lend reserves to one another overnight. The central bank does not set it by decree; it sets administered rates and conducts operations until the market rate lands inside the target range.
The reserve market model

4.6a What monetary policy does to output, and how late it arrives

A rate change is not the point of monetary policy - it is the first link in a chain that ends at output and the price level. This is where the money market, the reserve market and the AD-AS diagram turn out to be three pictures of one event.

How a rate change reaches the economy
Central banks move the nominal rate; the rate moves borrowing, and borrowing moves AD.In real lifeA firm that shelves a new warehouse when borrowing costs jump, and a household that puts off replacing the car for the same reason.Central banks can influence the nominal interest rate in the short run, which in turn will affect investment and consumption. HOW they influence it depends on the regime. In an economy with limited reserves, the central bank can influence the nominal interest rate by changing the money supply. In an economy with ample reserves, changes in the money supply do not effectively change the nominal interest rate; instead, the central bank can influence the nominal interest rate by changing its administered interest rates. Two regimes, two mechanisms, one destination.
Expansionary monetary policy
Lower rates to close a recessionary gap.In real lifeA central bank cutting rates during a downturn so that mortgages and business loans get cheaper and spending picks up.Expansionary monetary policy is used to restore full employment when the economy is in a negative (i.e., recessionary) output gap. Under limited reserves that means buying bonds to raise the money supply; under ample reserves it means cutting the administered rate. Either way the nominal rate falls, investment and consumption rise, and aggregate demand shifts right.
On the exam

An economy with ample reserves is in short-run equilibrium with a recessionary output gap. Which central bank action is expansionary, and what happens to real output in the short run?

  1. ARaise the interest rate paid on reserves; real output falls
  2. BLower the interest rate paid on reserves; real output rises
  3. CRaise the required reserve ratio; real output rises
  4. DSell government bonds; real output rises
Answer

B. In an ample-reserves system the administered rate is the working tool, so cutting the rate paid on reserves pulls the policy rate down with it. Cheaper borrowing raises investment and consumption, aggregate demand shifts right, and short-run real output rises toward full employment. Options C and D are both contractionary actions attached to an expansionary result.

Contractionary monetary policy
Higher rates to close an inflationary gap.In real lifeA central bank raising rates through a year of rising prices, and saying openly that it expects unemployment to rise as a result.Contractionary monetary policy is used to restore full employment when the economy is in a positive (i.e., inflationary) output gap. Selling bonds under limited reserves, or raising the administered rate under ample reserves, pushes the nominal rate up, discourages borrowing, and shifts aggregate demand left - lowering the price level and, in the short run, real output too.
What monetary policy can move
Interest rates, aggregate demand, real output and the price level - in that order.In real lifeA rate cut announced in March showing up in mortgage approvals by summer and in the inflation figures a year later.Monetary policy can influence interest rates, aggregate demand, real output, and the price level. The ordering is the useful part: the rate moves first, spending follows, and output and prices come last. An answer that jumps straight from “the central bank bought bonds” to “the price level rose” has skipped the two steps a rubric awards marks for.
The three models this topic uses
Money market, reserve market, or AD-AS - and a question tells you which.In real lifeAn exam question that hands you a blank set of axes labelled “Policy Rate” and “Quantity of Reserves” has already chosen the model for you.A money market model, a reserve market model, and/or the AD–AS model may be used to demonstrate the short-run effects of monetary policy. Three pictures of one event: the money market and the reserve market each show the rate moving, in their own regime, and AD-AS shows what the rate change does to output and the price level. Reading the axis labels tells you which one you have been given.
Lags to monetary policy
Time to see the problem, then time for the economy to respond.In real lifeA central bank that cut rates in the third month of a downturn and saw hiring pick up more than a year later.In reality, there are lags to monetary policy caused by the time it takes to recognize a problem in the economy and the time it takes the economy to adjust to the policy action. Two lags, and this pair is NOT the fiscal pair: fiscal policy’s lags include deciding on and implementing the action, which monetary policy largely skips because no legislature has to vote. Swapping the two lists is a standard way to lose a free-response mark.

  1. AThe time to pass legislation and the time to implement a spending program
  2. BThe time to recognize a problem and the time for the economy to adjust to the action
  3. CThe time to collect taxes and the time for households to receive transfers
  4. DThere are no lags, because a central bank can act on any day it chooses
Answer

B. Monetary policy skips most of the legislative delay, which is what makes it faster than fiscal policy - but a central bank still cannot see a downturn until the data confirms it, and the economy still takes time to respond once rates have moved. Option A is the fiscal list, and confusing the two is exactly what this distinction exists to prevent.

Worked example — the full chain, written the way a rubric reads it An economy with ample reserves is in a recessionary gap. The central bank wants to close it.
1. The action. Lower the interest rate paid on reserves - the administered rate.
2. The rate. No bank will lend reserves for less than it can earn holding them, so the policy rate falls with it.
3. Borrowing. A lower nominal rate makes loans cheaper: investment and consumption both rise.
4. AD. Higher investment and consumption shift aggregate demand right.
5. The outcome. Short-run real output rises toward full employment and the price level rises.
The check. Five steps, and steps 2 and 3 are the ones an answer under time pressure skips. A response that goes straight from “cut the rate” to “the price level rises” has jumped over the two links a rubric awards marks for.
The lags to monetary policy against the lags to fiscal policy

The line that separates themBoth lists start with recognition and then diverge. Monetary policy skips most of the deciding, which is what makes it the faster instrument; what it cannot skip is the time it takes the economy to adjust, and reciting the fiscal list on a monetary question is a standard free-response loss.
Trap — reciting the fiscal lags on a monetary question The CED gives monetary policy exactly two lags: the time it takes to recognize a problem in the economy, and the time it takes the economy to adjust to the policy action. Unit 3’s fiscal list names deciding on and implementing the action instead, and a central bank committee largely skips both. The two lists are close enough to look interchangeable and are not, and swapping them is a standard free-response loss.

4.7 The loanable funds market, and where the funds come from

The second of the unit’s two markets, and the one that looks almost exactly like the first. Read the vertical axis before anything else: nominal means you are in the money market, and real means you are here.

The line that separates themTwo nearly identical drawings answering two different questions, and the axis label is the whole defence. Fisher joins them: real = nominal minus inflation. A question about a central bank action, or about the price level, is on the money market; a question about saving, government borrowing or investment is on loanable funds.
The loanable funds market
Where savers meet borrowers, and the real interest rate is the price.also called the market for loanable funds, the credit market or the market for saving and borrowingWhatever it is called, its vertical axis is the REAL interest rate - which is the one thing that keeps it apart from the money market on an unlabelled figure.In real lifeA bank taking in deposits from households at one rate and lending them to a builder at a higher one is standing in the middle of this market.The loanable funds market describes the behavior of savers and borrowers. Its vertical axis is the real interest rate, not the nominal one - that single label is what keeps it apart from the money market, whose graph looks almost identical. Money demand is a stock people want to HOLD; loanable funds is a flow of saving being lent, and the two markets answer different questions about the same economy.
The demand for loanable funds
Borrowers want less when borrowing is dearer, so it slopes down.In real lifeA manufacturer with a list of possible projects funds all of them at 2 percent and only the best two at 9 percent.The demand for loanable funds shows the inverse relationship between real interest rates and the quantity demanded of loanable funds. Firms borrowing to invest, households borrowing to buy houses and cars, and governments borrowing to cover a deficit are all in this curve, and every one of them wants to borrow less as the real cost of borrowing rises.
The supply of loanable funds
Savers offer more when saving pays more, so it slopes up.In real lifeA household that leaves more of each paycheck in a savings account once the account starts paying a genuinely better return.The supply of loanable funds shows the positive relationship between real interest rates and the quantity supplied of loanable funds. This is the second half of a contrast worth holding onto: loanable funds supply SLOPES UP because savers respond to the return, while money supply is VERTICAL because the central bank sets it and the rate does not move it.
On the exam

Which of the following correctly describes the two supply curves in this unit?

  1. ABoth the money supply curve and the loanable funds supply curve slope upward
  2. BBoth are vertical, because both quantities are set by the central bank
  3. CThe money supply curve is vertical; the loanable funds supply curve slopes upward
  4. DThe money supply curve slopes upward; the loanable funds supply curve is vertical
Answer

C. Money supply is vertical because it is independent of the nominal interest rate - the central bank sets the quantity. The supply of loanable funds slopes up because it comes from savers, who genuinely do save more when the real return is higher. Drawing either one with the other’s shape gets every shift question that follows wrong.

National savings
Private savings plus public savings - all the saving done inside the country.In real lifeA country whose households save heavily while its government runs a large deficit may end up saving very little in total.In the absence of international borrowing and lending, national savings is the sum of public savings and private savings. It is what stands behind the supply of loanable funds, and it is where a budget deficit does its damage: public savings is tax revenue minus government spending, so a deficit makes it negative and drags the national total down.
On the exam

In a closed economy, private savings are $700 billion, tax revenue is $500 billion and government spending is $560 billion. What is national savings?

  1. A$1,200 billion
  2. B$760 billion
  3. C$640 billion
  4. D$60 billion
Answer

C. Public savings is tax revenue minus government spending: $500bn − $560bn = −$60 billion, a deficit. National savings is private plus public: $700bn + (−$60bn) = $640 billion. Option A adds tax revenue to private savings and forgets that the government spent it; option D reports the deficit alone.

Private savings
What households and firms did not spend out of their income.In real lifeMoney left in a savings account at the end of the month, plus profits a company retains instead of paying out.Disposable income that households and firms do not consume. It is the larger of the two components of national savings in most economies and it is the one that responds to the real interest rate, which is why a change in saving behaviour moves the supply of loanable funds.
Public savings
Tax revenue minus government spending - negative whenever the budget is in deficit.In real lifeA government that collected $500 billion and spent $560 billion contributed minus $60 billion to the country’s saving that year.The government’s own contribution to national savings. A budget surplus is positive public savings and adds to the funds available to borrowers; a deficit is negative public savings and subtracts from them. Reading it as a positive number whenever the government collects tax at all is the mistake that turns a crowding-out question into an addition.
Net capital inflow
Saving arriving from abroad, added to national savings in an open economy.also called net inflow of financial capital, net foreign saving or international financial capital inflowsA stem describing “a decrease in financial capital inflows” is telling you the supply of loanable funds is about to shift LEFT.In real lifeForeign pension funds buying a country’s government bonds are supplying loanable funds inside that country’s market.For an open economy, investment equals national savings plus net capital inflow. The inflow is ADDED, not netted out: it is why a country can invest more than it saves, and it is the reason a large government deficit does not always push the real interest rate up as far as the closed-economy picture predicts. If the inflow dries up, the supply of loanable funds falls and the real rate rises even with domestic saving unchanged.
Equilibrium in the loanable funds market
The real interest rate at which saving offered equals borrowing wanted.In real lifeThe prevailing mortgage rate, once the difference between what savers wanted and what borrowers wanted has been squeezed out.In the loanable funds market, equilibrium is achieved when the real interest rate is such that the quantities demanded and supplied of loanable funds are equal. The variable that adjusts here is the REAL interest rate - the same sentence with a different rate in it from the money market’s equilibrium condition, and the difference is the whole reason both statements exist.
The two supply curves, once and for all Money supply is vertical. The supply of loanable funds slopes upward. Money supply is a quantity the central bank chose, and no interest rate changes its mind. The supply of loanable funds comes from savers, who genuinely do save more when the real return is higher - which the CED states as a positive relationship. Drawing either curve with the other’s shape gets every shift question that follows it wrong.
Worked example — national savings, closed and then open An economy reports private savings of $700 billion, tax revenue of $500 billion and government spending of $560 billion.
Public savings = $500bn − $560bn = −$60 billion. The budget is in deficit, so this number is negative.
National savings = $700bn + (−$60bn) = $640 billion.
Closed economy: investment = national savings = $640 billion.
Now open the economy, with a net capital inflow of $90 billion.
Open economy: investment = $640bn + $90bn = $730 billion.
The check. National savings came out SMALLER than private savings, because the deficit subtracted. And investment in the open economy came out LARGER than national savings, because the inflow added. If either comparison ran the other way, a sign has been dropped.
Trap — putting a question on the wrong graph A question about a central bank action, or about the price level, belongs on the money market and its nominal rate. A question about saving, government borrowing, investment or capital from abroad belongs on loanable funds and its real rate. The two diagrams share a shape and share almost nothing else, and an answer drawn on the wrong one usually has the right words attached to the wrong axis.

4.7a Shifts in loanable funds: deficits, saving and crowding out

Every shift in this market moves two numbers, and the second one is the half that gets dropped. It is also the half that tells you which side of the market was hit, and the half crowding out is actually measured in.

Disequilibrium in the loanable funds market
Too high a real rate leaves savings unlent; too low leaves borrowers queuing.In real lifeA lender with more deposits than it can find borrowers for cuts the rate it charges until the loans go out.Disequilibrium real interest rates create surpluses and shortages in the loanable funds market. Market forces drive real interest rates toward equilibrium. Above equilibrium there is a surplus of funds - savers are offering more than borrowers will take - and competition among lenders pushes the real rate down; below it there is a shortage and borrowers bid the rate up.
Fiscal policy in the loanable funds market
Spending, taxes and borrowing all reach the real interest rate through this one graph.In real lifeA government that announces a large new spending program and funds it by issuing bonds is adding itself to the demand side of this market.The loanable funds market can be used to show the effects of government spending, taxes, and borrowing on interest rates. Three levers, not one. Borrowing adds to the demand for funds directly; higher taxes raise public savings and add to supply; higher spending lowers public savings and reduces it. A page that shows only government borrowing has left out the two routes the CED expects the model to handle.
Crowding out
Government borrowing raises the real rate, and private investment falls as a result.also called the crowding-out effect or the financial crowding-out effectAlways the fall in PRIVATE investment caused by a higher real interest rate. The total quantity of funds lent rises at the same time, which is why the two halves have to be reported separately.In real lifeA construction firm that abandons a planned factory because the loan it needs now costs three points more than it did last year.The fall in private investment caused by the higher real interest rate that government borrowing produces. It is the SECOND half of a deficit answer and the half most often left off: the first half is that the real rate rises, and the second is what that higher rate does to firms who were going to borrow. Crowding out is measured in QUANTITY - it is a movement back along the original demand curve to the new, higher rate.
On the exam

A government sharply increases its borrowing to fund a deficit, with no change in private saving behaviour. What happens in the loanable funds market to the real interest rate and to private investment?

  1. AThe real interest rate rises and private investment falls
  2. BThe real interest rate rises and private investment rises
  3. CThe real interest rate falls and private investment rises
  4. DThe real interest rate is unchanged and private investment falls
Answer

A. The government joins the demand side, so demand for loanable funds shifts right and the equilibrium real rate rises. At that higher rate, firms move back up their own unchanged demand curve and borrow less - which is crowding out. Option B is the answer that stops after the first half: total funds lent do rise, but the PRIVATE share of them falls.

What shifts loanable funds
A tax credit shifts demand; a change in saving habits shifts supply. Both move price and quantity.In real lifeA government offering firms a tax break on new equipment sees more of them wanting to borrow, at every real interest rate.Factors that shift the demand (such as an investment tax credit) and supply (such as changes in saving behavior) of loanable funds change the equilibrium interest rate and the equilibrium quantity of funds. Both halves are examined. The rate is the half everyone reports; the quantity is the half crowding out is actually measured in, and an answer that names only the rate has answered half the question.

Worked example — a deficit, reported in full A government increases borrowing by $120 billion to fund a deficit. Private saving behaviour does not change.
1. The demand for loanable funds shifts right - the government is now one of the borrowers.
2. The equilibrium real interest rate rises.
3. The equilibrium quantity of funds lent rises too, because the higher rate draws out more saving along the unchanged supply curve.
4. At that higher rate, private firms move back along their own original demand curve and borrow less. That reduction is crowding out.

Trap — naming the rate and stopping Factors that shift the demand and supply of loanable funds change the equilibrium interest rate AND the equilibrium quantity of funds. The rate is the half every summary reports. The quantity is the half a rubric asks for separately, and it is the only place crowding out can be seen - so an answer that names the rate and stops has answered half of a two-part question.
