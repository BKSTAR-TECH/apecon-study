1.1 Scarcity

Economics begins with one observation and one consequence. The observation is that there is not enough of most things to go round. The consequence is that every use of a resource is also a refusal to use it for something else.

The sentence the CED actually writes Individuals and societies are forced to choose because most resources are scarce. Read the hedge. Not “all” — most. Scarcity is a property a resource can lack, and the exam has asked directly what would happen to opportunity cost if it did. If nothing were scarce, nothing would have to be given up, and there would be no such thing as an opportunity cost to calculate.
Scarcity
There is not enough to go round, so somebody has to choose.In real lifeA city with one ambulance service and two emergencies on opposite sides of town at the same moment. Nobody is being stingy; there is one ambulance.The condition in which wants exceed the resources available to meet them. It is the reason economics exists as a subject, and the CED states it with a hedge worth reading carefully: individuals and societies are forced to make choices because most resources are scarce. Not all of them. Scarcity is a property a resource can lack, which is what stops “scarce” being a synonym for “limited” and what makes the counterexamples below worth knowing.
On the exam

In an economy where scarcity did not exist, which of the following would be true?

  1. AEvery choice would still carry an opportunity cost
  2. BNo choice would carry an opportunity cost, because nothing would have to be given up
  3. COpportunity costs would be higher, because more goods would be available
  4. DOnly capital goods would carry an opportunity cost
Answer

B. An opportunity cost is the alternative surrendered. If a resource is unlimited, using it for one thing does not stop you using it for another, so there is nothing to surrender and no cost to measure.

A resource that is not scarce
A resource one person using does not stop another using.In real lifeThe Pythagorean theorem. Every builder in the world can use it at once, and none of them uses it up. So can a piece of open-source software, and so can sunlight falling on a desert.The CED says most resources are scarce, and the exception is what makes the word mean something. Established knowledge, a mathematical result and a broadcast signal can all be used by one more person at no cost to anyone already using them, so no choice has to be made about who gets them. Everything else in this course - land, hours of labour, machinery, a hospital bed, a barrel of oil - can only be in one place doing one thing, and is therefore scarce.

What the resources are

Four categories, and every input to every production process in the course belongs to exactly one of them. Each earns its own payment, and those four payments are where household income comes from — which is the loop Unit 2 measures as GDP.

Factors of production
The four kinds of resource everything is made out of.also called economic resources, inputs or the factorsA stem that says “an increase in the economy’s resources” and a stem that says “an increase in the factors of production” are asking for the same answer.In real lifeA bakery uses the site it stands on, the bakers, the ovens, and whoever decided to open a bakery there rather than a bike shop. That is one of each.Land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship - the four categories every input belongs to. Each earns its own payment, and those four payments together are where household income comes from, which is the loop the circular flow draws and the loop Unit 2 measures as GDP. Money is not among them. Neither is time. The exam tests this as a “which of the following is NOT” and the money option is reliably present.
On the exam

Which of the following is NOT a factor of production?

  1. AThe delivery van a courier firm owns
  2. BThe bank loan the firm took out to buy the van
  3. CThe driver’s licence and training
  4. DThe land the firm’s depot stands on
Answer

B. Money buys factors of production; it produces nothing by itself. The van is capital, the training is human capital, and the depot site is land. A loan is a claim on money, and money is not a resource in the economist’s sense.

Landpaid in rent
Everything nature supplied.In real lifeThe seabed a wind farm is anchored to, the wind that turns the blades, and the iron ore the towers were smelted from are all land.All natural resources: the ground itself, what grows on it, what is under it and the weather over it. It is deliberately broader than the everyday word. A macro stem describing an oil discovery, a drought or a newly navigable river has changed the economy’s stock of land, and that is a change in a factor of production.
Labourpaid in wages
Human effort, of any kind, applied to production.In real lifeThe surgeon, the porter and the person who wrote the hospital’s scheduling software are all labour. So is the effort, not the person.The physical and mental work people contribute. Its quantity is the size of the workforce and its quality is what the course calls human capital - the skills and training embodied in workers. Both matter in macro: an economy grows when the workforce grows, and it also grows when the same workforce is better trained, and those are two different sentences a stem can give you.
Capitalpaid in interest
Things that were produced so they can produce other things.also called physical capital, investment goods or producer goodsNever money. A macro stem about “capital” on a PPC means machines, buildings and tools — the things used to make other things.In real lifeA port crane, a delivery van, a hospital scanner and a factory building. All were made, and all exist to make something else.Manufactured resources used in further production - machines, tools, buildings, infrastructure. Two warnings. It is never money: cash buys capital and is not capital. And a good is capital because of what it is for, not what it is: an oven in a restaurant is capital, and the same oven in a kitchen at home is a consumer good. Capital is the factor macro cares about most, because choosing to build more of it today is the one decision that moves the whole frontier tomorrow.
Entrepreneurshippaid in profit
Whoever decides what to make and carries the risk of being wrong.In real lifeThe person who put a coffee cart outside a station at 6 a.m. and paid for the cart, the beans and the pitch before a single customer appeared.The factor that organises the other three and bears the uncertainty. Its payment is profit, which is the only one of the four that can be negative - a wage is owed whatever happens, and a residual is not. That asymmetry is what makes it a separate category rather than a specialised kind of labour.
Trap — money is not a factor of production Money buys resources; it does not produce anything. A firm’s bank balance, a loan and a share issue are all ways of getting hold of land, labour and capital, and none of them is capital in the economist’s sense. The exam asks this as a “which of the following is NOT” and the money option is always there.

The other word this unit needs before anything else is the one that names the giving-up. Every choice made under scarcity has a trade-off, and the best of the things given up has a name and a number.

Trade-off
Having more of one thing means having less of another.In real lifeFour hours before an exam. Every hour spent revising economics is an hour not spent on maths, and there is no arrangement of the evening that avoids that.The relationship scarcity forces between any two uses of the same resource. The CED spells it “tradeoffs” and the PPC exists to show them: every point on the curve is a different trade-off between the two goods, and the curve slopes downward precisely because a trade-off cannot be avoided. A trade-off names the whole set of things given up; the opportunity cost picks out one of them.
Opportunity cost
The value of the next best thing you gave up.In real lifeTurning down an $80 shift to revise means revising costs $80, whatever the tuition was. The money never left your account, and it is still the cost.The value of the next best alternative forgone - one alternative, the best of the rejected ones, never the sum of all of them. On a PPC it is measured in units of the other good, which is why an opportunity cost in this unit is a quantity rather than a price. A cost quoted in the same good you are buying is always wrong: the cost of a car is a number of computers, not a number of cars.
On the exam

A government can use a budget surplus to build a hospital, resurface a motorway it values slightly less, or repay debt it values least of the three. If it builds the hospital, the opportunity cost of the hospital is

  1. Athe motorway and the debt repayment together
  2. Bthe motorway alone
  3. Cthe debt repayment alone
  4. Dnothing, because the money was available
Answer

B. Opportunity cost is the single next-best alternative surrendered, not the sum of every rejected option. The debt repayment was also given up, but it was never the best alternative, so it is not the cost.

A trade-off against an opportunity cost
How many things it namesWhat it is measured inWhere it shows on a PPC
Trade-offAll the alternatives given upNothing — it is a relationship, not a numberThe fact that the curve slopes down at all
Opportunity costExactly one: the best of themUnits of the other goodThe steepness of the curve between two points
The line that separates themA trade-off is the situation; an opportunity cost is the number. Scarcity forces a trade-off between every pair of uses, and the opportunity cost picks out the single best alternative you actually surrendered. Adding up every rejected option is the standard wrong answer, and it is wrong because you could only ever have taken one of them.

1.2 Opportunity cost and the PPC: reading the curve

One model, five ideas, and the CED puts all five in one sentence. The trap is that a page which draws a bowed curve and labels the opportunity cost has covered two of them while looking finished.

MOD-1.B.2, in full The PPC illustrates scarcity, opportunity cost, efficiency, underutilized resources, and economic growth or contraction.

Scarcity is the fact that the curve has an edge at all. Opportunity cost is its slope. Efficiency is being on it. Underutilized resources is being inside it. Growth and contraction are the curve itself moving, and that is section 1.2a.

The model itself, and the vocabulary for a point.

Production possibilities curve
Every combination of two goods an economy could produce using everything it has.also called production possibilities frontier (PPF), production possibilities boundary or transformation curveThe macro CED writes PPC everywhere and so does the exam. PPF is what most college textbooks and most practice material call it. Same curve, same rules, same marks.In real lifeA statistics office publishing what the country could grow and build this year if nobody were idle. Real countries are never on it exactly; the curve is the ceiling, not the forecast.A model used to show the tradeoffs associated with allocating resources. Two goods on two axes, and a boundary between what is attainable and what is not. It assumes the economy’s resources and its technology are fixed, which is what makes the curve stay still - and what makes a change in either of those the only way to move it. The macro course gives it a further job the micro course does not: the curve is standing in for the economy’s full employment level of output, which is why a point inside it will turn out to be a recession.
Efficiency
Being ON the curve: no output going spare.In real lifeA factory running every line at capacity with no worker standing idle. It could still be making the wrong things, and it would still be efficient in this sense.Producing at a point on the frontier, where the only way to get more of one good is to accept less of the other. Every point on the curve is efficient and the model has nothing to say about which of them is best - that is a question about what people want, not about production. In macro this state has a second name you will meet in Unit 2: it is full employment of the economy’s resources.
Underutilized resources
Being INSIDE the curve: resources sitting idle.In real lifeA shipyard with two of its four docks empty and welders on reduced hours. The docks and the welders exist; they are not being used.Producing at a point inside the frontier, so that some resources are unemployed or badly deployed. A point with underutilized resources is attainable and wasteful at once: the economy could have more of both goods without acquiring anything new. This is the concept the exam most often finds missing, because it has no diagram of its own and every classroom PPC is drawn with the interesting points on the curve. It is also the one that matters most later: an economy inside its frontier is the picture of a recession.
On the exam

In the figure, an economy is producing at point B. Which of the following is true?

  1. AThe economy could produce more of both goods with the resources it already has
  2. BThe economy has grown relative to point A
  3. CPoint B is unattainable without more resources
  4. DThe economy must give up some of one good to get more of the other
Answer

A. B lies inside the frontier, so some resources are idle. Putting them to work raises output of both goods without any new resource or technology - which is exactly why moving from B to the curve is not economic growth.

Unattainable
Being OUTSIDE the curve: not possible now, however desirable.In real lifeA country announcing an output target its entire workforce and capital stock could not reach even flat out. Announcing it does not move the curve.A combination beyond the frontier. Not forbidden, not inefficient - simply not producible with the resources and technology the economy currently has. There are exactly two ways to reach one: the frontier moves outward, which is economic growth, or the economy trades, which lets it consume outside its curve while still producing on it. Those two are not the same thing and topic 1.3 turns on the difference.
Three verdicts on a point
Where the point isAttainable?Wasting anything?What the exam calls it
EfficiencyYesNo — everything is in useOn the curve: efficient, full employment of resources
Underutilized resourcesYesYes — resources are idleInside the curve: inefficient, underutilized resources
UnattainableNoNot applicable — it is not happeningOutside the curve: unattainable
The line that separates themOne question decides all three: is the point on, inside, or outside the line? Nothing else about the point matters, and no amount of information about what the economy would prefer changes the verdict. Note that all three points on a curve are equally efficient — the model cannot say which of them is best, because that is a question about what people want rather than about what can be produced.

Why the curve bows, and when it does not

The shape is not a convention. It is a consequence, and the CED names all three possibilities: MOD-1.B.3 says the shape depends on whether opportunity costs are constant, increasing, or decreasing.

Constant opportunity cost
Every extra unit costs the same as the last. The PPC is a straight line.In real lifeA print shop whose two products use the same press in the same way. Switching a batch from posters to flyers costs the same either way round, whichever batch it is.The case where the trade rate never changes, so the frontier is straight. It arises when resources are equally suited to both goods, and it is the case that shows up under almost every comparative-advantage table in topic 1.3: a table saying “60 hammocks OR 40 looms” and nothing else is describing constant opportunity cost. Students taught only the bowed curve treat the straight one as an error. It is not - it is the special case, and the exam relies on it.
Increasing opportunity cost
Each extra unit costs more than the last. The PPC bows outward.In real lifeTurning farmland over to vineyards. The first fields you convert are the ones that were poor at wheat and good at grapes; by the tenth you are ploughing up your best wheat land.The general case, and the reason the classroom PPC is bowed. Resources are not equally suited to both uses, so the ones switched first are the ones least valuable where they were, and each further switch surrenders more. The curve gets steeper from left to right, and that steepening IS the increasing cost - a bowed curve is not a stylistic choice, it is a claim about the resources.
Decreasing opportunity cost
Each extra unit costs LESS than the last. The PPC bows inward.In real lifeAn industry where concentrating effort pays off - a shipyard that builds one ship badly and ten well, because the tenth is built by people who have now built nine.The third possibility the CED lists, and the one no exam figure draws. The frontier would be bowed toward the origin, and the trade rate would get gentler as you specialise. It is named here because MOD-1.B.3 names it and a stem may list all three; nothing else in this course uses it. Knowing it exists and that the curve would bow the other way is the whole of what is wanted.
The three cost patterns, and the shape each one draws
Cost patternEach extra unit costsShape of the PPCWhere you meet it
Constant opportunity costThe same as the last oneA straight lineUnder almost every comparative-advantage table in 1.3
Increasing opportunity costMore than the last oneBowed outward, away from the originThe default classroom PPC, and most figures on the paper
Decreasing opportunity costLess than the last oneBowed inward, toward the originNamed by the CED; drawn by nothing in this course
The line that separates themAsk what happens to the cost of the next unit as you keep going. Resources equally good at both jobs give a constant cost and a straight line; resources suited to one job more than the other give a rising cost and a bowed curve, because you convert the least-suited resources first. The shape is a conclusion, not a drawing convention — which is why a straight PPC in a trade question is correct rather than careless.
Why this matters two sections later The comparative-advantage tables in 1.3 all produce straight PPCs, and students who have only ever been shown the bowed one think a straight frontier is a mistake. It is not: a table that says “60 hammocks OR 40 looms” and nothing else is a table with a constant trade rate, and a constant trade rate is a straight line. If you cannot say why the straight line is allowed, you will second-guess yourself on every trade question on the paper.
Worked example — getting a per-unit cost out of a table A country can produce at most 20 shirts or at most 5 chairs, with constant trade-offs.
Cost of one chair. All 5 chairs cost all 20 shirts, so one chair costs 20 ÷ 5 = 4 shirts.
Cost of one shirt. Turn it upside down: 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25 chairs.
The check. The two costs are reciprocals. If yours are not, you have divided the wrong way round in one of them.
The units are the giveaway: the cost of a chair is measured in shirts. A cost expressed in the same good you are buying is always wrong.

1.2a Moving the curve: growth, contraction, and what does not move it

Two sentences of the CED, and between them the single most examined confusion in the topic: the curve moving against the point moving.

Economic growth
The whole frontier moves OUTWARD. The economy can now do more than it could.In real lifeA country that finishes a decade-long programme of ports, power stations and schools can produce more of everything in the eleventh year than it could in the first.An increase in the economy’s productive capacity, drawn as an outward shift of the PPC. The CED states the direction outright: economic growth results in an outward shift of the PPC. What it does not mean is output going up - an economy moving from inside its curve onto it produces more and has not grown by a single unit of capacity. Ask what changed about what the economy could do, not about what it did.
On the exam

A country’s unemployment rate falls from 9 percent to 5 percent and its real output rises. On a production possibilities curve, this is best shown as

  1. Aan outward shift of the curve
  2. Ba movement from a point inside the curve toward the curve
  3. Ca movement along the curve from one end toward the other
  4. Dan inward shift of the curve
Answer

B. Output rose because idle resources went back to work, not because the economy acquired any new capacity. The frontier is exactly where it was, so nothing shifts - the point moves toward it. Calling this growth is the single most examined error in the topic.

Economic contraction
The whole frontier moves INWARD. The economy can now do less than it could.In real lifeA war or an earthquake that destroys ports, rail and factories. The skills and the people are still there; the capital they worked with is not.The direction classrooms skip. MOD-1.B.2 names growth or contraction in the same breath, so a page that only ever shifts the curve outward has taught half the statement. A contraction is a genuine loss of capacity - resources destroyed, a workforce shrinking, technology lost - and it is not the same as a recession, which is an economy that has stopped using capacity it still has. On the diagram the difference is total: contraction moves the curve, a recession moves the point.
What shifts the PPC
Two causes: more resources, or a better way of using them.In real lifeA country can build more next year because it has more workers, or because it has learned to build faster with the workers it already had. Different sentences, same direction on the graph.The PPC can shift because of changes in the factors of production as well as changes in productivity/technology. Those are two causes, not one. More land, labour, capital or entrepreneurship shifts the whole curve outward. Better technology gets more output from unchanged inputs - and if it only improves one industry, it pivots the curve instead of sliding it, leaving the other intercept exactly where it was. Which of the two the stem gave you decides whether you draw a parallel shift or a pivot.
Full employment of resources
Every resource the economy has is being used. That is what being on the curve means.In real lifeAn economy where anyone looking for work at the going wage can find it, and no factory is standing idle for want of orders.The state a point on the frontier represents. This unit’s Enduring Understanding says the PPC model is used to demonstrate the full employment level of output and to illustrate changes in it, which is the whole reason the macro course teaches a curve the micro course also teaches: for macro, the frontier is the economy’s capacity, and the two things worth knowing about capacity are whether output has reached it and whether it is growing. Unit 2 gives this a number and Unit 3 gives it a graph; here it is a point on a curve.
The test, and it never fails
The curve movedgrowth, or contraction
Only the point movedidle resources going to work, or being idled
Ask what is different about the economy’s CAPACITY — not about its output
Output rising is not growth. Output rising because the frontier moved is growth. An economy going from inside the curve onto it produces more and has not grown by one unit of capacity.
The curve moving against the point moving
What changedWhat the graph doesIs it economic growth?

Economic contractionCapacity lost: resources destroyed or a shrinking workforceThe whole curve shifts inwardNo — it is the opposite

The line that separates themAsk what changed about what the economy could do, not about what it did. Output rising is not growth; output rising because the frontier moved is. An economy climbing out of a recession produces more every quarter and has not added one unit of capacity, and a stem that gives you a falling unemployment rate is describing exactly that.

What actually shifts it

MOD-1.B.4 names two causes and they are two, not one: changes in the factors of production, and changes in productivity/technology. The first is more of the same inputs; the second is getting more out of the inputs you have.

The two causes of a shift, and the two shapes they draw
CauseWhat it meansWhat the curve doesStem language
What shifts the PPCMore of an input, or better technologyOutward for growth, inward for contractionThe umbrella statement — both causes below sit under it
Factors of productionMore land, labour, capital or entrepreneurshipBoth intercepts move: a parallel outward shiftAn oil discovery, immigration, a larger cohort finishing school, new factories

The line that separates themWhether the shift is parallel or a pivot, and the stem decides it. A resource used right across the economy moves both intercepts. A technology specific to one good moves that good’s intercept and leaves the other exactly where it was — so the two curves touch on one axis and separate as you move away from it. Drawing a parallel shift for a one-industry improvement loses the mark for the intercept that should not have moved.
Trap — “the economy discovered a new oil field” against “a new refining process” Both shift the curve outward, and if the question only asks which way, both answers are the same. They stop being the same the moment the stem asks about one good: more of a resource used across the whole economy moves both intercepts, while a technology specific to one industry pivots the curve and leaves the other intercept exactly where it was. Read which of the two the stem gave you before you draw the second curve.
Something older papers test that this unit no longer lists Released macro items from the 2000s and 2010s ask about command, market and mixed economies and about who answers “what to produce”. Those statements sit in AP Microeconomics Unit 1 in the current CED and are not Essential Knowledge for AP Macroeconomics Unit 1. They are worth being able to answer if you meet them in an old paper; they are not worth revision time here.

1.3 Comparative advantage and gains from trade

Two definitions that open with word-for-word the same clause, and everything in the topic turns on the ends of the sentences.

Absolute advantage
Producing MORE of a good than anyone else from the same resources.In real lifeOne farm harvests 900 tonnes from a hundred hectares where its neighbour gets 600 from the same hundred. It has the absolute advantage, and that is a statement about output.A situation in which an individual, a business or a country can produce more of a good or service than any other producer with the same quantity of resources. That last clause is the whole definition, and dropping it turns absolute advantage into a claim about size: without it, any large country “produces more” than any small one and the term means nothing. It is also the advantage that decides nothing - a producer can hold it in every good and still gain from trade.
Comparative advantage
Producing a good by giving up LESS of the other good than anyone else.In real lifeA surgeon who types faster than her receptionist still lets the receptionist type, because an hour of her typing costs an operation and an hour of his costs almost nothing.Being able to make something while giving up less of everything else than a rival would - a lower opportunity cost. It compares costs, where absolute advantage compares outputs, and it is the one that decides who should specialise in what. Two consequences the exam leans on. A producer better at everything still has a comparative advantage in only one of two goods, because giving up less of A means giving up more of B. And the two comparative advantages always split between the two producers - if your working puts both on one country, you have inverted a ratio somewhere.
On the exam
TractorsWheat (tons)
Norland4080
Sudmark3090

The table shows the maximum quantity of tractors OR of wheat each country can produce with the same resources, at constant opportunity cost. Which of the following is true?

  1. ANorland has the comparative advantage in both goods
  2. BSudmark has the comparative advantage in tractors
  3. CNorland has the comparative advantage in tractors and Sudmark in wheat
  4. DNeither country can gain from trade, because neither has an absolute advantage in both goods
Answer

C. A tractor costs Norland 80 ÷ 40 = 2 tons of wheat and costs Sudmark 90 ÷ 30 = 3 tons, so Norland gives up less and holds the comparative advantage in tractors. Turning it over, a ton of wheat costs Norland 0.5 tractors and Sudmark 0.33, so Sudmark holds it in wheat. The two comparative advantages always land on different producers, one each.

Specialization
Each producer concentrating on the good it gives up least to make.In real lifeA country that grows coffee and buys its steel is not bad at metallurgy. It is putting its land and labour where they cost the least to use.Reallocating a producer’s resources toward the good in which it holds the comparative advantage. The point is not that the specialist is best at the job in some absolute sense; it is that the world gives up least when that producer does it. Complete specialization - everything on one good - is what the straight-line PPCs in this topic assume, which is why the trade diagrams start at an axis intercept.
The two definitions, side by side Absolute advantage is about output. Comparative advantage is about cost. Absolute advantage describes producing more of a good than any other producer with the same quantity of resources. Comparative advantage describes producing it at a lower opportunity cost than another producer. One producer can hold absolute advantage in everything and comparative advantage in only one thing — which is why absolute advantage decides nothing about who should specialise in what.
Absolute against comparative advantage
What it comparesHow you calculate itCan one producer hold it in both goods?
Absolute advantageOutput, from the same quantity of resourcesRead the bigger number straight off the tableYes — and it decides nothing when it happens
Comparative advantageOpportunity cost, in units of the other goodDivide the other good’s number by this good’sNo — never. The two always split one each
The line that separates themOutput against cost. Absolute advantage asks who makes more; comparative advantage asks who gives up less, and only the second decides who should specialise. The reason one producer cannot hold comparative advantage in both is arithmetic rather than economics: giving up less of good A per unit of good B means, by the same two numbers, giving up more of B per unit of A.

Getting them out of a table

The exam almost never draws this. It gives you a table, and there are two kinds of table, printed to look alike.

The two kinds of table, which look identical
Table typeWhat the number isBigger number meansCost of one unit of this good

The line that separates themRead the column heading before dividing anything. “Units produced with equal resources” is an output table and more is better; “labour hours per unit” or “workers needed to make one” is an input table and more is worse. The ratio flips between them, so treating one as the other gives a clean, confident and completely inverted answer to every part of the question.
Worked example — an output table With the same resources, Norland can make 24 computers or 12 cars. Sudmark can make 45 computers or 15 cars.
Absolute advantage. Sudmark makes more of both. It has the absolute advantage in both goods, and we now stop caring.
Norland’s cost of one car: 24 ÷ 12 = 2 computers.
Sudmark’s cost of one car: 45 ÷ 15 = 3 computers.
Norland gives up less to make a car, so Norland has the comparative advantage in cars. Turning it over: a computer costs Norland 0.5 cars and Sudmark 0.33 cars, so Sudmark has the comparative advantage in computers.
The check that catches most errors. The two comparative advantages must land on different countries, one each. If you get both on one country you have divided the wrong way round somewhere.

Why anybody bothers

Gains from trade
After specializing and trading, both sides consume a combination neither could produce.In real lifeTwo neighbouring islands, one good at fish and one at rice, end the day each eating both - in quantities neither could have caught and grown by itself.Production specialization according to comparative advantage creates exchange opportunities that lead to consumption opportunities beyond the PPC. That phrase is the payoff of the whole topic and the one that makes trade look like something other than a rearrangement: production stays on the curve, and consumption moves outside it. A stem that says trade “makes both better off” has asserted the conclusion; the answer the exam wants shows the mechanism - each side produces what it is relatively good at, exchanges some of it, and ends up at a point its own frontier does not reach.
Terms of trade
The rate one good actually swaps for the other. It has to sit between the two costs.also called the exchange ratio, the real exchange ratio or the trading ratioMacro stems pair the names in one breath — “the real exchange ratio, also referred to as the terms of trade”. Both mean the rate at which one good swaps for the other.In real lifeAgreeing to hand over two and a half sacks of grain for one machine part. That ratio, not the money price, is the terms of trade.Comparative advantage and opportunity costs determine the terms of trade for exchange under which mutually beneficial trade can occur. A workable rate lies strictly between the two producers’ opportunity costs of the good being sold. Below the seller’s own cost the seller is worse off than making it; above the buyer’s own cost the buyer is worse off than making it. Exactly at either boundary one side gains nothing, which is why the exam’s wording is “mutually beneficial” rather than “acceptable”. Checking one side only is the standard way to lose this mark.
On the exam

A tractor costs Norland 2 tons of wheat to produce and costs Sudmark 3 tons. Which of the following exchange rates would make trade mutually beneficial?

  1. A1.5 tons of wheat per tractor
  2. B2 tons of wheat per tractor
  3. C2.5 tons of wheat per tractor
  4. D3.5 tons of wheat per tractor
Answer

C. Norland sells tractors and needs more than the 2 tons one costs it, or it would keep the tractor. Sudmark buys tractors and will pay less than the 3 tons one would cost it to build, or it would build its own. Only a rate strictly between 2 and 3 satisfies both, and 2.5 is the only option inside that band.

An input-per-unit table
A table of hours or workers PER UNIT. More is worse, so the ratios invert.In real lifeA recipe that says one loaf takes 40 minutes here and 25 minutes there. The bigger number is the slower kitchen, not the better one.The exam prints two kinds of table and they look alike. An output table gives what each producer can make with fixed resources, so a bigger number is better and the cost of one good is the other row’s number divided by this one’s. An input table gives labour hours or workers needed per unit, so a bigger number is worse and the ratio flips: the cost of one car is the hours a car takes divided by the hours a plane takes. Read the column heading before you divide anything. Reading an input table as an output table gives you a clean, confident, wrong answer in every part of the question.

Worked example — is this rate acceptable to both? Keeping Norland and Sudmark: a car costs Norland 2 computers and Sudmark 3 computers.
Norland is selling cars, so it needs more than 2 computers per car or it would rather keep the car.
Sudmark is buying cars, so it will pay less than 3 computers per car or it would rather make its own.
Any rate strictly between 2 and 3 computers per car works. 2.5 works. 2 does not — Norland gains nothing. 3 does not — Sudmark gains nothing. 3.5 does not — Sudmark is worse off than making its own.
A stem that gives you one rate and asks whether trade is mutually beneficial is asking you to check it against both opportunity costs. One of the two is not enough.

1.4 Demand

Two phrases that sound identical and are not: demand, and quantity demanded. Nearly every mark lost in this topic is lost between them, and the CED’s own note for this unit says so.

Demand
The whole schedule: how much buyers would take at every price.In real lifeA grain merchant who knows mills would take 40,000 bushels at $6, 55,000 at $5 and 70,000 at $4 knows the demand. Not one number - the list.The entire relationship between price and the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy, over every price, holding everything else constant. Demand is a curve, never a number. That is why “demand rose” and “quantity demanded rose” are different claims: the first says the whole schedule moved, the second says buyers slid to a different point on the schedule they already had.
Quantity demanded
One number: what buyers take at one particular price.In real lifeAt $5 the mills take 55,000 bushels. 55,000 is the quantity demanded at $5, and it says nothing about what happens at $4.The amount buyers are willing and able to buy at a specific price. It is a single point on the demand curve, so it can only change if the price changes - and when it does, the point moves along a curve that has not itself moved. Every answer that begins “quantity demanded” is describing a movement along the curve.
The law of demand
Price up, quantity demanded down. Always the opposite way.In real lifeA city raised its bus fare by three quarters and daily rides fell by about a tenth. Nobody had to be persuaded; the price did it.There is an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, which is what gives the demand curve its downward slope. The law is about the good’s own price and nothing else, so a change in any other price is a different event entirely - and the exam builds items out of exactly that substitution. Note that the macro course states the law and its slope and stops there; the income and substitution effects behind it belong to AP Micro.
On the exam

Which of the following describes a movement along the demand curve for coffee rather than a shift of it?

  1. AA report links coffee to a health benefit and buyers want more of it
  2. BThe price of coffee falls and buyers take more of it
  3. CHousehold incomes rise and buyers take more coffee at every price
  4. DThe price of tea rises and coffee buyers take more coffee
Answer

B. Only a change in the good’s OWN price moves you along its own curve. Options A and C are determinants of demand, and D is the price of a different good - all three shift the coffee curve rather than sliding a point along it.

The distinction the rest of the unit rests on

A change in the good’s own price can never shift its own curve. There is no third case and no exception, which is what makes this the most reliable single test in the unit.

The exam does not always use those words, and the substitutes are exactly where the marks go.

A movement along the curvealso called a change in quantity demanded, a change in quantity supplied or sliding along the curveOnly a change in the good’s own price does this. Nothing else can.

A shift of the curvealso called a change in demand, a change in supply or an increase or decrease in demandThe wording is the trap. “A change in DEMAND” means the curve moved; “a change in QUANTITY demanded” means it did not. One word apart, opposite answers.

The line that separates themAsk one question: did the good’s own price cause this? If yes it is a movement, always. If no it is a shift, always. There is no third case and no exception, which is why this is the most reliable single test in the unit — and the CED’s own note for Unit 1 names this as the thing students find hardest.

What moves the whole curve

The CED names one determinant by name and leaves the rest open: factors that influence consumer demand, such as changes in consumer income, cause the market demand curve to shift. Income is the one it reaches for, so income is the one to know cold.

Determinants of demand
The things that move the whole curve, none of which is the good’s own price.In real lifeA newspaper report linking a breakfast cereal to heart health moves every supermarket’s cereal sales, at every price, at once. No shelf price changed first.Factors that influence consumer demand cause the market demand curve to shift. The CED names one of them explicitly - changes in consumer income - and leaves the list open with “such as”. Income is therefore the one to know cold; the others the exam uses are tastes, the prices of related goods, expectations about future prices or income, and the number of buyers. What unites them is what is absent: the good’s own price is not on the list and cannot be, because a change in own price moves you along the curve instead of moving it.
Normal good
You buy more of it when your income rises.In real lifeA pay rise, and the weekly takeaway becomes a restaurant meal. Restaurant meals are a normal good.A good whose demand increases when consumer income rises and decreases when income falls. Most goods are normal, which is why the case is easy to forget until a stem turns it around. The direction is about demand - the whole curve - so a normal good’s curve shifts right on a pay rise at every price, not only at the price it happened to be selling for.
Inferior good
You buy LESS of it when your income rises.In real lifeInstant noodles and long-distance coach tickets. Students buy them; the same students, five years into a career, buy fewer - not because the price rose, but because they can now afford otherwise.A good whose demand decreases when income rises, and increases when income falls. “Inferior” is a statement about how buyers respond to income, not a judgement about quality. This is the most reliable trick in the topic: a macro stem describing a recession and asking about demand for a budget brand is testing whether you will say demand rises, which it does.
Substitutes
Two goods that do the same job, so buyers swap one for the other.In real lifeButter and margarine. When butter jumps in price, margarine sales rise - and margarine’s own price never moved.Two goods where a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other. The sentence moves between the two vocabularies on purpose: the price of butter changes quantity demanded of butter, a movement along butter’s curve, and demand for margarine, a shift of margarine’s curve. Which market the question puts you in decides which of the two happened.
Complements
Two goods used together, so they rise and fall together.In real lifeCars and petrol. A jump in fuel prices sells fewer large cars, at every car price on the forecourt.Two goods where a rise in the price of one decreases demand for the other, because they are consumed as a pair. The sign is what separates them from substitutes: for substitutes the other good’s price pushes this good’s demand the same way it moved, for complements the opposite way. Saying the pair out loud with “instead of” and “along with” settles which is which faster than any mnemonic.

The line that separates themThe sign on income, and nothing else. “Inferior” is not a judgement about quality — it says only that people buy less of the thing as they get richer. A recession stem asking about a budget brand is testing whether you will say demand rises, which it does.
Substitutes against complements

SubstitutesDemand for this one shifts rightThey replace each otherButter and margarine; bus and train

The line that separates themWhich way the two move together. Substitutes move in opposite directions: one gets dearer, you buy more of the other. Complements move in the same direction: one gets dearer, you buy less of both. Say the pair out loud with “instead of” and “along with” — whichever fits names the relationship.
The stem that catches everyone “The price of coffee rises. What happens in the market for coffee?” — quantity demanded falls, a movement.
“The price of coffee rises. What happens in the market for tea?” — demand for tea rises, a shift.
Same sentence, same price change, and the answers are different kinds of thing. The word that decides it is the one naming which market you are standing in: a price is the own price of exactly one good.

1.5 Supply

Everything in 1.4 applies again, with one sign reversed. That is the whole topic, and it is worth being exact about which one.

Supply
The whole schedule: how much sellers would offer at every price.In real lifeA grower who would cut 200 stems at $1, 320 at $1.50 and 500 at $2 has described the supply of her flowers. Again the list, not one number.The entire relationship between price and the quantity sellers are willing and able to produce, over every price, holding everything else constant. Like demand it is a curve rather than a number, and like demand the market curve is the sum of the individual ones: at each price, add up what every seller would offer.
Quantity supplied
One number: what sellers offer at one particular price.In real lifeAt $1.50 the grower cuts 320 stems. 320 is the quantity supplied at $1.50 and nothing else.The amount sellers are willing and able to sell at a specific price - a single point on the supply curve. It changes only when the good’s own price changes, and when it does the point moves along a curve that has not moved. The vocabulary is deliberately parallel to the demand side, because the exam tests the same distinction twice in one paper.
The law of supply
Price up, quantity supplied up. The same way, not the opposite way.In real lifeWhen wheat prices doubled, land that had been left to pasture was ploughed and planted. The land was always there; at the old price it was not worth using.There is a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied, which is what gives the supply curve its upward slope. This is the one place where the supply side is not a mirror of the demand side, and the sign is the thing to memorise: demand moves opposite, supply moves the same way. Everything else about the two - point against curve, own price against determinants - is word for word identical.
On the exam

Which of the following best illustrates the law of supply?

  1. AA rise in the wage rate leads bakeries to bake fewer loaves
  2. BA rise in the price of bread leads bakeries to bake more loaves
  3. CA rise in household income leads buyers to buy more bread
  4. DA new oven halves baking time and bakeries bake more loaves
Answer

B. The law of supply is about the good’s own price and quantity supplied moving the same way. A is an input price and D is technology - both shift the curve. C is about buyers, not sellers, and belongs to demand.

The one difference Demand moves opposite. Supply moves the same way. Everything else is word for word identical: own price moves you along the curve, a determinant moves the curve, quantity supplied is a point and supply is the whole schedule. Only the direction differs, because a higher price is a worse deal for whoever is paying it and a better one for whoever is collecting it.
The two laws, against each other

The law of demandQuantity demanded fallsQuantity demanded risesDownward — an inverse relationship
The law of supplyQuantity supplied risesQuantity supplied fallsUpward — a positive relationship
The line that separates themOnly the sign. A higher price is a worse deal for whoever is paying it and a better one for whoever is collecting it, so buyers retreat and sellers advance. Everything else about the two — point against curve, own price against determinants — is word for word the same, which is why the exam can test the same distinction twice on one paper.

What moves the supply curve

MKT-2.D.1 is the mirror of the demand statement and names one determinant: factors that influence producer supply, such as changes in input prices, cause the market supply curve to shift.

Determinants of supply
The things that move the whole supply curve, none of which is own price.In real lifeA drought does not wait for the price of wheat to change. It cuts what every farm can offer, at every price, at once.Factors that influence producer supply cause the market supply curve to shift. The CED names one - changes in input prices - and leaves the rest open. The others the exam uses are technology, the prices of other goods the seller could produce instead, expectations, the number of sellers, and taxes or subsidies on production. As on the demand side, the good’s own price is not among them and cannot be.
Input prices
What it costs the seller to make the thing. Costs up, supply left.In real lifeAluminium gets dearer and every drinks canner’s costs rise with it - so at any given can price, fewer cans get made.The prices of the resources used in production - wages, energy, materials, components. They move supply in the opposite direction to themselves: dearer inputs shift supply left, cheaper inputs shift it right. This is the determinant the exam uses most, usually disguised as a wage settlement, an energy price or a tariff on a component, and the direction is the half students reverse.
Technology
A better way of making it, so more gets made at every price.In real lifeDrip irrigation lets the same field of tomatoes be grown on a third of the water. The tomato price did not change; the farm can simply offer more of them.Any improvement in the method of production. It lowers the cost of each unit, so it shifts supply right - and it is the one determinant that essentially never shifts supply left, because a way of doing something is not usually forgotten. Note it appears twice in this unit, doing two jobs: here it moves the supply curve of one good, and in 1.2 it moves the whole economy’s production possibilities curve.
What moves the supply curve, and which way
DeterminantA rise in it shifts supplyWhyStem language

The line that separates themBoth act on the cost of making one more unit, and supply shifts away from cost. Dearer inputs push it left; better technology pushes it right, and essentially never left, because a way of doing something is not usually forgotten. The trap is that both are “a price went up” sentences and only one of them is the price of the good on the horizontal axis.
Trap — which price is in the stem An input price and supply move opposite ways: wages up means supply left. Students who have just learned that supply slopes upward shift it right on any price rise, because a price went up. The price that slopes the curve upward is the price of the output. The price in this determinant is the price of an input, and it is a cost.
Worked example — sorting four events in the wheat market Fertiliser gets dearer. Input price — supply shifts left.
The price of wheat rises. Own price — a movement along, up the curve. Quantity supplied rises; supply does not.
A drought destroys a quarter of the crop. A fall in a factor of production — supply shifts left.
A new seed variety raises yields per acre. Technology — supply shifts right.
Only the second is a movement, and it is the only one where the thing that changed was the price of wheat itself.

1.6 Market equilibrium and disequilibrium

Three things happen in this topic and the CED gives each its own learning objective: what equilibrium is, what a market does when it is not there, and what happens when a curve moves. The middle one is the one classrooms rush.

Market equilibrium
The one price where the quantity wanted and the quantity offered are the same number.In real lifeA fish market that closes with no unsold boxes and no buyer turned away found that price some time around dawn.Equilibrium is achieved at the price at which quantities demanded and supplied are equal. Not a fair price, not the price a seller wanted, not an average - the price at which the two sides’ plans are consistent, so that nobody is left holding stock and nobody is left unserved. On a graph it is the single crossing of the two curves; in a schedule it is the row where the two quantity columns match.

MKT-2.E.1, and the word it turns on Equilibrium is achieved at the price at which quantities demanded and supplied are equal. Equal quantities, not a fair price, not the price the seller wanted, not the average of anything. It is the price at which the two plans are consistent — and every buyer and seller in the market took it as given rather than choosing it.

When the price is somewhere else

A market away from that price is in disequilibriumalso called a market imbalance, a market that has not cleared or a non-clearing priceThe CED’s own sentence uses “imbalances” and “disequilibrium prices” in the same line, so both turn up in stems., and it does not stay there. The CED states the mechanism in one line: whenever markets experience imbalances, creating disequilibrium prices, surpluses, and shortages, market forces drive prices toward equilibrium.

Disequilibrium
The price is somewhere other than the crossing, so the two quantities do not match.also called a market imbalance, a market that has not cleared or a non-clearing priceThe CED’s own sentence uses “imbalances” and “disequilibrium prices” in the same line, so both turn up in stems.In real lifeA concert that sells out in ninety seconds was priced below its clearing price, and the resale market immediately says by how much.Any state in which the market price is not the equilibrium price. Whenever markets experience imbalances - creating disequilibrium prices, surpluses, and shortages - market forces drive prices toward equilibrium. Two things in that sentence are examinable. It is prices that are driven, not curves: nothing shifts while a market clears. And the movement is always toward the crossing, so a price above equilibrium falls and a price below it rises, whatever the stem’s scenario.
Surplus
Price too high: sellers offer more than buyers want, and the price gets pushed down.In real lifeA greengrocer whose stall still has half its stock at closing time drops the price tomorrow. Every greengrocer doing the same is a market price falling.The excess of quantity supplied over quantity demanded at a price above equilibrium. It is measured horizontally, as quantity supplied minus quantity demanded at that one price, so a surplus is always a number of units and never a number of dollars. The unsold stock is the pressure: sellers cut the price, which draws buyers in and pushes marginal sellers out, and the gap closes from both sides at once.
Shortage
Price too low: buyers want more than sellers offer, and the price gets pushed up.In real lifeA queue around the block for a console that sells out on the morning it lands, and appears on resale sites that afternoon at twice the price.The excess of quantity demanded over quantity supplied at a price below equilibrium. Like a surplus it is a horizontal distance and a quantity of units: a shortage of 300 shirts, never of $300. The pressure runs the other way - buyers bid against each other, sellers discover they can ask more, and as the price rises quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises until the two meet. Note it is not the same as scarcity: scarcity is permanent and applies to a resource, a shortage is temporary and applies to a market at one wrong price.
On the exam

In a competitive market for shirts, 1,200 shirts are offered for sale at the current price but buyers want to purchase 1,500. Which of the following is true?

  1. AThere is a surplus of 300 shirts and the price will fall
  2. BThere is a shortage of 300 shirts and the price will rise
  3. CThe market is in equilibrium, because both quantities are positive
  4. DThe supply curve will shift right until the two quantities are equal
Answer

B. Buyers want 300 more than sellers offer, so the current price is below equilibrium and the imbalance is a shortage of 300 shirts. The price is bid up, which raises quantity supplied and lowers quantity demanded along curves that do not move - option D describes a shift, and nothing here shifts anything.

The line that separates themWhere the price sits relative to the crossing, and that alone. The size of either one is a horizontal distance — the two quantities at one price, subtracted — so the answer is a number of units and never a number of dollars. And in both cases the price moves toward the crossing while both curves stand perfectly still: if your answer shifts a curve, you have answered a different question.
How to answer any disequilibrium question in three steps
  1. Find P*, the crossing. Then ask whether the price in the stem is above it or below it.
  2. Above the crossing gives a surplus; below gives a shortage. Read the two quantities off the price line and subtract the smaller from the larger — that number is the size of the imbalance.
  3. The price moves toward the crossing, always. Above the crossing it falls; below it, it rises.
Nothing shifts in any of those steps. If your answer moves a curve, you have answered a different question.

1.6a Changes in equilibrium: the eight cases

MKT-2.G.1 is one sentence — changes in the determinants of supply and/or demand result in a new equilibrium price and quantity — and it generates eight cases. Four are easy, and four are the reason this topic is worth a section of its own.

A change in equilibrium
A determinant moved a curve, so the crossing moved. New price, new quantity.In real lifeA flood destroys part of a wheat crop. Nobody wanted less bread; there is simply less wheat, and both the price and the amount traded end up somewhere new.Changes in the determinants of supply and/or demand result in a new equilibrium price and quantity. The method never varies: move the curve the stem tells you to move, leave the other one alone, and read the new crossing. When only one curve moves, the new equilibrium slides along the curve that did not - and that single sentence generates all four single-shift answers without memorising any of them.
An indeterminate outcome
Two curves moved, and one of the two answers genuinely cannot be signed.In real lifeIncomes rise in the same season as a drought. Bread will certainly be dearer. Whether more or less of it is sold depends on which force was stronger, and nobody has told you.When both curves move at once, one variable is pushed the same way by both shifts and the other is pulled opposite ways. The first is determinate; the second is indeterminate, and “cannot be determined” is a real and frequently correct AP answer rather than an evasion. The trap is the drawing: textbooks shift both curves by equal amounts, which makes the indeterminate variable land exactly where it started, and reading “unchanged” off that picture is the standard wrong answer.

Where this unit goes, and where Micro is not the same course

Two things worth saying before you leave Unit 1: what the PPC becomes later, and where the Micro course’s version of this unit is quietly different.

What the PPC turns into

The Enduring Understanding this unit opens with is not the one a Micro student would expect. The macro CED says the PPC model is used to demonstrate the full employment level of output and to illustrate changes in full employment. That is a macro job description: the curve is standing in for the economy’s capacity, and the two states you will meet again are the two this unit already drew.

The circular flow model
A map of the economy as two loops running opposite ways between households and firms.In real lifeYour wage goes out of a firm and comes back to it when you spend it. The same money, one lap.Households own the factors of production and sell them to firms; firms produce goods and sell them back to households. Real things go one way round and money goes the other. Around the outside sit three pairs where money leaves the flow and returns to it - savings and investment, taxes and government purchases, imports and exports - and every one of those pairs is a market with its own graph later in the course. The CED lists this under Analyze Provided Graphs and Visuals, so you read one and are never asked to draw one on the free-response section.
What the PPC becomes later
Inside the curve is a recession; the curve moving out is long-run growth.In real lifeEvery news report about an economy “running below capacity” is describing a point inside a curve somebody drew in a first-week lesson.The macro course keeps the PPC and puts it to work. A point inside the frontier - underutilized resources - is the state Unit 3 draws as a recessionary gap on an aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram. An outward shift of the frontier is the state Unit 5 calls long-run economic growth, and Unit 5 says in as many words that it is analogous to a rightward shift of the long-run aggregate supply curve. Both of those sentences belong to those units and neither is examined here. Knowing where this unit’s two pictures are going is worth more than learning the later vocabulary early.
The two carries, stated once
Inside the curvebecomes a recessionary gap in Unit 3
The curve moving outbecomes long-run growth in Unit 5
Same two pictures, drawn on different axes
Unit 5 states the second one outright, and it is worth knowing that it does — but the sentence that makes the connection belongs to Unit 5, not here. In this unit the PPC is still a PPC.

Where AP Micro says something different

Almost everything in this unit also appears in the Micro course, which makes the handful of genuine differences dangerous: a student who has taken both reads the familiar opening of a sentence and supplies the wrong ending.

The line that separates themThe dangerous differences are the two at the top, and both are about the PPC. In Micro the curve is a producer’s allocation problem; in Macro it is the whole economy’s capacity, and it is later reused as such. Everything else on this page is the same economics stated in slightly different sentences — which is exactly why a student who has taken both courses reads a familiar opening and supplies the other course’s ending.
The one that costs marks The Micro CED writes that specialization according to comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, leads to consumption beyond the PPC. The Macro CED writes the same sentence without that clause. The economics is identical — absolute advantage still decides nothing — but the Macro paper will not print the reminder for you. If you learned the rule as “the sentence that says not absolute advantage”, you have learned a piece of typography rather than a piece of economics.
