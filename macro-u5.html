5.1 Fiscal and monetary policy actions in the short run

Units 3 and 4 taught one lever each. This topic is the only place in the course where both are pulled at once, and the reason it is asked about so often is that the four outcomes do not all follow from the same rule.

POL-1.F.1, both directions in one sentence A combination of expansionary or contractionary fiscal and monetary policies may be used to restore full employment when the economy is in a negative (recessionary) or positive (inflationary) output gap. Two gaps, two directions, one sentence. A page that draws only the recession case has covered half the statement while looking complete - which is why the figure column carries the contractionary case beside the expansionary one rather than mentioning it in a clause.
A policy mix
Fiscal and monetary policy fired together, in either direction, to close an output gap from the demand side.In real lifeA stimulus bill passed in the same quarter the central bank cuts its policy rate. Neither institution acted alone, and neither had to move as far as it would have had to on its own.A combination of expansionary or contractionary fiscal and monetary policies used to restore full employment when the economy is in a negative (recessionary) or positive (inflationary) output gap. Both directions are named in the same statement, and the pairing is the point: this is the only place in the course where the two levers are pulled at once. Two policies aimed at the same gap means each can be smaller than it would have to be alone - and it also means the two levers may disagree about the interest rate even while they agree about output.
On the exam

An economy is operating well below full employment. Which combination of fiscal and monetary policy actions is most appropriate?

  1. AAn increase in government spending and an open-market sale of bonds
  2. BAn increase in taxes and a reduction in the reserve requirement
  3. CAn increase in government spending and an open-market purchase of bonds
  4. DA reduction in government spending and an increase in the policy rate
Answer

C. Below full employment the gap is negative, so both levers should be expansionary: higher government spending shifts aggregate demand right, and an open-market purchase adds reserves and lowers the policy rate, which raises investment and shifts aggregate demand right again. The other three each pull one lever the wrong way, and the last pulls both.

Both levers expansionary
Aggregate demand shifts right twice: real output up, price level up, interest rate not determined.In real lifeA government cutting income tax in the same year its central bank buys bonds in the open market. Households have more to spend and borrowing is cheaper at the same time.The recessionary case of the combined-policy statement. Both levers push aggregate demand to the right, so real output rises and the price level rises with it - they move the same way whenever the shock is on the demand side. The interest rate has no determinate direction: financing the fiscal expansion means more government borrowing, which raises the real rate, while the monetary expansion adds reserves, which lowers it. Which effect is larger is not something the model settles, and “it cannot be determined” is a real answer on this exam.
Both levers contractionary
Aggregate demand shifts left twice: real output down, price level down, interest rate again not determined.In real lifeA budget that raises taxes in the same year the central bank sells bonds - both institutions leaning against an economy running above capacity.The inflationary case, and the half a page can silently omit while looking finished. Both levers pull aggregate demand left, so real output falls back toward full employment and the price level falls with it. The interest rate is indeterminate for the mirror-image reason: less government borrowing pulls the real rate down while fewer reserves push it up. Any statement of the combined-policy rule that shows only the expansionary case has covered one of the two gaps the CED names.

On the exam

Which of the following outcomes of a combined fiscal and monetary policy action cannot be determined from the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model alone?

  1. AThe direction of the change in real output
  2. BThe direction of the change in the price level
  3. CThe direction of the change in the real interest rate
  4. DThe direction of the shift in aggregate demand
Answer

C. The aggregate demand and aggregate supply model has real GDP on one axis and the price level on the other, so it delivers output, the price level and the shift in aggregate demand directly. It carries no interest rate at all - that is read off the loanable funds market or the money market, which is why a question about interest rates needs a second diagram.

An indeterminate interest rate
When the two levers push a variable in opposite directions, the model gives a direction only if you are told which effect is larger.In real lifeTwo people pushing a stalled car from opposite ends. You know it will not move much; you cannot say which way it goes without knowing which of them is stronger.Not a gap in the theory but a result of it. Fiscal expansion raises the real interest rate through government borrowing; monetary expansion lowers it through added reserves. Fire both and the rate has two opposing pressures on it, and the model as this course states it does not rank them. The useful consequence is the reverse case: when the two levers oppose on aggregate demand - contractionary fiscal with expansionary monetary, or the other way round - output and the price level become the indeterminate pair and the interest rate becomes the certain one. Exactly one of the two questions is answerable in each combination.

The line that separates themThey agree about aggregate demand and disagree about the interest rate, and that single fact generates every result in this topic. Trap — reading an interest rate off AD-AS A stem asking which combination produces “expansion with the smallest change in interest rates” cannot be answered from AD-AS, because the diagram has real GDP on one axis and the price level on the other and no rate anywhere. The interest rate is read off the loanable funds market or the money market, and the reason a mixed policy can hold it still is that the two levers push it in opposite directions.
Worked example — picking the combination from what is held fixed A country is at full employment. Policymakers want to hold the price level and encourage investment.
Step 1 - what must not move. The price level is read off AD-AS, so the two AD shifts have to cancel: one lever expansionary, one contractionary.
Step 2 - what must move, and which way. Investment rises when the real interest rate falls, so both levers must lower it.
Step 3 - the only combination that does both. Contractionary fiscal (the government borrows less, so demand for loanable funds falls) plus expansionary monetary (more reserves, so supply rises). Both push the rate down; the AD shifts oppose.
The check. Output and the price level are roughly unchanged, but the composition of output has shifted from government purchases to private investment - which is why this is a long-run growth policy dressed as a short-run one.

5.2 The Phillips curve: two curves, two horizons

Two curves, drawn on one pair of axes, that appear to contradict each other. They do not - and the order they are introduced in decides whether a reader ends up believing that.

Short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)
The downward-sloping curve: at a moment in time, lower unemployment comes with higher inflation.In real lifeA quarterly report showing unemployment down a point and inflation up a point over the same three months is one step along this curve.The short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment, illustrated by the downward-sloping short-run Phillips curve. It is the Phillips-curve statement of exactly what the upward-sloping short-run aggregate supply curve says: a higher price level comes with more output, more output comes with more hiring, and more hiring - with the labour force held constant - is a lower unemployment rate. The two diagrams carry point-for-point the same information, which is why an exam question can hand you one and ask about the other.
On the exam

According to the short-run Phillips curve, a decrease in the unemployment rate is associated with

  1. Aa lower inflation rate
  2. Ba higher inflation rate
  3. Can unchanged inflation rate
  4. Da lower natural rate of unemployment
Answer

B. The short-run Phillips curve slopes downward, so unemployment and inflation move in opposite directions along it: less unemployment comes with more inflation. An unchanged inflation rate describes the vertical long-run curve, and the natural rate is not something a movement along the short-run curve changes.

Always on some SRPC
The economy is always operating somewhere along a short-run Phillips curve. Being off one is not an available state.In real lifeAny month in which a statistics office publishes both an inflation rate and an unemployment rate has, by publishing them, put the economy at a point on some short-run curve.Eight words in the CED that do a lot of work: an economy is always operating somewhere along the short-run Phillips curve. The consequence is that every question in this topic is a question about WHERE on a curve the economy sits, or about WHICH curve it is on - never about whether it is on one. “The economy is off the curve” is not an answer, and a stem describing an inflation rate together with an unemployment rate has already fixed a point for you.
Long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)also called the vertical Phillips curve or the natural-rate lineAll three name the same vertical line standing at the natural rate of unemployment. “The natural-rate line” is the more descriptive name and the one that makes MOD-3.B.3 obvious: the only thing that can move this curve is a change in the natural rate itself.
A vertical line at the natural rate of unemployment: in the long run there is no trade-off, only whatever inflation is expected.In real lifeTwo decades in which one country averaged 2% inflation and another averaged 9%, and both ended with about the same unemployment rate. The higher inflation bought the second country nothing.The long-run relationship between inflation and unemployment, illustrated by the long-run Phillips curve, which is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment. Vertical means the horizontal reading does not depend on the vertical one: whatever inflation rate the economy settles at, unemployment comes back to the natural rate. It is the Phillips-curve twin of the vertical long-run aggregate supply line, and the two move together - anything that shifts one shifts the other.

A vertical curve at the natural rate means unemployment returns to that one rate whatever the inflation rate turns out to be, so every inflation rate is compatible with it and none of them buys lower unemployment permanently. 5.2 continued — shocks: what moves along, what shifts

Three things can happen to a point on this diagram, and the exam’s favourite question is which one it just described. The tell is whether inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions or in the same one.

A demand shock, on this diagram
Moves the economy ALONG one short-run curve. Inflation and unemployment move in opposite directions.In real lifeA collapse in household confidence that cuts spending pushes unemployment up and inflation down at the same time - one slide down one curve.Demand shocks correspond to movement along the short-run Phillips curve. Nothing shifts, because a demand shock does not change what it costs firms to produce - it changes how much is bought. The diagnostic is the direction: a demand shock moves inflation and unemployment in opposite directions, up-and-left for a positive shock and down-and-right for a negative one, and either move stays on the curve it started on. On the aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram the same event is aggregate demand shifting along a fixed short-run aggregate supply curve.
A supply shock, on this diagram
SHIFTS the short-run curve. Inflation and unemployment move in the SAME direction, which no movement along can do.In real lifeA sharp rise in the price of a fuel every industry uses raises costs everywhere at once: firms produce less and charge more, so unemployment and inflation both rise.Supply shocks correspond to shifts of the SRPC. A shock that changes production costs changes the inflation rate associated with every unemployment rate at once, so the whole curve moves - outward for an adverse shock, inward for a favourable one. The signature that identifies it is that inflation and unemployment move the same way, which is impossible along a downward-sloping curve. This is the Phillips-curve twin of stagflation on the aggregate demand and aggregate supply diagram, where short-run aggregate supply falls and output drops while the price level rises.
On the exam

A country reports that its inflation rate and its unemployment rate both increased over the same year. Which of the following best explains the change?

  1. AA positive demand shock, moving the economy up along its short-run Phillips curve
  2. BA negative demand shock, moving the economy down along its short-run Phillips curve
  3. CA negative supply shock, shifting the short-run Phillips curve outward
  4. DA decrease in the natural rate of unemployment, shifting the long-run Phillips curve left
Answer

C. A movement along a downward-sloping curve always moves the two rates in opposite directions, so no demand shock in either direction can raise both. Only a shift of the short-run curve can, and an adverse supply shock is what shifts it outward. A change in the natural rate moves the vertical long-run curve, which is a different event with a different signature.

Expected inflation
The inflation rate people build into wages and contracts. It sets which short-run curve the economy is on.In real lifeA union negotiating a three-year wage deal has to guess what prices will do over those three years, and whatever it guesses is written into the contract whether or not it turns out right.Each short-run Phillips curve is drawn for one expected inflation rate, and it crosses the long-run curve exactly at that rate. Raise what people expect and the whole short-run curve moves outward, because firms and workers now write higher price and wage increases into their decisions at every level of unemployment. The natural rate does not change, so the long-run curve does not move - which makes this the clearest case of one curve shifting past a stationary other one, and a case the exam sets far more often than the CED’s own wording suggests.
On the exam

An increase in the expected rate of inflation will most likely cause which of the following?

  1. AA movement up along the existing short-run Phillips curve
  2. BAn outward shift of the short-run Phillips curve, with the long-run curve unchanged
  3. CA leftward shift of the long-run Phillips curve
  4. DA movement down along the existing short-run Phillips curve
Answer

B. Higher expected inflation raises the inflation rate associated with every unemployment rate, so the short-run curve shifts outward. It is not a change in frictional or structural unemployment, so the natural rate is unchanged and the vertical long-run curve stays where it is. Movements along the curve are what demand shocks produce, not what a change in expectations produces.

A shift of the LRPC
Only a change in the natural rate of unemployment moves the vertical curve. Nothing else does.In real lifeA national online job-matching service that shortens the average gap between leaving one job and starting the next lowers frictional unemployment, and with it the natural rate.Factors that cause the natural rate of unemployment to change will cause the long-run Phillips curve to shift. Because the natural rate is frictional plus structural unemployment, the things that move it are things that change how well workers and jobs match: how job search is conducted, how quickly skills go out of date, how easily people move between regions and industries, and the composition of the labour force itself. Improved matching moves the curve left; worse matching moves it right. The same event moves long-run aggregate supply the other way - left here is right there - and a question that gives you one is testing whether you can produce the other.
On the exam

A country automates much of its factory work, and a large group of those workers find their skills no longer match the jobs on offer. What happens to that country’s long-run Phillips curve?

  1. AIt shifts right, because the natural rate of unemployment has risen
  2. BIt shifts left, because the economy can now produce more with fewer workers
  3. CIt does not move; only the short-run curve shifts
  4. DIt stops being vertical, because unemployment and inflation now trade off in the long run
Answer

A. The long-run curve stands at the natural rate of unemployment, so it moves only when the natural rate moves - and the natural rate is frictional plus structural unemployment. Workers whose skills no longer match the available jobs are structurally unemployed, so the natural rate rises and the vertical curve shifts right. Automation raising output does not move it: the curve is about the labour market, not about capacity. Expectations move the SHORT-run curve, and the long-run curve stays vertical however severe the mismatch gets.

The stagflation signature
Both rates rising together. It rules out every demand-side explanation in one step.In real lifeA year in which a country reports both its worst unemployment and its worst inflation in a decade is not describing a boom or a bust - it is describing a cost shock.Not a CED term but the diagnostic the CED’s two shock statements imply, and worth naming because it is how a stem is usually written. If inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions, you slid along one short-run curve and the cause was on the demand side. If they moved in the same direction, the curve itself moved and the cause was on the supply side. There is no third pattern, and the test takes one reading of the direction of each rate.
MOD-3.B.1 and MOD-3.B.2, a matched pair The pair, in the CED’s own words: demand shocks correspond to movement along the SRPC, and supply shocks correspond to shifts of the SRPC. Stated on its own, either half is a rule with nothing to apply it against. Together they are a test you can run on any stem: opposite directions means you slid along one curve, same direction means the curve moved.

5.3 Money growth and inflation

One identity, asked for by rearrangement in almost every year of the released papers - and then read a second way, in growth rates, where it becomes the whole of what this topic claims about inflation.

Where sustained inflation comes from
Money supply growing too fast for a sustained period of time. Not any increase - a rate, held above what output can absorb, for years.In real lifeA country whose central bank finances government spending by printing money year after year ends up with prices rising faster every year, which is a different phenomenon from a one-off price rise after a bad harvest.Inflation (deflation) results from increasing (decreasing) the money supply at too rapid of a rate for a sustained period of time. That last clause is the whole difference between this statement and a contradiction of Unit 4, where a single open-market purchase lowers a rate and raises real output. What produces inflation is not an increase in the money supply but a growth rate of the money supply held above what real output growth can absorb, over a run of years. Deflation is the same claim with both signs reversed.
The quantity theory of money
In the long run, the growth rate of the money supply sets the growth rate of the price level.In real lifeA central bank that announces it will expand the money supply at the same rate the economy grows is announcing a target of roughly zero inflation, and this is the theory it is relying on.In the long run, the growth rate of the money supply determines the growth rate of the price level - the inflation rate - according to the quantity theory of money. It is the equation of exchange read in rates of change rather than in levels: money growth plus velocity growth equals inflation plus real output growth. With velocity steady, whatever money growth exceeds real output growth by is what comes out as inflation, and money growing at exactly the rate output does leaves none.
On the exam

5.4 Government deficits and the national debt

Two words students use interchangeably and the CED never does. One is a single year; the other is every year that has already happened, added up - and the arithmetic that connects them is the whole of this topic.

The government budget balance
Tax revenues minus government purchases PLUS transfer payments, in a given year. Three quantities, not two.In real lifeA national budget statement that lists what the government bought and what it paid out in pensions and benefits on separate lines is reporting both halves of the outlay side.The government budget surplus (deficit) is the difference between tax revenues and government purchases plus transfer payments in a given year. Transfers buy the government nothing, so they are not counted in G and not counted in GDP - but they are money leaving the treasury, so they belong in the budget. A balance computed from purchases alone can come out positive when the true balance is negative, which is why the omission is not a small error.
On the exam

Answer

B. Total outlays are purchases plus transfer payments: $800 billion plus $250 billion is $1,050 billion. Revenues of $900 billion minus outlays of $1,050 billion is a deficit of $150 billion. A budget deficit

5.5 Crowding out

The CED’s third tension, and the one that costs the most marks: crowding out is defined as a short-run fall in one specific kind of spending, and its consequence is a long-run loss of growth. Collapse the two and the interest rate - the thing the whole effect runs through - disappears from the answer.

How a deficit is financed
By borrowing. A government in deficit typically issues bonds, which is what puts it into the loanable funds market.In real lifeA treasury auctioning a new tranche of government bonds is raising the money for this year’s shortfall from the same pool of savings every firm borrows from.When a government is in budget deficit, it typically borrows to finance its spending. This one-line statement is the hinge of the whole topic: it is what turns a budget fact into a market event. The government does not conjure the money - it competes for it, in the market where households’ saving is lent to whoever will pay for it, and that competition is what everything else in this topic follows from.
The loanable funds market as the mechanism
Government borrowing shifts demand for funds right, the equilibrium real interest rate rises, and private investment at that rate falls.In real lifeA firm that would have built a new plant at a 4% borrowing cost shelves it at 6%. The plant is the investment that was crowded out, and the two percentage points are why.A loanable funds market model can be used to show the effect of government borrowing on the equilibrium real interest rate and the resulting crowding out of private investment. Naming the model is the point: crowding out is not a claim to be asserted, it is two readings off one diagram. Demand for funds shifts right; the real rate rises; the quantity of private investment demanded at the new rate is smaller than at the old one. An answer that reaches the conclusion without the rate has skipped the only part that can be drawn.
On the exam

5.6 Economic growth

The one topic in the unit that is not about a policy at all. It is about the ceiling every policy in the other six topics is pushing against - how the ceiling is measured, what raises it, and why one of the three things that raises it behaves differently from the other two.

Productivity is the thing growth policy is aimed at, because it is the only one of the two routes to a higher standard of living that does not run out: a country can raise output per person by putting more people to work, but only until everybody is working.
What determines productivity
Three things: the level of technology, and physical and human capital per worker.In real lifeThe same worker digging with a shovel, then a mechanical digger, then a mechanical digger they have been trained to operate well. Three different outputs, three different determinants.Productivity is determined by the level of technology and physical and human capital per worker. Three determinants, and technology is the odd one out: it is not a per-worker quantity, so it is not subject to the same diminishing returns as the other two. Add machines to a fixed workforce and each machine adds less than the last; improve the method and every worker with every machine produces more. That difference is the whole reason topic 5.7 spends as much of its wording on technology and research as it does on capital.
Physical capital
The produced means of production: machines, buildings, vehicles, roads, ports.In real lifeA bakery’s ovens and delivery vans. Somebody produced them, and they exist to help produce something else.One of the three determinants of productivity, and the one crowding out acts on. Physical capital per worker is what rises when a country invests, and investment is the component of aggregate demand that a higher real interest rate suppresses - which is the link between topic 5.5 and this one. Accumulating it raises output per worker, but by less each time, so a country that grows only this way sees its growth rate fall even as its capital stock keeps rising.
Human capital
The skills, education, training and health embodied in workers.In real lifeAn apprenticeship that leaves a mechanic able to diagnose in an hour what used to take a day. Nothing physical was added to the workshop.The second per-worker determinant, and the one that is easiest to confuse with simply having more workers. A larger labour force raises total output and does nothing on its own to output per worker; better-trained workers raise output per worker directly. Like physical capital it runs into diminishing returns eventually, and like physical capital it is something public policy can invest in - which is exactly what topic 5.7 is about.

The level of technology
The methods available for turning inputs into output. The determinant that is not a per-worker quantity.In real lifeA new production method that lets the same workers with the same machines produce more. Nothing was bought and nobody was hired.The third determinant, and the one that behaves differently from the other two. Physical and human capital are measured per worker, so adding more of either to a given workforce eventually buys less and less. Technology is a level, not a ratio: an improvement raises output at every level of capital per worker at once, which is a shift of the whole relationship rather than a step along it. That is why it is described as the growth that does not run out, and why research and development turns up in the growth policy list.
The per capita production function
Output per capita rises with both physical and human capital per capita.In real lifeTwo countries with the same workforce, one with twice the machinery and twice the years of schooling, producing more per person. Both differences pull the same way.The aggregate production function shows that output per capita is positively related to both physical and human capital per capita. It is the same function read one level down, and it is where the two accumulable determinants live. The relationship is positive but flattening in each of them, which is the shape that makes capital deepening a growth strategy with a limit and technological improvement one without.
Capital deepening
Raising capital per worker. It raises output per worker by less each time - the growth that runs out.In real lifeA delivery firm’s tenth van adds less to what each driver moves than its second did, because the drivers, routes and depots did not grow to match.Not a CED term, and included because it names the shape of the per-worker curve, which is examined constantly. Each equal increase in capital per worker buys a smaller increase in output per worker: the curve rises throughout and flattens throughout, and never turns down. A country growing purely by accumulating capital therefore sees its growth rate slow even while its capital stock rises. Only a shift of the curve - better technology, better human capital - escapes that, which is the substance of the contrast between the two per-worker figures on this page.
The PPC and the LRAS curve
An outward shift of the PPC and a rightward shift of long-run aggregate supply are the same event on different axes.In real lifeA decade of new ports, schools and power stations raises what a country can produce every year afterwards - and there are two standard ways of drawing that one fact.An outward shift in the PPC is analogous to a rightward shift of the long-run aggregate supply curve. Both draw one event - the economy’s maximum sustainable capacity rising - on different pairs of axes, so a growth question can be answered on either diagram and an answer that moves one and not the other is inconsistent. The corollary is the trap: a point moving from inside the frontier back onto it is a recovery using capacity that was already there, and it is not growth however much real GDP rises.

5.7 Public policy and economic growth

The last topic is the one that decides whether the vertical line in every other diagram in this unit stays where it is - and the CED is careful, twice over, about what a “growth policy” is allowed to be.

What makes a policy a growth policy
It has to move productivity or labor force participation. Anything else changes what is bought, not what can be produced.In real lifeA childcare subsidy that lets a parent return to work raises the number of people producing; a training grant raises what each of them produces. Both are growth policies, by different routes.Public policies that impact productivity and labor force participation affect real GDP per capita and economic growth. This is the test to run on any policy a stem describes: does it raise output per worker, or does it raise the number of people working? A policy that does neither may raise aggregate demand, may close an output gap, may be a good idea - and is still not a growth policy in this unit’s sense, because the vertical line has not moved.
Policies aimed at participation
Anything that raises the share of the adult population willing and able to work: childcare, transport, retraining, retirement rules.In real lifeAffordable childcare places that let a parent who wanted paid work take it. The person existed before; the job did not fit around them.The second of the two routes named in the CED’s policy statement, and the one that is easy to overlook because it is not about productivity at all. Raising participation raises real GDP per capita by putting more of the same population to work - the numerator rises while the denominator does not. It also lowers the natural rate of unemployment where it works by improving how well people and jobs match, which shifts the vertical long-run Phillips curve left and long-run aggregate supply right. It is the one route with a natural ceiling: everybody who can work eventually is.
Infrastructure
The shared physical capital an economy runs on: roads, ports, power, water, communications networks.In real lifeA new rail line that takes two hours off the journey between a factory and its port lowers costs for every firm using it, not only for the one that lobbied for it.Government policies that invest in infrastructure and technology affect growth. Infrastructure is physical capital with a particular property: it raises productivity for many firms at once, and most of them would not have built it themselves. That is what puts it on the government’s side of the ledger in this topic. It is also the clearest case of a growth policy that is simultaneously an aggregate demand policy, since the construction is bought in the year it happens.
Policies that invest in technology
Research funding, development subsidies, education in technical fields - spending aimed at the determinant that does not run out.In real lifeA public grant for research whose results are published and used by firms that never paid for it. The knowledge does not get used up when a second firm uses it.The other half of the investment statement, and the half aimed at the determinant that is not a per-worker quantity. Technology is the source of growth that does not run into diminishing returns, so policies that raise it - research funding, development subsidies, technical education - raise the long-run growth rate rather than merely the level of output. It is also the clearest example of why the government does it: the results are hard to keep to yourself, so a firm deciding alone would fund less of it than is worth funding.

Potential output
What the economy can sustainably produce with its current resources and technology. The vertical line every other topic treats as fixed.In real lifeThe output an economy could keep producing year after year without the labour market overheating - the level economists are referring to when they say a country is running above or below capacity.The same quantity as full-employment real output, as the position of the long-run aggregate supply line, as a point on the production possibilities frontier, and as the output that goes with the natural rate of unemployment on the Phillips diagram. Four descriptions, one number. Every other topic in this unit takes it as given and asks what policy can do around it; this topic is the only one in which policy moves it, which is what makes growth policy different in kind from stabilization policy.
Incentives, as the mechanism

Policies of this kind do not act on a curve directly - they change what households and firms find worth doing, and households and firms then act on both sides of the market. Work more and you earn more and spend more; invest more and you buy capital goods now and produce more later. That is why the CED refuses to file these policies on one side of the model, and why an exam answer that picks one column is wrong for a reason rather than by bad luck.

Short run or long run: the unit in two columns

Every one of the CED’s four tensions in this unit is the same tension: a claim that is true at one horizon and false at the other. This section holds nothing new. It sorts what the seven topics have already said into the two columns the exam is choosing between.

The short run, in this unit
Wages, prices and expectations have not finished adjusting. Real output can move; the trade-off is live.In real lifeA two-year wage contract signed last year. Whatever prices do this quarter, that wage is what it is until the contract expires.Not an amount of calendar time but a state of prices. In the short run some input prices are fixed, so aggregate supply slopes up, the short-run Phillips curve slopes down, policy moves real output, and money is not neutral. Every verdict in this unit’s left-hand column depends on that one condition. When a stem says “in the short run” it is telling you which column to read from, and it is doing so precisely because the other column says the opposite.
The long run, in this unit
Every price and expectation has caught up. Real output is at potential; only nominal variables are free to move.In real lifeA decade after a burst of inflation, every contract has been renegotiated at least once and every expectation has been revised. Nothing is left stuck.The state in which nothing is fixed by contract or by an out-of-date expectation. Aggregate supply is vertical, the Phillips curve is vertical, output sits at potential, and a change in the money supply moves the price level and nothing real. The one thing that can move real output in the long run is a change in capacity itself - more capital, more human capital, better technology, more participation - which is why topics 5.6 and 5.7 are the only two in this unit whose answers are about real quantities at this horizon.
The words that pick the horizon
“In the long run”, “fully anticipated”, “prices and wages are flexible”, “in long-run equilibrium” - all four choose the same column.In real lifeTwo questions with identical numbers and one extra clause. The clause is not decoration; it is the whole question.A stem in this unit almost never asks a bare question. It attaches a horizon, and the attachments are formulaic: in the short run, with an upward-sloping aggregate supply curve and unexpected all pick the left column. In the long run, fully anticipated, if all prices including wages are completely flexible, in long-run equilibrium and at full employment all pick the right one. Learning the phrases is worth as much as learning either column, because a correct verdict read from the wrong column scores nothing.
