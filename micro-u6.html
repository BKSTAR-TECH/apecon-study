6.1 Socially efficient and inefficient market outcomes

Unit 2 proved that a competitive market maximises total surplus. This unit is the list of ways that proof can quietly stop applying, and the sentence that tells you when it still does.

The efficient quantity
Where the last unit's marginal benefit equals its marginal cost.In real lifeA bakery that keeps baking loaves exactly until the next loaf would cost more flour and labour than the next customer would pay for it, and stops there.The quantity at which the marginal benefit of consuming the last unit equals the marginal cost of producing it, which is exactly the quantity that maximises total economic surplus. This is Unit 2's equilibrium result restated as a target rather than as a description: a competitive market REACHES this quantity, but reaching it and being defined by it are two different claims, and this unit is about the gap between them.
When the market gets it right
The market lands on the efficient quantity only if nothing is external.In real lifeA farmers' market for tomatoes, where every cost of growing them falls on the grower and every benefit of eating them falls on the buyer. Nobody else's costs or benefits are riding along on that trade, so the market price does the whole job by itself.The market equilibrium quantity equals the socially optimal quantity only when all social benefits and costs are internalized by the individuals actually in the market - that is, when nothing about the trade spills onto anybody outside it. Total economic surplus is maximised exactly there. Every topic that follows in this unit is a way that condition can fail: an externality that is not internalized, a good nobody can be excluded from, a firm large enough to set its own price. The rule itself never changes; what changes is whether its condition holds.
The hinge the whole unit turns on The market equilibrium is the socially optimal quantity only when all social benefits and costs are internalized. Read that condition as load-bearing, not as a footnote. Every topic after this one - externalities, public goods, market power, government policy - is a way that condition fails, and the fix in every case is to make somebody bear a cost or receive a benefit that is currently falling on nobody in the transaction at all.

Why the equilibrium can be wrong even though nobody is behaving badly

Market power
The ability to influence price rather than simply accept it.In real lifeThe only bakery in a small town can raise the price of bread and still sell most of it, because customers who want bread nearby have nowhere else to go. A bakery on a street with twenty competitors cannot.Rational agents can pursue private actions to exploit or exercise market power - characteristics of a market, such as being the only seller or holding better information than the other side, that let an agent affect the price or terms of trade rather than simply take them. Exercising market power is not irrational or unusual; it is what a self-interested agent does when the market lets it, and the result can still be an inefficient allocation even though nobody involved made a mistake.
Private optimization, and where it can go wrong
Agents equate their OWN benefit and cost, not society's.In real lifeA commuter who drives instead of taking the train weighs their own time and petrol against their own convenience - and does not weigh the extra minute of congestion that decision adds to every other driver on the highway that morning.Rational agents make what is, from where they stand, an optimal decision: they equate their own private marginal benefits and private marginal costs. That equation can still result in market inefficiencies, because private marginal benefit and private marginal cost are not always the same numbers as marginal benefit and marginal cost to society as a whole. The agent is not behaving badly; the accounting it is using is simply incomplete.
Cost-benefit analysis, for policy
Weighing what a policy costs against what it is expected to fix.In real lifeA city comparing the cost of installing a traffic light against the value of the accidents it is expected to prevent, before deciding whether to install it.Policymakers use cost-benefit analysis to evaluate different actions meant to reduce or eliminate a market inefficiency: what would the intervention cost to put in place and to enforce, and what inefficiency would it remove? An intervention that costs more to run than the inefficiency it removes is a real possibility this analysis is built to catch, which is part of why "government could fix this" is not the same claim as "government fixing this is worth it".
The social-efficiency condition
Fix the private incentive so it equals the social one, and the inefficiency goes with it.In real lifeA refundable bottle deposit, sized so that returning the bottle is worth exactly what the litter it prevents is worth to everyone else. Once that is true, a self-interested returner and a socially optimal returner want the same thing.Market inefficiencies can be eliminated by designing policies that equate marginal social benefit with marginal social cost - not by ordering agents to behave differently, but by changing the private numbers an agent is optimising until they match the social ones. Every remedy named later in this unit, from a corrective tax to a lump-sum subsidy, is one way of doing exactly that.
Trap — inefficiency is not a synonym for irrationality Nothing in this unit says agents stop maximising. A firm with market power is still choosing its best available action; so is a buyer who ignores a cost that falls on somebody else, because from where that buyer stands, it genuinely is somebody else’s cost. The word doing the work is private: private marginal benefits and private marginal costs are optimised perfectly, and the outcome is inefficient anyway, because private is not the same set of numbers as social.

What can go wrong, and what it always costs

Seven causes of market failure
A short, examinable list, and half of it is not externalities.In real lifeA single cable provider in a town (monopoly), three airlines on one route (oligopoly), dozens of coffee shops each with a slightly different brand (monopolistic competition), a polluting factory (an externality), a used-car lot where only the seller knows which cars are lemons (asymmetric information), and a lighthouse nobody would build alone (insufficient public goods) - six different reasons the same phrase "the market got it wrong" can be true.Equilibrium allocations can deviate from efficient ones for several distinct reasons, and the CED names them together on purpose: monopoly; oligopoly; monopolistic competition; negative and positive externalities in production or consumption; asymmetric information; and insufficient production of public goods. A page, or a student, who names externalities and stops has covered one of six causes while reading as though the list were finished. Sections 6.2 and 6.3 take up externalities and public goods in depth; the market-structure causes were Units 3 and 4's subject and return here only as the object of 6.4's policy.
Deadweight loss, without a cause attached
ANY quantity that is not the efficient one costs surplus - too little or too much.In real lifeA concert venue that sells fewer tickets than fans would gladly buy at a price that still covers costs loses exactly the same KIND of value as a factory that keeps running past the point where the next unit costs more to make than anyone will pay for it - one undershoots, one overshoots, and both leave money that trade could have created sitting on the table.Producing any non-efficient quantity results in deadweight loss. That sentence runs both directions, and most of this course's deadweight-loss triangles are the one direction: a tax, a ceiling, a monopoly, all of which REDUCE output below the efficient quantity. This unit's G-M03 figure draws the other direction too - trade running PAST the efficient quantity, from a subsidy, a positive externality nobody paid for, or a floor - because the loss is a property of missing the target, not a penalty reserved for undershooting it.
The efficiency rule, in private terms and in social terms
VersionStated usingTrue at the market equilibriumTrue at the socially optimal quantity
The efficient quantityMarginal benefit and marginal costAlways — it is what equilibrium meansOnly if nothing external is happening
The socially optimal quantity, in social termsMarginal SOCIAL benefit and marginal SOCIAL costOnly if nothing external is happeningAlways — it is the definition of socially optimal
The line that separates themThe two rules are the SAME sentence with every word promoted from private to social, and they name the identical quantity whenever 6.1’s internalization condition holds. They come apart only once something external enters — which is the whole of 6.2 — and a page that presents them as two competing rules rather than one rule under two assumptions invites “which one do I use”, when the honest answer is that a market with nothing external makes the question moot.
Worked example — reading a departure from Q* either way A city caps the rent on a class of apartments below the market rate. The bank of units actually rented sits BELOW the quantity that would clear the market at the true marginal cost and marginal benefit.
Which figure applies? The “too little traded” one. Every apartment between the capped quantity and the market quantity would have been worth more to a tenant than it cost a landlord to offer, and none of those units exist.
Now a subsidised activity that pollutes as a byproduct, priced as if the pollution were free. Output runs PAST the quantity that would equate marginal benefit with the true marginal cost, pollution included.
Which figure applies? The “too much traded” one - same triangle, opposite side of Q*, because deadweight loss is not a penalty for producing too little. It is what ANY non-efficient quantity costs.

6.2 Externalities

Every non-efficient outcome in 6.1 needed a cause. This topic gives the two commonest ones a name: a cost or a benefit that lands on somebody outside the trade.

The socially optimal quantity, in social terms
Where marginal SOCIAL benefit equals marginal SOCIAL cost.In real lifeThe quantity of vaccinations at which one more dose's benefit - to the person vaccinated AND to everyone they will not infect - just equals the cost of producing it. Not the quantity at which the vaccinated person alone breaks even.The socially optimal quantity of a good occurs where the marginal social benefit of consuming the last unit equals the marginal social cost of producing it, maximising total economic surplus. This is the same sentence as 6.1's efficient-quantity rule with every word promoted from private to social - the two coincide exactly under 6.1's internalization condition, and diverge only once something external enters the picture, which is what the rest of this topic is about.
Why externalities happen at all
Undefined ownership, or bargaining too costly to be worth arranging.In real lifeNobody owns the air over a factory, so nobody can charge the factory for using it as a dump, and the cost of tracking down and negotiating with every affected household would swamp any deal that negotiation could produce.Externalities, positive or negative, arise from a lack of well-defined property rights and/or high transaction costs. Property rights give a reason to price something: if the air, the view, or the quiet were owned, the owner could charge for using it up. Where rights exist but the cost of enforcing or negotiating them is too high to bother, the same gap appears for a second reason. Both causes point toward the same two remedies in 6.2's policy list: define the right, or lower the cost of bargaining over it.
Private costs and benefits, once more
Agents respond to what lands on THEM, not to what lands elsewhere.In real lifeA commuter choosing whether to drive weighs petrol and time. The extra congestion imposed on every other driver that morning is real, and it plays no part in that commuter's decision at all.In the presence of externalities, rational agents respond to private costs and benefits and not to external costs and benefits. This is the same private-optimization idea from 6.1, restated as the reason externalities persist rather than correct themselves: nothing in an ordinary market transaction forces either side to notice a cost or benefit that is not landing on them, so the gap is stable until something outside the transaction - a tax, a rule, a bargain - forces it into view.
The free-rider incentivealso called free riding or the free-rider incentiveAlways about non-excludability. A good can be as non-rival as you like; without non-excludability there is no free ride, because a non-payer can be kept out.
Why pay for something you would get anyway?In real lifeA public radio pledge drive that most listeners never call in to. The signal keeps broadcasting whether or not any one listener pays, so each has a private reason to let somebody else's pledge cover it.Rational agents have the incentive to free ride when a good is non-excludable - when a non-payer cannot be kept from enjoying it. Note what the incentive is pinned to: non-excludability alone, not rivalry and not "public good" as a bundled idea. A good can be perfectly rival and still invite free riding, provided nobody can be excluded from it - which is exactly 6.3's open-access case.
Which curve moves, in one line
In PRODUCTIONthe COST curve splits — MPC against MSC
In CONSUMPTIONthe BENEFIT curve splits — MPB against MSB
Negative means the social curve is the worse one to be on; positive means it is the better one
Memorising “negative means overproduce” gets three of the four cases right and the fourth wrong. Positive-production ALSO overproduces less than it should relative to the social optimum - no, it UNDERproduces, exactly like positive-consumption - because “positive” always means the social curve sits on the side that would call for MORE of the good, whichever curve it is.
Externalities: all four cases
CaseWhich curve splitsSocial curve, relative to privateMarket result
Negative, in productionCost: MPC against MSCMSC lies ABOVE MPCOverproduces
Positive, in productionCost: MPC against MSCMSC lies BELOW MPCUnderproduces
Negative, in consumptionBenefit: MPB against MSBMSB lies BELOW MPBOverproduces

The line that separates themTwo questions, asked in order, answer all four rows without any memorising of a table. First: production or consumption — that alone says which curve moves. Second: positive or negative — that alone says which side of the private curve the social one falls on, and whether the result is over- or under-production. “Negative means overproduce” is right twice and wrong nowhere in this table BECAUSE both negative rows say overproduce — the trap is a shortcut that skips the first question and still happens to work here, and then gets applied somewhere it should not.

Why the gap exists at all

Externalities are not a separate law of nature from the rest of the market model - they are what happens when the two things Unit 2 quietly assumed are missing.

Five remedies for an externalityalso called a Pigovian tax or an internalizing taxNamed for Arthur Pigou, who proposed taxing the externality itself rather than banning the activity. The exam says “corrective tax” or just “per-unit tax”; a textbook usually says Pigovian.
Price it, limit it, provide it, own it, or bargain over it.In real lifeA carbon tax prices emissions directly; a factory emissions cap limits them by rule; a municipal park is publicly provided rather than left to a private developer; a fishing quota assigns a property right to a fixed catch; and two neighbouring farms splitting the cost of a shared drainage ditch settle an externality through private transactions with no government involved at all.Policies that address positive or negative externalities include taxes/subsidies, environmental regulation, public provision, the assignment of property rights, and the reassignment of property rights through private transactions. The first three are the ones a page reaches for by reflex; the last two are the ones most often dropped, and they are the interesting pair - the state can decide who owns the harm instead of pricing it, and once ownership is settled, the two sides can simply trade the right between themselves.

The last of the five - the reassignment of property rights through private transactionsalso called the Coase approach, bargaining around an externality or a Coasian solutionThe CED’s own wording is the clunky one; almost every other source calls this the Coase theorem, after Ronald Coase. - needs no tax collector and no regulator at all: once a right is defined, the two sides can simply bargain their way to the efficient outcome, provided the cost of bargaining is not itself the problem.

Trap — the agents are not the ones behaving badly
Two different reasons the market misses the efficient quantity
CauseWhat is actually brokenWhere the gap shows upTypical remedy
An externalityA cost or benefit lands on somebody outside the tradeA wedge between the private and social versions of ONE curveA corrective tax/subsidy, or an assigned property right
Market powerThe seller can set price rather than take itMarginal revenue falls below demand, so MR = MC undershoots the competitive quantityPrice regulation, or antitrust action on the market’s structure
The line that separates themAn externality is a problem with what a curve MEASURES — it is missing a cost or benefit that belongs on it. Market power is a problem with which curve a firm actually TRADES ON — marginal revenue, not demand. Nothing stops a market from having both at once, which is exactly what 6.2a’s figure draws, and the two distortions do not simply add: which one dominates the final quantity is a question about their relative size, not a rule to memorise.
Worked example — naming the case from a sentence “A brewery's wastewater raises downstream cleanup costs for a water utility.” A cost, on the production side, landing on somebody who is not the brewery or its customers: negative externality in production. MSC sits above MPC, and the market overproduces beer relative to the efficient quantity.
“A homeowner's flower garden raises the resale value of every house on the street.” A benefit, on the consumption side - the homeowner is the one enjoying the flowers, and the neighbours are the ones who gain from having planted nothing. Positive externality in consumption; MSB sits above MPB, and the market underproduces gardening relative to the efficient quantity.
What fixes each one? A per-unit tax on the wastewater case, sized to the external cost; a per-unit subsidy on the gardening case, sized to the external benefit - the same corrective-tax construction run with the sign reversed.

When two failures meet: market power and an externality

6.2's causes of inefficiency were listed separately in 6.1 - monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition, externalities - but nothing says a market suffers from only one at a time. This is the CED's own worked case where they collide.

Read the market outcome off the curve the firm is actually on MR = MPC gives the firm's own choice. MSC = MSB gives society's. Neither is P = MC, and neither is the plain competitive answer from 6.2. A firm with market power is never on the demand curve for its pricing decision - it is on marginal revenue - so the market quantity in this figure comes from a different pair of curves than the socially optimal one does, and reading both off the same pair is the error this figure exists to catch.
Trap — “monopoly restricts output, externalities inflate it, so they cancel out” They do not cancel; they compete, and the arithmetic decides the winner. A negative production externality on its own pushes the competitive quantity DOWN toward the efficient one from above. Market power pushes the SAME firm's quantity down further still, from its own private-cost benchmark. Whether the result sits above or below the true efficient quantity depends on which push is larger - there is no general answer, and the CED's own figure is drawn with the markup winning.

6.3 Public and private goods

Two questions, asked of any good: can a non-payer be kept out, and does one person’s use leave less for the next person? The four honest combinations of yes and no are the whole topic.

Private and public goods, by two tests
Rival and excludable makes a private good; neither makes a public one.In real lifeA sandwich is rival (eating it leaves none for anyone else) and excludable (the shop can refuse to hand it over unpaid) - an ordinary private good. A lighthouse's beam is neither: one ship's use of it leaves just as much light for the next ship, and no ship passing within sight can be made to pay for it.A good is rival if one person's consumption reduces what is left for anyone else, and excludable if a non-payer can be kept from consuming it. Private goods are rival and excludable; public goods are non-rival and non-excludable - the two extreme corners of the grid this section draws in full.
Why the market underprovides a public good
Free riding leaves government as the only reliable producer.In real lifeA neighbourhood flood levee that protects every house on the street whether or not that house's owner chipped in to build it. Left to private funding, too few owners pay and the levee is never built at the size the street actually needs.Because of the free rider problem, private individuals usually lack the incentive to produce public goods on their own, which leaves government as the only producer likely to supply them at anything near the efficient quantity. This is a market-failure argument for public provision specifically for goods that fail BOTH tests - it is not a general argument that government should provide anything the market underprovides for other reasons, which is a different failure entirely.
Government can also provide a PRIVATE good
Public schooling is rival and excludable - it is provided anyway.In real lifeA seat in a public school classroom is rival (one more student in a full classroom crowds it) and could be made excludable (a school could charge tuition and turn non-payers away) - and yet many governments provide it and allow free access.Governments sometimes choose to produce goods that are, by the rival/excludable tests, PRIVATE goods - educational services are the CED's own example - and to allow free access to them. The reason is a policy choice about equity or opportunity, not a public-goods argument: nothing about a school seat fails either test. Students who see "the government provides it" and conclude "so it must be a public good" have the causation backwards; rivalry and excludability decide the category, not who happens to be paying for it.
Open-access resourcesalso called a common resource, a common-pool resource or a common-property resourceNot a public good. Rival and non-excludable, the box public goods and private goods both leave empty - fish stocks, grazing land, an uncongested-until-it-isn’t road.
Rival, but nobody can be kept out - so it gets used up.In real lifeAn ocean fishing ground within reach of any boat that puts to sea. Every fish one boat lands is a fish no other boat can land - rival - and no boat can be turned away from the water - non-excludable.Some natural resources are, by their nature, non-excludable and rival, and are therefore open access. Because no individual can be excluded and every unit taken is a unit gone for everyone else, private individuals inefficiently overconsume such resources - nobody who cannot be kept off the resource has a private reason to leave any of it for later. This is the fourth cell of the rival/excludable grid, and it is the one students most often mislabel: it shares non-excludability with a public good and rivalry with a private good, but belongs to neither.
The rival/excludable grid, as a table
GoodRival?Excludable?Typical market outcome
Private goodYesYesMarkets handle it well — no failure from this cause
Open-access / common resourceYesNoOverconsumed — nobody who cannot be excluded has a reason to hold back
Club goodNoYesUnderused at any positive price, even though serving one more person costs nothing
Public goodNoNoUnderprovided or not provided at all — the free-rider case
The line that separates themOnly the bottom-right cell — failing BOTH tests — is a public good and the free-rider case this unit warns about. The other two failing cells fail only one test each, and they fail in opposite directions: a club good is easy to charge for and hard to justify restricting, an open-access resource is impossible to charge for and easy to overuse. Treating “shared” as one category collapses three different market outcomes into one wrong prediction.
The two tests are independent A good can fail either test alone. Failing BOTH is what makes it a public good. Non-rival and non-excludable are not two names for the same idea, and the two middle cells of the grid exist to prove it: a club good is non-rival but still excludable, and a common resource is excludable’s opposite - non-excludable but still rival. A student who collapses the grid to “shared versus private” cannot place either of them.
Trap — “the government provides it” is not the test
Two different things government does, for two different reasons
StatementWhat is being providedBy which test is it that categoryWhy government does it
Why the market underprovides a public goodA true public good (non-rival, non-excludable)Both tests, togetherThe free-rider problem — private supply is too little or none
Government can also provide a PRIVATE goodA private good (rival, excludable) — education is the CED’s exampleNeither test is failedA policy choice about access or opportunity, unrelated to the two tests
The line that separates themThe provider does not decide the category; the two tests do. A government providing something is evidence about POLICY, not about whether the thing is rival or excludable, and the trap this pair exists to catch is collapsing “government provides it” into “therefore it is a public good”, which would make a public school seat — rival, and excludable if anyone chose to charge for it — a public good by definition. It is not.
Worked example — four goods, four verdicts A subscription streaming service. Excludable (a password wall), non-rival (one more viewer costs nothing) - a club good, priced above marginal cost and underused relative to the efficient quantity.
An unmetered village well. Not excludable (anyone can draw from it), rival (one bucket drawn is one bucket gone) - an open-access common resource, and it is at risk of being drawn down faster than it refills, with no single user having a reason to hold back.
A municipal fireworks display. Not excludable (anyone within sight sees it for free) and not rival (one more spectator subtracts nothing from anyone else's view) - a public good, and the reason towns fund it out of taxes rather than selling tickets to a public park.
A haircut. Rival and excludable on both counts - an ordinary private good, and the market handles it without any of this topic's machinery.

6.4 Government intervention in different market structures

A per-unit tax, a price ceiling, a price floor - Unit 2 taught what each does in a competitive market. This topic asks the question Unit 2 never needed to: does the market structure change the answer?

Per-unit taxes and subsidies, what they touch
One tool, six things it moves, sized by two elasticities.In real lifeThe same $2 fuel tax that barely dents purchases of a fuel with no substitute changes almost nothing about who buys how much of it - and the identical $2 tax on a fuel with an easy substitute reshapes the market noticeably. The tax did not change size; the elasticities did.Per-unit taxes and subsidies affect the total price consumers pay, the net price firms receive, the equilibrium quantity, consumer and producer surplus, deadweight loss, and government revenue or cost - six distinct things worth naming separately rather than folding into "the price changes". The size of every one of those six changes depends on the price elasticity of demand and of supply, which Unit 2 established and this unit takes as read: nothing about the mechanics is new here, only the reminder of everything a single wedge moves at once.
Binding controls across four market structures
The same ceiling or floor does different things depending on the structure it lands on.In real lifeA minimum wage set between the wage a competitive labour market would pay and the (lower) wage a single dominant local employer - a monopsonist - would otherwise pay: in the competitive market it creates unemployment; against the monopsonist, over some range, it can raise BOTH the wage and the number of people hired.Binding price ceilings and floors affect prices and quantities differently depending on the market structures they land on - perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition, and monopsony all respond differently to the same tool - and on the price elasticities of supply and demand on top of that. Perfect competition is the case Unit 2 already drew: a binding ceiling always shrinks the quantity traded there. Impose the same kind of ceiling on a monopolist's price, or the same kind of floor on a monopsonist's wage, and the quantity can move the OTHER way, because both are already restricting the quantity below the competitive one before the policy arrives.
The same binding control, three market structures
Market structureStarting point, before the controlWhat a binding ceiling/floor does to quantity
Perfect competitionAlready at the efficient quantityALWAYS shrinks the quantity traded below it — Unit 2’s result
MonopolyAlready restricting quantity below the competitive levelA price ceiling set between the monopoly price and marginal cost can RAISE quantity
MonopsonyAlready hiring below the competitive level, at a depressed wageA wage floor set between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage can RAISE employment
The line that separates themThe direction the tool pushes quantity depends entirely on whether the market was already at the efficient quantity before the tool arrived. Perfect competition starts there, so any binding control can only pull it away. Monopoly and monopsony start SHORT of it, so a control that pulls the firm toward price-taking behaviour can push quantity the opposite way from what the competitive-market case trained you to expect.
Trap — the same tool, a different verdict A binding price ceiling in a competitive market always creates a shortage - that is settled, from Unit 2. Set a ceiling on a MONOPOLIST'S price instead, between the monopoly price and marginal cost, and it can raise output: a monopolist facing a price floor on what it can charge behaves, at the margin, more like a price taker, and price takers produce more. The tool has not changed. The market structure has, and that changes what the tool does.

The size of the wedge is not the only thing that matters

Lump-sum taxes and subsidiesalso called a fixed fee, a flat charge or a franchise feeA flat amount charged once, regardless of output - a licence fee, a franchise fee, a fixed annual levy. The exam’s own phrase is “lump-sum”; the others are what a business page calls the same thing.
A flat amount, however much or little you produce - so it never touches the margin.In real lifeA restaurant's annual health-permit fee. It costs the same whether the kitchen serves ten meals a day or four hundred, so it never enters the decision about whether to cook one more meal.Lump-sum taxes and lump-sum subsidies do not change either marginal cost or marginal benefit; only fixed costs are affected. Because the profit-maximising quantity is found by comparing MARGINAL cost against marginal benefit or marginal revenue, a lump-sum payment that never touches either one leaves the quantity produced exactly where it was. It can still push a firm into or out of business - a large enough lump-sum tax can turn a normal profit into a loss - but it never moves the quantity a firm chooses to make while it keeps operating.
Per-unit against lump-sum, taxes or subsidies
What changesEffect on the profit-maximising quantityEffect on fixed cost
Per-unit taxes and subsidies, what they touchMarginal cost (a per-unit tax) or the wedge at every quantityMoves it — the whole point of drawing it as a vertical shiftNone
Lump-sum taxes and subsidiesA single flat amount, however much is producedNever moves it — nothing marginal changedAbsorbs the whole amount
The line that separates themAsk what the payment is a function OF. A per-unit tax is a function of quantity, so it changes the marginal calculation and therefore the quantity chosen. A lump-sum tax is a function of nothing the firm produces, so the marginal calculation is untouched and the quantity chosen does not move — the payment can still close a firm down, but it does so through the bottom line, not through the margin.

When intervention actually helps

When intervention helps, and the condition that makes it true
It can raise efficiency - but only aimed at the actual failure.In real lifeA tax on a factory's emissions, sized to the pollution damage, raises efficiency because it targets exactly the gap between private and social cost that caused the market to overproduce. An unrelated tax on the factory's payroll would raise revenue and change nothing about the pollution at all.Government intervention in imperfect markets can increase efficiency if the policy correctly addresses the incentives that led to the market failure. Read against Unit 2's parallel rule - that intervention in an ALREADY efficient market can only reduce it - the two statements are not in tension: they describe different starting points. The condition here is not decorative. A tax that is the wrong size, or aimed at the wrong margin, can leave a market no better off than the failure it was meant to fix, or worse off.
The condition that makes the promise honest Government intervention in imperfect markets can increase efficiency if the policy correctly addresses the incentive that caused the failure. This is the direct counterweight to Unit 2's rule that intervention in an ALREADY efficient market can only reduce efficiency (see micro-u2 topic 2.8a). Both are true at once because they are talking about different markets: one about a market with nothing wrong with it, the other about a market with something wrong that the policy is actually aimed at. Drop the qualifier from either one and it becomes a slogan - "government intervention always helps" or "government intervention always hurts" - and the exam rewards neither.

Regulating a natural monopoly

Price regulation of monopoly
A regulator can set the price a monopolist may charge.In real lifeA regional electricity distributor's prices are set by a public utility commission rather than by the company's own unconstrained choice of the price that maximises its profit.Government can use price regulation to address the inefficiency that comes from monopoly - setting a maximum price forces the firm off the profit-maximising point it would otherwise choose. Where that maximum lands matters: 6.4's natural-monopoly figure shows the same regulator choosing between a fair-return price and an allocatively efficient one, and the two are not the same target.
A natural monopoly needs a subsidy to be priced efficientlyalso called a declining-cost industry or a decreasing-cost firmThe label describes the cost structure, not the conduct: average total cost keeps falling over the whole range of output the market could use, so one firm genuinely serves it more cheaply than two.
Marginal-cost pricing on a falling ATC always loses money.In real lifeA rural water utility ordered to charge exactly the cost of the next gallon, when its pipes and treatment plant are a huge fixed cost spread over relatively few customers. It cannot recover that fixed cost from a price set at marginal cost alone, however efficient that price is.A natural monopoly will require a lump-sum subsidy to produce at the allocatively efficient quantity, because on a firm whose average total cost keeps falling across the relevant range, marginal cost sits below average total cost everywhere - so a price set at marginal cost guarantees a loss. A LUMP-SUM subsidy, rather than a per-unit one, is the point worth noticing twice: it can cover the loss without changing the marginal incentive that put the firm at the efficient quantity in the first place.

The line that separates themQuantity rises as the price rule moves from the firm’s own preference toward society’s, and profit falls the same way — by the time the price is allocatively efficient, the firm needs outside help to keep operating at all. There is no rule here that is free: the unregulated price wastes surplus, the fair-return price is not quite allocatively efficient, and the efficient price needs a subsidy from somewhere else.

Antitrust: the other tool

Antitrust policyalso called competition policy or anti-monopoly lawThe same body of law is called competition policy almost everywhere outside the United States; the exam only ever says antitrust.
Law aimed at keeping markets competitive, not at any one price.In real lifeA regulator blocking a merger between the two largest firms in an industry, on the grounds that combining them would leave too few competitors for the market to discipline prices on its own.Governments use antitrust policy in an attempt to make markets more competitive - blocking mergers that would concentrate market power, breaking up firms found to hold it unlawfully, or prohibiting coordinated pricing between rivals. It is a STRUCTURAL remedy, aimed at the number and behaviour of firms in a market, which is what separates it from the price regulation and subsidy tools aimed at a single firm's price or output.
Two tools aimed at market power, from different directions
ToolWhat it targetsLeaves the market structureTypical use
Price regulation of monopolyA single firm’s price or outputUnchanged — still one firmA natural monopoly that genuinely serves the market more cheaply as one firm
Antitrust policyThe number and behaviour of firmsChanged — a merger blocked, a firm broken up, collusion prohibitedA market where nothing about the cost structure requires one firm
The line that separates themPrice regulation accepts the market structure and constrains what the firm inside it may charge. Antitrust policy changes the structure itself. Using the wrong one is the examined error: regulating the PRICE of a natural monopoly can work because the cost structure genuinely favours one firm; trying to break a natural monopoly up into competing firms throws away the cost advantage that made it natural in the first place.
What the CED rules out here Not on the exam: a graph of the inefficiency collusion causes, or of a policy response to it. Collusion stays on the list of things that can make a market allocation inefficient - it is one of the seven causes named back in 6.1 - but no figure on this page, and none on the AP Exam, draws its deadweight loss the way this topic draws a monopoly's.
Worked example — naming the right remedy A single water utility whose average cost keeps falling as it serves more customers. A natural monopoly. Left alone it earns economic profit at a high price; regulate it to price at marginal cost and it loses money on every unit. The fix named by the CED is a lump-sum subsidy - a payment that covers the resulting loss without touching the marginal incentive to produce one more unit, which is exactly what a lump-sum payment does and a per-unit one would not.
Two firms found to have coordinated prices instead of competing. Not a natural-monopoly problem and not a per-unit-tax problem - an antitrust case, aimed at the market's STRUCTURE or conduct rather than at a curve on a graph.

6.5 Inequality

The last topic turns the page from efficiency to distribution: two economies can each sit exactly at their efficient quantity and still divide the resulting income in very different ways.

How unequal, and where
Income and poverty vary a lot, both across groups and within them.In real lifeTwo households of the same age, in the same city, working in the same industry, can still sit at very different points on the income ladder - the variation this statement names is not only between countries or between obviously different groups, it persists within groups that look alike from the outside.Income levels and poverty rates vary greatly both ACROSS and WITHIN groups - by age, gender, race, and other characteristics - and across countries. The "within" half is the one a page can skip past: two people who share every demographic category a stem might name can still be far apart in income, for the eight reasons 6.5's second half lists.
The Lorenz curve and Gini coefficientalso called the income-distribution curveNamed for Max Lorenz, who published it in 1905. The CED calls it only by name; nothing else on this page calls it anything else either, so this row exists mainly because a reader meeting it in a textbook should know it is the same curve.
A picture and a number for the same idea: how far from equal.In real lifeTwo countries reporting the identical average income per person can still produce very different Lorenz curves - one close to the diagonal, one bowed far below it - because the average says nothing about how that income is actually split up.The Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient are used to represent the degree of inequality in a distribution, and to compare distributions across different countries, policies, or time periods. The Lorenz curve is the picture: cumulative share of households against cumulative share of income, sagging below the diagonal of perfect equality by more as inequality is greater. The Gini coefficient turns that same picture into one number between 0 (perfect equality) and 1 (total inequality) - useful for a headline, at the cost of the shape the curve itself shows.

The Gini coefficientalso called the Gini ratio or the Gini indexThree names, one number between 0 and 1. Where the differences in income come from

Factor payments as a source of inequality
Pay tracks marginal product - so unequal product becomes unequal pay.In real lifeA software engineer whose work adds a large amount to a firm's revenue is paid more than a worker whose labour adds less, in a competitive labour market, with nobody having decided to treat either of them unfairly - Unit 5's factor-market result arriving here as a cause rather than a conclusion.Each factor of production receives the value of its marginal product, which can contribute to income inequality: differences in productivity, whatever their source, translate directly into differences in factor income. This is one source on 6.5's list, not the whole explanation for income inequality - it says nothing by itself about WHY marginal products differ, which is where the rest of this section's causes come in.
Eight sources of income and wealth inequality
A page that names two of these has covered a quarter of the statement.In real lifeOne household benefits from a large inheritance, ready access to credit at a low rate, and strong bargaining power at a unionised employer; a second, with similar current earnings, has none of the three. Nothing about their current wage explains the gap that opens up between them over a working life.Sources of income and wealth inequality include differences in tax structures (progressive and regressive tax structures), human capital, social capital, inheritance, effects of discrimination, access to financial markets, mobility, and bargaining power within economic and social units such as firms, labor unions, and families. Eight separately examinable causes, and the four most often dropped from a shorter list - social capital, discrimination, mobility, and bargaining power - are exactly as examinable as the four usually remembered.

The line that separates themNone of the eight is a restatement of another. A page that offers two of them — usually education and discrimination — and stops has covered a quarter of an examinable list while reading as though the list were finished; tax structure, inheritance, access to financial markets and bargaining power are each worth a mark on their own.
Trap — unequal pay is not automatically unfair pay Unit 5's factor-market result reappears here as a CAUSE rather than a conclusion: each factor of production earns the value of its own marginal product, so two workers with different marginal products earn different wages with nobody having discriminated against either of them. That is one source on an eight-item list, not the whole explanation - discrimination, inheritance, tax structure, and access to financial markets sit on the same list and are separately examinable, each with its own mechanism.
Worked example — two households, two different sources Household A saved through a workplace retirement plan for thirty years and invests the proceeds; the returns compound long after the working years end.
Household B works the same number of years at a similar wage but has no access to a retirement plan and no spare income to invest.
The GAP between them at retirement is not explained by differences in current wages at all - it is access to financial markets and the compounding it makes possible, one of the eight sources on the list, operating on top of whatever the wage gap between them was to begin with.

The card to memorize

The rules

  • Market equilibrium = socially optimal quantity only when costs and benefits are fully internalized
  • ANY non-efficient quantity creates deadweight loss — too little AND too much
  • Production externality: the COST curve splits, MPC against MSC
  • Consumption externality: the BENEFIT curve splits, MPB against MSB
  • Negative externality: the market OVERPRODUCES. Positive: it UNDERPRODUCES
  • Free riding needs only non-excludability — rivalry is irrelevant to it
  • Public good = non-rival AND non-excludable, both tests, not one
  • An open-access resource is RIVAL and non-excludable — not a public good
  • Lump-sum taxes/subsidies change fixed cost only, never marginal cost or quantity
  • A natural monopoly priced at MC needs a lump-sum subsidy to survive
  • Intervention can raise efficiency — only if it targets the actual incentive that failed

The five externality remedies

  • Taxes and subsidies — price the externality directly
  • Environmental regulation — limit the activity by rule, not by price
  • Public provision — government supplies the good itself
  • Assignment of property rights — define who owns the harm or the benefit
  • Reassignment through private transactions — let the two sides bargain it out (Coase)

Sources of income inequality

  • Differences in the value of marginal product
  • Progressive and regressive tax structures
  • Human capital, social capital, and inheritance
  • Discrimination, mobility, and access to financial markets
  • Bargaining power within firms, unions and families
If you can do these six things, you are ready for Unit 6

  2. Design a corrective tax or subsidy that closes an externality gap exactly, and explain why it works without banning anything.
  3. Sort any good onto the rival/excludable grid and name the market failure, if any, that follows from where it lands.
  4. Explain why a natural monopoly priced at marginal cost loses money, and what fixes that without moving the price.
  5. Read a percentage of income off a given Lorenz curve, and say which of two countries is more equal without computing anything.
  6. List at least five separate sources of income inequality, none of them “discrimination” twice in different words.
