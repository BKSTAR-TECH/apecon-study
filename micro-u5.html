5.1 Factor markets, and why their demand is derived

Everything in this unit happens on the other side of the firm. Units 3 and 4 watched a firm sell; Unit 5 watches the same firm buy — workers, machines, land — and the picture is a market like any other, with the firm standing where the customer used to stand.

The sentence the whole unit unwinds from Nobody wants a worker. They want what the worker makes, and they will pay for the worker exactly what that is worth to them. Hold that and the rest is derivable. Labour demand slopes down, because each extra worker adds less. It shifts when the output’s price changes, because the same physical output is suddenly worth more or less. It shifts when productivity changes, because the same worker is suddenly making more. And the firm stops hiring at the point where the next worker would cost more than they bring in. Four results, one premise.

What a factor market is

Factor market
A market where firms buy the inputs they use to produce, instead of selling output.also called resource market, input market or the market for factors of productionThe CED itself uses “factor” and “resource” interchangeably, sometimes in the same sentence, which is why the parenthesis appears in the name of half the terms below.In real lifeThe market for warehouse forklift drivers in one metro area. No box is bought or sold there - only the labour that moves boxes elsewhere.A market in which firms are the buyers and households (or other firms) are the sellers of an input to production - labour, capital or land. It has the same two curves and the same crossing as a product market, and every tool from Units 2 and 3 - a shift, a movement along, an elasticity, a surplus - carries over unchanged. What changes is who stands on which side.
Laborpaid in wage
Human effort and skill, sold by the hour or by the job.In real lifeA plumber’s morning: the plumber sells hours and expertise, and is paid a wage for the callout.One of the three factors this unit’s CED names - labor, capital, and land - and the only one that arrives attached to a person. Its payment is the wage, and because a person supplies it, labour markets are the only factor market where the supply side is examined in any depth: working conditions, immigration and a taste for leisure all move a supply curve that a plot of land or a lease of machinery does not have.
Capitalpaid in interest
Equipment, tools and buildings used to produce - not money in a bank account.In real lifeThe ovens, mixers and delivery vans a bakery uses. Buying more of them means borrowing, and interest is the price of that loan.The second of the CED’s three named factors: physical capital, built rather than found - machinery, structures, tools. Its payment is interest, because acquiring more of it usually means borrowing to pay for it. This course keeps “capital” to this physical sense throughout; a firm’s cash balance is not a factor of production.
Landpaid in rent
Natural resources used in production - not only acreage.In real lifeThe plot a shopping centre sits on, and the seam of ore a mining company works. Neither was manufactured; both are used up or occupied to produce.The third of the CED’s three named factors: natural resources, not only the ground under a building but also the coal, the water and the wavelength a firm uses. Its payment is rent. A fourth factor, entrepreneurship, earns profit and organises the other three, but it sits outside this unit’s three-factor list and its own factor market has no separate graph on this exam.
The hiring decision
Three things decide it: how productive the worker is, what the output sells for, and what the worker costs.In real lifeA café deciding whether to schedule a third barista weighs how many extra drinks that barista can make, what those drinks sell for, and the wage on the roster - all three, not one.Firms respond to factor prices - wages, interest and rent - and their decision to hire is based on three things together: the productivity of the factor, the output price it sells for, and the cost of the factor itself. A page that names only productivity has stated a third of the rule and read as though it finished the sentence. Drop any one of the three and the decision cannot be made: a highly productive worker making an unsellable product is not worth hiring at any wage, and a cheap worker making a valuable product is worth hiring at almost any productivity.
Labour demand (the curve)
Downward-sloping: firms want fewer workers as the wage rises.In real lifeA landscaping firm keeps a full crew at $18 an hour and trims it to a skeleton crew at $30, without a single job changing.In a given labour market, other things constant, the quantity of labour demanded is negatively related to the wage rate. Firms are on this curve, standing where the customer stood in a product market. It slopes down for the same reason every demand curve does and for a reason specific to factor markets: it is built from marginal product, which itself falls as more workers are added - see 5.3.
Labour supply (the curve)
Upward-sloping: more people are willing to do the job as the wage rises.In real lifeA hospital struggling to fill night shifts at $22 an hour finds the roster fills itself at $34.In a given labour market, other things constant, the quantity of labour supplied is positively related to the wage rate. Households are on this curve, standing where the firm stood in a product market. Giving up an hour of one’s own time is worth more the higher the pay for it, so a higher wage draws more people - and more hours from people already working - into this market.
Economic rent
The surplus a factor earns above the minimum it would have accepted.also called producer surplus in a factor market or payment above transfer earningsNot on the current CED’s list of Essential Knowledge statements, and it has been examined more than once. It costs one sentence to hold.In real lifeA footballer who would still play the game for a modest salary and is paid many times that. Almost all of the pay cheque is economic rent.The area above a factor’s supply curve and below the price it actually receives - producer surplus, applied to a factor market rather than a product market. It is not on the current CED’s list of Essential Knowledge statements, and it has been asked about more than once, so it costs one sentence to hold: it is who keeps the gains from a rising wage, and it belongs to whichever side of the market supplies the factor - workers in a labour market, landlords in a market for land.

FactorWhat it isPaymentWho typically supplies it

The line that separates themThe CED names exactly these three for this unit’s hiring decision, and each has its own factor market with its own price and its own payment name. A fourth factor, entrepreneurship, earns profit and organises the other three, but sits outside this three-factor list. Whichever factor a stem names, the same hiring logic applies: a firm buys it up to the point where the next unit’s marginal revenue product no longer exceeds its price.
Trap — which side of the market the firm is on In a product market the firm supplies and the household demands. In a factor market that reverses: the household supplies and the firm demands. Every consequence you already know keeps its shape and swaps its owner. A rise in the wage is good for the seller of labour and bad for the buyer, exactly as a rise in the price of bread is good for the baker; the surplus above the labour supply curve belongs to workers; and a “shortage of nurses” is a shortage in the ordinary Unit 2 sense — quantity demanded above quantity supplied at the going wage — not a statement about how many nurses exist.

Derived demand, and the two directions

Derived demand
Demand for an input that exists only because there is demand for what it makes.also called demand derived from the product market or indirect demandA stem almost never uses the phrase. It describes the chain instead - homes stop selling, so builders stop being hired - and asks what concept that illustrates.In real lifeNobody wants a crane operator for the sake of the operator. Builders want finished flats, and hiring the operator is a means to that end.Demand for a factor of production is derived from demand for the good or service that factor helps produce, rather than existing on its own. It follows that a factor market can never be read in isolation from the product market it feeds: a change in the price of the output, or in how much of it a worker can make, changes what the worker is worth to hire - even though nothing about the worker changed at all. This is the single premise the rest of the unit unwinds from.
Reading a chain backwards
Trace a factor-market question to the product market it started in.In real lifeA trade deal makes imported furniture cheaper. The question is about carpenters, but the story starts on the showroom floor.Because labour demand is derived, a stem that seems to be about a factor market is usually a product-market event in disguise. The reliable method is to find where the story actually starts - a price of the output, a change in technology, a new law - trace what that does to marginal revenue product, and only then read the factor-market curve. Starting from the factor market and guessing is how “the wage fell, so workers must have become lazier” gets written by a student who has the chain running the wrong way.
Labour demand and labour supply: whose curve is whose
SlopesWho stands on itWhy
Labour demand (the curve)DownFirmsMarginal revenue product falls as more workers are hired
Labour supply (the curve)UpHouseholdsGiving up an hour of one’s own time is worth more the higher the pay for it
The line that separates themThis is the Unit 2 market, with the seats swapped: firms buy here instead of selling, households sell here instead of buying. Every consequence keeps its shape and changes its owner - the surplus above labour supply belongs to workers, not to the firms as it would above a supply curve in a product market, and a “shortage of nurses” is the ordinary shortage of Unit 2, quantity demanded exceeding quantity supplied at the going wage, not a claim about how many nurses exist in the world.
Worked example — reading a chain backwards A country signs a trade deal and imported steel becomes much cheaper. What happens in the market for domestic steelworkers?
Start in the product market. Cheap imports mean domestic steel sells for less — the output price falls.
Carry it across. Each steelworker still makes the same number of tonnes, but each tonne is now worth less, so the revenue an extra worker brings in has fallen. Labour demand shifts left.
Read the new crossing. Wage down, employment down. Both, not one.
Notice that nothing happened to the workers. Their skill, their willingness to work and their number are all exactly as they were, and their wage fell anyway. That is what “derived” means, and it is the whole reason this concept has a name.

5.2 What moves labour demand

Two determinants do almost all the work here, and the CED names them outright: the output price and the productivity of the worker. Everything else on the list is one of those two arriving by a different route.

Determinants of labour demand
Whatever changes MRP at every quantity, other than the wage.In real lifeA furniture maker whose product suddenly sells for more wants more carpenters at every wage it could name - nothing about hiring got easier, but MRP rose.Changes in the determinants of labour demand - the CED names the output price and the productivity of the worker - cause the labour demand curve to shift. Every determinant on a longer list (new technology, the price of a substitute or complement input, the number of firms hiring) works by moving one of those same two numbers, because labour demand is MRP, and MRP is marginal product times marginal revenue.
A change in the output price
The same physical work is suddenly worth more, or less, to sell.In real lifeA rise in the world coffee price makes each picker’s basket worth more, and plantations want more pickers without a single tree growing faster.Raises marginal revenue at every quantity of the factor, which raises MRP at every quantity, which shifts labour demand right. A fall in the output price shifts it left. Nothing about productivity changed in either case - the same physical output is simply worth a different amount.
A change in productivity
The same worker suddenly makes more, or less, output.In real lifeNew scanning equipment lets a warehouse worker process twice the parcels per hour. The wage on offer elsewhere has not moved, but this firm now wants more of them.Better training, better machinery to work with, or new technology raises marginal product at every quantity of the factor, which raises MRP, which shifts labour demand right. Worse equipment, an ageing capital stock, or a workplace that gets in its own way shifts it left. This is the determinant that most exam distractors disguise as something else - a new machine story is a productivity story, however it is worded.
Substitutes in production
Two inputs that can each do the same job.In real lifeA self-checkout lane and a cashier. Either can ring up the groceries; a store rarely needs both for the same till.Inputs where either one can perform the task, so a firm can swap toward whichever is cheaper. A cheaper substitute for labour - a falling price of the robots that could replace assembly workers - shifts labour demand left: the firm buys more of the substitute and needs fewer workers. A dearer substitute shifts labour demand right, for the mirror reason.
Complements in production
Two inputs where each needs the other to be useful at all.In real lifeA delivery van and a driver. A van with nobody to drive it and a driver with no van are both useless to the courier firm.Inputs used together, so more of one raises the value of having the other. A cheaper complement - vans getting cheaper to buy - shifts labour demand for drivers right: more vans bought means more drivers wanted to drive them. This is the reversal students most often get backwards, because “cheaper input” sounds like it should always threaten workers, and here it does the opposite.
Quantity of labour demanded
What changes when the WAGE moves and nothing else does.In real lifeA restaurant cuts back its shifts the week the minimum wage rises. Its labour demand curve has not moved; it has slid up the one it already had.A change caused by the wage itself, read as a movement along a fixed labour demand curve rather than a shift of the curve. A stem describing a wage change and asking what happens to hiring is asking for this, and answering “labour demand fell” instead of “quantity of labour demanded fell” is graded as a different, wrong claim - it says the whole relationship between wage and hiring changed, and it did not.
Why exactly two things move this curve Labour demand is MRP, and MRP is marginal product times marginal revenue. A product of two numbers can only move if one of the numbers moves. Change what a worker physically produces and you have changed the first. Change what that output sells for and you have changed the second. That is the entire list, and every named determinant reduces to one of them: better training raises marginal product, better machines raise marginal product, a rise in demand for the good raises the price, a subsidy on the good raises the price the firm receives. Ask which of the two factors moved and the direction follows without any further reasoning.

The line that separates themLabour demand is MRP, and MRP is marginal product times marginal revenue - a product of two numbers, which can only move if one of the two numbers moves. Every determinant on any longer list, however it is worded, is one of those two arriving by a different route. Ask which of the two moved and the direction of the shift follows without further reasoning.

A shift, or a movement along

This is the same distinction Unit 2 draws, and it is worth more marks here than it was there, because in a factor market the price has a name of its own. If the wage changed, the firm moved along its labour demand curve and the answer is quantity of labour demanded. If anything else changed, the curve moved and the answer is labour demand.

A movement along labour demand, or a shift of it
What caused itWhat happened to the curveWhat the answer is called
Quantity of labour demandedThe wage changed, and only the wageNothing - the same curve, a different point on itQuantity of labour demanded
A shift of labour demandAnything else - output price, productivity, a substitute or complement inputThe whole curve moved, at every wageLabour demand
The line that separates themExactly the Unit 2 distinction, worth more marks here because the price has a name of its own. If the stem says the wage changed, the firm moved along a curve it already had. If it names anything else, the curve itself moved. Confusing the two is graded as answering a different claim, not as a rounding error on the right one.
Trap — substitutes and complements, in production Two inputs are substitutes in production if either can do the job: robots and assembly workers, self-checkout and cashiers. Two are complements in production if each needs the other: a crane and a crane operator, a delivery van and a driver. The direction reverses between them and stems test the reversal directly. A cheaper substitute input pushes labour demand left — the firm buys robots instead. A cheaper complement pushes labour demand right — more vans means more drivers. If you cannot tell which you are looking at, ask whether the firm could do the job with only one of the two.
Worked example — three changes, three answers A firm sells furniture in a perfectly competitive market and hires carpenters in a perfectly competitive labour market. Take each change on its own.
The market price of furniture rises. Every carpenter’s output is worth more, so MRP rises at every quantity: labour demand shifts right. Wage up, employment up.
The market wage for carpenters rises. Nothing about MRP changed. The firm slides up its existing curve and hires fewer: a movement along, not a shift.
A new jig lets each carpenter make half as many chairs again. Marginal product is up, so MRP is up at every quantity: labour demand shifts right again — from the other factor this time.
Three stems, and the only question each one is really asking is which number in MP × MR moved, or was it the wage.

5.2a What moves labour supply

The CED puts seven determinants of labour supply in a single parenthesis. A revision page that names two of them reads as though it finished the sentence, so all seven are below, with the two that get dropped named first.

Determinants of labour supply
Anything that changes how many people are willing to do THIS job, at the wage it already pays.In real lifeA city that opens a large nursing school changes nothing about nursing pay, and five years later far more people are willing to nurse at that pay.Changes in the determinants of labour supply - immigration, education, working conditions, age distribution, availability of alternative options, preferences for leisure, and cultural expectations - cause the labour supply curve to shift. Seven determinants named in one CED sentence, and a page that offers two of them reads as though it finished the list. The two most often dropped are immigration, because pages tend to start from the firm’s side of the market, and preferences for leisure, because it is the only one of the seven that is a choice rather than a condition someone is handed.
Immigration
New arrivals willing to work shift the whole labour supply curve right.In real lifeA region that opens its border to seasonal farm workers finds its harvest labour supply curve sitting well to the right of where it stood the year before.A rise in the number of people able and willing to work in a market - most directly through immigration - shifts labour supply right: more workers offered at every wage. A tightened border, the mirror event, shifts it left. It is one of the CED’s own named determinants and one of the two this unit flags as commonly skipped.
Preferences for leisure
The only labour-supply determinant that is a CHOICE rather than a condition.In real lifeA generation that comes to value a four-day week more than the one before it supplies fewer hours at every wage, with nothing about the job or the workers themselves having changed.A shift toward valuing leisure more, relative to income, shifts labour supply left at every wage - people who would have worked a given hour now choose not to. A shift toward valuing income more shifts it right. The CED lists preferences for leisure as one of seven determinants, and it is the one worth pausing on: every other item on the list describes something happening to a worker (a law, a demographic shift, a rival wage), while this one describes a worker deciding differently with nothing external having changed at all.
The wage in an alternative occupation
A rival job paying more pulls workers OUT of this market - the wage in THIS market did not move it.In real lifeNursing pay stands still while software salaries double. Fewer people train as nurses, and the nursing market’s own wage had nothing to do with it.One of the CED’s “availability of alternative options”: a rise in what a rival occupation pays draws people away, shifting this market’s labour supply left, and a fall in the rival wage shifts it right. The trap this term exists to name is confusing it with a movement: a change in this market’s own wage is never a supply shift, however large, because that is exactly what the supply curve already describes.

The line that separates themOne question sorts all seven: are there now more people willing to do THIS job at the wage it already pays? Whatever produced the answer - a border opening, a licence, a rival wage, a change of mind about leisure - the direction of the shift follows from that alone. What is not on this list, ever, is a change in this market’s own wage: that moves a point along the curve, never the curve itself.
The one question that sorts every labour-supply stem Are there now more people willing to do this job at the wage it already pays? More willing bodies is a rightward shift, whatever produced them: arrivals from abroad, a training programme that qualifies people, a rival occupation becoming worse paid, working conditions improving, a generation reaching working age, a culture that expects more of its adults to take paid work. Fewer willing bodies is leftward: retirement, a licence that disqualifies, a rival occupation paying better, the work becoming more dangerous, a stronger taste for time off. Notice that the last one is the only item on the list that is a choice rather than a condition, which is exactly why it is the one that gets left out.
Trap — a wage change is never a supply shift “Wages rose, so more people want the job, so supply increased” is wrong twice over in one sentence. A change in this market’s wage moves you along labour supply: quantity supplied, not supply. What shifts the curve is a change in the wage available somewhere else — the alternative option. If nursing pay is unchanged but software pay doubles, the supply of nurses shifts left, and the nursing wage then rises as a result of that shift rather than as its cause.
Worked example — both curves at once A country’s population ages. Demand for health care rises, and at the same time a large cohort of experienced nurses retires. What happens in the market for nurses?
Demand side. More patients means more health care sold, so each nurse’s work brings in more revenue: labour demand shifts right.
Supply side. Retirement removes workers: labour supply shifts left.
The wage. Both shifts push it the same way — up. The wage definitely rises.
Employment. The demand shift raises it, the supply shift lowers it, and nothing in the stem says which is bigger. The answer is indeterminate, and on the AP exam that is a real option rather than a cop-out. Write it, and say why: the two shifts work in opposite directions on this variable and the relative sizes are not given.

5.3 Marginal revenue product, and the hiring rule

One rule, stated by the CED twice — once as a stopping point and once as a licence to carry on. They are the same rule, and the page says so before the reader has time to look for the case where they disagree.

Marginal product (MP)
The extra physical output the next worker adds. Falls as more workers are hired.also called marginal physical product, MPP or marginal product of laborPhysical units - loaves, cars, haircuts. The moment it is multiplied by money it stops being marginal product and becomes MRP or VMP, and the exam separates the three on purpose.In real lifeThe fifth line cook in a small kitchen adds fewer extra meals per hour than the second one did - there is only one grill, and it is getting crowded.The change in total output from adding one more unit of the factor, holding every other input fixed. It rises briefly while workers can specialise, then falls - diminishing marginal returns begin at MP’s maximum, not where MP turns negative. Multiplying marginal product by money - by price, or by marginal revenue - is the entire content of this section, and it is worth keeping physical units (loaves, haircuts, tonnes) and dollar amounts visually distinct while learning it.
Marginal revenue product (MRP)
What the next worker adds to the firm’s revenue - the firm’s own labour demand curve.also called MRP, the firm’s demand curve for labor or marginal revenue product of laborThe second name is the one students never see coming: a factor-market stem that asks for “the firm’s labor demand curve” is asking for the MRP curve, and that is the whole question.In real lifeA sign shop’s third employee finishes four extra signs an hour, each selling for $15: that employee’s marginal revenue product is $60 an hour.The change in total revenue from hiring one more unit of a factor. Because it falls with every unit hired - marginal product falls, and for a price setter marginal revenue falls too - MRP is the firm’s labour demand curve, which is the fact behind one of this unit’s aliases for the term: a factor-market stem asking for “the firm’s demand curve for labor” wants the MRP curve, and answering with anything else has answered a different question.
Marginal factor cost (MFC)
What hiring one more unit of a factor costs the firm.also called marginal resource cost, MRC, MFC or the marginal cost of hiringOne line, four names, and the CED prints two of them in a single parenthesis. In a competitive labour market it is just the wage, which is why students forget it is a curve at all - and then cannot draw the monopsony.In real lifeA café that can hire a fourth barista at the going rate pays exactly that rate for the fourth one - nothing more, nothing hidden.The change in total cost from hiring one more unit of a factor. In a perfectly competitive labour market a firm is a wage taker: the labour supply curve it faces is horizontal at the market wage, so hiring one more worker costs exactly the wage and marginal factor (resource) cost and the wage are the same number. This is a special case, not the meaning of the term - Unit 5’s own monopsony topic is built entirely on the case where the two come apart.
The hiring rule (MRP = wage)
A wage-taking firm hires until MRP falls to the wage.In real lifeA landscaper keeps adding workers while each one still brings in more revenue than the day-rate costs, and stops on the worker where the two are level.In a perfectly competitive labour market, the wage is set by the market and each firm hires the quantity of workers where the marginal factor (resource) cost (the wage) equals the marginal revenue product of labor. The clause the CED adds and a page most easily drops: a firm can be a perfect competitor in the labor market even while it is an imperfect competitor in its output markets. Being a wage taker and being a price taker are two separate facts about one firm, and either can hold without the other - see 5.3a for exactly the case where they come apart.
On the exam

In the figure, a firm hires labor in a perfectly competitive labor market. Which curve gives the wage this firm pays, and why is it drawn flat?

  1. AThe MRP curve, because the wage is whatever the firm decides its workers are worth
  2. BThe labour supply curve, drawn flat because the firm is too small to move the market wage by hiring more or fewer workers
  3. CThe labour demand curve for the whole market, because one firm cannot be separated from the industry
  4. DThere is no single wage; each worker is paid a different amount depending on when they were hired
Answer

B. A firm that is one buyer among many faces a supply curve that is flat at the market wage - it can hire as many or as few workers as it likes at that one rate, exactly as a price-taking seller faces a flat demand curve. Because the curve is flat, the marginal cost of one more worker equals the wage itself, which is what lets the firm simply hire down its MRP curve until MRP meets that flat line.

The hiring rule (MRP > wage)
The same rule stated as a reason to keep going, not to stop.In real lifeA firm reasons worker by worker: “does the next one still bring in more than the wage?” - and hires as long as the answer is yes.A typical firm hires labor in a perfectly competitive labor market as long as the marginal revenue product of labor is greater than the market wage. This is not a second rule alongside “hire where MRP equals the wage” - it is the same rule seen while still climbing rather than at the point it stops. MRP falls with every worker hired, so the first worker not worth hiring is exactly the one whose MRP has fallen to the wage. Reading the CED’s two sentences as though they might disagree is the trap; reading them as one idea told twice is the fix.
Two sentences, one rule
Carry on whileMRP is greater than the wage
Stop whereMRP has fallen to the wage
The second is where the first runs out
A worker who brings in more than they cost is worth hiring, and MRP falls with every worker hired, so the first worker who is not worth hiring is the one whose MRP has dropped to the wage. There is no case where the two sentences disagree, and there is no third rule to learn. On the graph it is one point; in a table it is the last row where the MRP column is still at least the wage.
Two sentences, one hiring rule
CED statementSaysWhen it applies
The hiring rule (MRP = wage)Hire where MRP equals the wageThe stopping point
The hiring rule (MRP > wage)Hire as long as MRP exceeds the wageEvery worker before the last one
The line that separates themThese read as two rules on the CED’s topic page and they are one rule told from both ends. MRP falls with every worker added, so the last worker worth hiring - MRP still above the wage - is necessarily the one just before MRP has fallen to the wage. There is no case where a firm following one sentence and a firm following the other end up hiring a different number of workers.
Worked example — from a total-output table to a hiring decision A firm sells its output at $4 in a perfectly competitive market and hires workers at $40 a day. Labour is the only variable input.
1 worker → 20 units. Marginal product 20. MRP = 20 × $4 = $80. Above $40, so hire.
2 workers → 36 units. MP 16. MRP = $64. Hire.
3 workers → 48 units. MP 12. MRP = $48. Hire.
4 workers → 58 units. MP 10. MRP = $40. Exactly the wage — the last worker worth hiring.
5 workers → 64 units. MP 6. MRP = $24. Below $40. Stop.
The answer is 4 workers. Two habits come out of it. Always build the marginal column first by subtracting, because a table that gives you TOTAL output is trying to get you to multiply the wrong number. And compare the MRP column with the wage row by row rather than looking for a formula: the arithmetic is subtraction, one multiplication and a comparison, and that is all it ever is.
Trap — the wage does not tell you whether to hire “The wage is $40 and each worker produces 10 units, so the firm is fine” is not an argument — ten units of what, sold for how much? A hiring decision needs the output price as well as the physical product, and a stem that gives you productivity and a wage but no price has left a number out on purpose. The mirror error is comparing the wage with average product, or with total revenue. The rule is marginal against marginal, in dollars, both times.

5.3a Marginal revenue product against the value of the marginal product

This is the smallest distinction in the unit and the one that separates a mark from a near miss. Two labels, one number apart, and the CED spends a whole sentence on why.

Value of the marginal product (VMP)
What the next worker’s output is worth to BUYERS - marginal product times price.also called VMP or value of marginal product of laborEqual to MRP only when the firm sells its output in a perfectly competitive market. Treating the two as synonyms is safe on most questions and wrong on exactly the ones that test it.In real lifeA patented-parts workshop’s next unit sells for $90. What that unit is worth to the buyer is $90, whatever the workshop itself actually took home for making it.Marginal product multiplied by price, rather than by marginal revenue. It answers a different question from MRP: not what the firm gains from hiring, but what the good is worth to whoever eventually buys it. The two numbers are equal only when price and marginal revenue are equal - a perfectly competitive output market - which is exactly what the next entry states as CED content rather than as a coincidence.
MRP, VMP, and when they are equal
MRP = MP x MR always. MRP = VMP only when the firm is a price taker on its output.In real lifeTwo workshops with identically productive staff: the one that must cut its price to sell more has an MRP below its VMP, and hires fewer workers for it.Marginal revenue product of a factor is the change in total revenue divided by the change in that factor, which is also equal to the marginal physical product of the factor multiplied by marginal revenue (MRP = MP × MR). Firms in a perfectly competitive output market have marginal revenue product of labor equal to the value of the marginal product of labor (VMPL = MPL × P), because marginal revenue for each unit of output equals price there. On the majority of exam items the firm is such a price taker, so the two labels give the same number and either name will do - which is exactly what makes the tenth question, the one naming an imperfectly competitive seller, dangerous to a habit built on the other nine. The labour demand curve a firm hires from is always MRP, never VMP.
On the exam

A firm sells its output in an imperfectly competitive market. Compared with a perfectly competitive seller with an identical marginal product schedule, this firm’s MRP curve will lie:

  1. AAbove its VMP curve, because market power raises the price it charges
  2. BBelow its VMP curve, because its marginal revenue is below its price at every quantity
  3. CExactly on its VMP curve, since marginal product is identical for both firms
  4. DAbove its VMP curve only at the very first unit, then below it forever after
Answer

B. MRP is marginal product times marginal revenue, and VMP is marginal product times price. A price setter’s marginal revenue sits below its price at every positive quantity, so multiplying by the same marginal product carries that gap straight into the factor market: MRP lies below VMP everywhere except the very first unit, where nothing has been discounted yet and the two curves meet.

The employment effect of output-market power
A price setter hires fewer identical workers than a price taker would, at the same wage.In real lifeTwo equally skilled fruit pickers, paid the same daily rate, working for two farms - one that sells into an open market and one with an exclusive contract it must discount to fill. The second farm keeps a smaller crew.Because a price setter hires where its own MRP meets the wage, and that MRP lies below VMP, it stops hiring at a smaller quantity than a firm that could hire all the way to where VMP meets the wage. The gap between the two hiring quantities is a real cost of market power that shows up nowhere in the product-market graph - it is visible only by standing in the factor market and comparing the two curves, and it exists with no monopsony anywhere in the picture.

The line that separates themOn most exam items the firm sells in a perfectly competitive market, so marginal revenue equals price and the two numbers coincide - which is exactly what makes the distinction dangerous. A firm hires on what a worker adds to its own revenue, never on what the output is worth to somebody else, and the one exam question that separates the two labels is the one that names an imperfectly competitive seller.
Two independent questions about one firm Is it a price taker when it sells? Is it a price taker when it hires? These are separate conditions, and the CED says so outright: a typical firm may be a perfect competitor in the labour market even if it is an imperfect competitor in its output markets. So there are four cases, not two. A monopolist hiring in a town full of employers is a wage taker with MRP below VMP. Trap — when the two labels really are interchangeable On the majority of exam questions the firm sells in a perfectly competitive market, so marginal revenue equals price, so MRP and VMP are the same number and either name will do. That is exactly what makes the distinction dangerous: a habit that works on nine questions out of ten is a habit, and the tenth question is the one that names an imperfectly competitive seller and asks which curve is the labour demand curve. The answer is always MRP — a firm hires on what a worker adds to its revenue, never on what the output is worth to somebody else.
Worked example — the same worker, two firms Two firms each hire a worker whose marginal product is 10 units. Both pay the market wage of $50.
Firm A sells in a perfectly competitive market at $6 a unit. Its marginal revenue is $6, so MRP = 10 × $6 = $60. That is also its VMP. $60 beats $50, so hire.
Firm B is a price setter whose price is also $6, but whose marginal revenue at its current output is $4. Its VMP is still $60 — the output is worth just as much to buyers — but its MRP is 10 × $4 = $40. $40 is under $50, so do not hire.
Same worker, same productivity, same wage, same product price, opposite decisions. Everything turned on the one number the two firms do not share.

5.3b The least-cost rule: choosing between inputs

Every hiring rule so far has asked whether to buy one more of something. This one asks a different question: given that you are buying two things, are you buying them in the right proportions? It is examined more often than any other single idea in the unit.

Least-cost rule
Buy inputs so that a dollar spent on any one of them buys the same extra output.also called the cost-minimizing rule, equal marginal product per dollar or the equimarginal rule for inputsThe third name is the useful one: the rule says each dollar has to buy the same amount of extra output whichever input you spend it on.In real lifeA print shop buying both paper-folding time and delivery-van time gets the same extra jobs finished per dollar from either - if it does not, it is spending some dollars in the wrong place.To minimize costs or maximize profits, firms allocate inputs such that the last dollar spent on each input yields the same amount of marginal product - MPL÷PL = MPK÷PK. If the two ratios differ, moving a dollar from the low-ratio input to the high-ratio one buys more output for the same money, and the very act of moving it closes the gap: buying more of an input lowers its marginal product, buying less of the other raises that one, until the ratios meet. It answers how cheaply a given output is made, not how much to make - that second question is the profit-maximizing rule, MRP = P for every input, and the two rules answer different questions even though they look alike.
Profit-maximizing input rule
Hire every input up to the point where it pays for itself: MRP = its price.In real lifeA firm keeps adding both machines and workers as long as each still brings in more than it costs, stopping each input at its own break-even point.The multi-input version of the hiring rule from 5.3: hire labour until MRPL = PL (the wage) and capital until MRPK = PK (the rental price). It implies the least-cost rule - divide both equalities through and both ratios equal one, so they equal each other - but the implication runs only one way. A firm can satisfy the least-cost rule while producing the wrong total quantity, which is why “minimising cost” and “maximising profit” are different instructions in a stem even though a firm doing the second is always also doing the first.
The rule, in the form that actually gets used MPL ÷ PL = MPK ÷ PK Divide each input’s marginal product by its price and you get the extra output that input gives you per dollar spent. If the two are not equal, the firm is buying the wrong mix: a dollar moved from the low-ratio input to the high-ratio one buys more output for the same money. So it moves dollars across, and the moving itself closes the gap — buying more of an input lowers its marginal product, buying less of the other raises that one — until the two ratios meet. When they meet there is no dollar left worth moving, and the mix is least-cost.

The line that separates themThe implication runs one way only. Profit maximisation implies cost minimisation - divide both of its equalities through and the two ratios both equal one, so they equal each other - but a firm can satisfy the least-cost rule while producing an output that makes no profit at all. If a stem says “minimising cost”, use the ratios. If it says “maximising profit”, either rule will do, and the ratios are usually the quicker arithmetic.
Worked example — which way should the dollars move? A firm uses labour and capital. Right now: MP of labour = 30 units, wage = $3. MP of capital = 60 units, rental price = $12.
Labour: 30 ÷ 3 = 10 units per dollar.
Capital: 60 ÷ 12 = 5 units per dollar.
Labour is buying twice as much output per dollar, so the firm should hire more labour and use less capital.
Watch what happens as it does. More labour drives labour’s marginal product down; less capital drives capital’s marginal product up. The 10 falls, the 5 rises, and the firm stops when they meet. Note the answer is not “use more capital because its marginal product is higher” — 60 beats 30 and capital is still the wrong buy, because it costs four times as much. That comparison is the trap the numbers are built around.
Trap — two rules that look alike and answer different questions MPL/PL = MPK/PK is cost minimisation: it says you are producing whatever you are producing as cheaply as possible. It does not say you are producing the right amount, and a firm can satisfy it while making a loss.
MRPL = PL and MRPK = PK is profit maximisation: each input is hired up to the point where it pays for itself. Profit maximisation implies cost minimisation — divide both equations through and the ratios are both equal to 1 — but the implication does not run backwards. If a stem says “minimising cost”, use the ratios. If it says “maximising profit”, you may use either, and the ratios are usually quicker.

5.4 Monopsony: when the buyer sets the wage

Everything so far assumed the firm could hire one more worker without moving the wage. Take that assumption away and one line on the graph splits into two — and the wage stops being what the last worker costs.

Monopsony
A factor market with a single buyer, who must raise the wage to hire more.also called a single-buyer market, a wage setter or buyer’s market powerThe mirror of monopoly, and the fourth item on Unit 4’s list of imperfectly competitive markets - the only one that is about a buyer.In real lifeThe one mine in a small town. There is nowhere else to dig for a living, so the mine posts a wage instead of reading one off a market.The fourth imperfectly competitive market structure, and the only one on the buying side. A monopsonist faces the market labour supply curve rather than a flat one, because it is the only buyer there is - so hiring one more worker means raising the wage, and raising it for everyone already hired, since a firm cannot legally pay the newest worker more than the ones already on the payroll and pay the rest less. That single fact is why marginal factor cost separates from the wage here, having coincided with it everywhere else in the unit.
Monopsony hiring rule
Hire while MRP exceeds MFC - the same rule as 5.3, with MFC now above the wage.In real lifeA sole employer keeps adding workers while each one is still worth more than the RAISE that worker triggers for the whole crew, and stops exactly there.In a monopsonistic labor market, a typical firm hires additional labor as long as the marginal revenue product is greater than the marginal factor (resource) cost - the wage of a new unit of labor plus the wage increase given to all existing labor. Nothing about the hiring rule changed from 5.3; what changed is what marginal factor cost is. Skip the parenthesis and the statement reads as though it merely restates the competitive rule, and the entire mechanism - and the entire reason this firm hires fewer workers than a competitive one would - goes missing.

B. Work in total wage bills, never in the posted wage. Three workers cost $90 in total; four cost $140. The fourth worker’s marginal factor cost is the difference, $50 - not the $35 that worker is personally paid. The extra $15 is the $5 raise the firm must give to each of the three workers already on the payroll, which is the whole reason MFC lies above the wage in this market.

Supply price of labor
The height of the labour SUPPLY curve - what the monopsony wage is actually read off.also called the height of the labour supply curve or the wage workers will acceptThe CED’s own phrase, and the one that makes the monopsony graph readable: find the quantity on MFC, then read the wage DOWN on supply.In real lifeAt the quantity a mine settles on, the workers themselves are willing to work for less than what hiring the last of them truly cost the mine - the gap between those two numbers is the whole complaint against monopsony.When a typical firm hires additional workers in a monopsonistic labor market, the marginal factor (resource) cost is greater than the supply price of labor - the CED’s own name for the height of the labour supply curve at that quantity, and the number workers are actually willing to accept. It is what makes the monopsony graph readable: find the hiring quantity where MRP crosses MFC, then read the wage straight down onto supply, never onto MFC. Reading the wage off MFC instead is the single most common wrong answer on this figure in every released paper.
On the exam

In the figure, a monopsonist has found the quantity of labor where MRP crosses MFC. To find the wage it actually pays, a student should:

  1. ARead straight across from that same crossing point to the wage axis
  2. BDrop down to that quantity, then go up only as far as the labour supply curve and read across
  3. CDrop down to that quantity, then go up only as far as the labour demand curve and read across
  4. DAdd the wage increase given to existing workers to the height of the MRP curve
Answer

B. MRP crossing MFC gives the quantity of labor and nothing else - that crossing sits above the labour supply curve, not on it. The wage a monopsonist actually pays is what workers are willing to accept at that quantity, which is the supply price of labor: the height of the supply curve, found by moving down to it from the chosen quantity. Reading the wage off the MRP/MFC crossing itself is the examined error, because it reports a number that is not a wage at all.

How to read the monopsony graph, in order
  1. Find where MRP crosses MFC. Drop straight down. That is the quantity of labour, and it is the only thing this crossing tells you.
  2. From that same quantity, go up only as far as the labour supply curve. Read across to the wage axis. That is the wage.
  3. The competitive benchmark, if the question wants one, is where MRP crosses supply — further right and higher. The monopsonist hires fewer workers and pays a lower wage than a competitive market would, on the same two curves.
  4. Notice what is true at the monopsonist’s own optimum: the wage is below MRP. Workers are paid less than the last one of them brings in, and the gap is the whole complaint against this market structure.

The line that separates themOne fact does all the work: whether the firm is one buyer among many, so the supply curve it faces is flat, or the only buyer there is, so the supply curve it faces is the whole market’s upward-sloping one. A flat supply curve makes MFC coincide with the wage; an upward-sloping one forces MFC above it, because hiring one more worker means a raise for everyone already on the payroll. Both firms use the identical hiring rule, MRP against MFC - only what MFC is has changed.
Trap — the two heights at one quantity At the quantity a monopsonist hires there are three different dollar amounts stacked on one vertical line: the wage down on supply, the marginal factor cost up on MFC, and the marginal revenue product where MRP crosses MFC. The exam picks two of them and asks which is which. Hold one sentence and all three sort themselves out: MFC is what the next worker costs the firm; the wage is what the worker gets. They are different numbers here because hiring the next worker also costs a raise for everybody already there — and that raise is a cost to the firm that is not part of the new worker’s own wage.
Worked example — why marginal factor cost outruns the wage A firm is the only employer in town. To get 3 workers it must offer $30 each; to get a 4th it must offer $35 — and it has to pay $35 to all four, because it cannot pay the new arrival more than the people already doing the job.
Total wage bill at 3 workers: 3 × $30 = $90.
Total wage bill at 4 workers: 4 × $35 = $140.
Marginal factor cost of the fourth worker: $140 − $90 = $50.
The fourth worker is paid $35 and costs $50. The extra $15 is the $5 raise, taken on the three who were already there. That is why MFC lies above supply, why the gap widens as the firm grows, and why a monopsonist stops hiring sooner than the wage alone would suggest — it is comparing MRP with $50, not with $35.
Where the monopsony rule and the competitive rule are the same rule Both say: hire while marginal revenue product exceeds marginal factor cost. Nothing about the hiring rule changed between 5.3 and 5.4. What changed is what marginal factor cost is. For a wage taker the labour supply curve it faces is flat, so the cost of one more worker is exactly the wage and the two coincide — which is why the competitive graph seems to have no MFC curve on it. For a single buyer the supply curve slopes up, so the two separate. Learn the rule in the general form and the competitive case is the special case, rather than the other way round.

The card to memorize

The rules

  • Marginal product: change in total output ÷ change in the input
  • MRP = MP × MR. In a competitive output market MR = P, so MRP = VMP = MP × P
  • Hire while MRP > MFC; stop where MRP = MFC
  • Competitive labour market: MFC = the wage, and the firm’s labour supply curve is flat
  • Monopsony: MFC lies above supply. Quantity off MFC, wage off SUPPLY
  • Least-cost mix: MPL/PL = MPK/PK
  • Labour demand shifts when output price or productivity changes
  • Labour supply shifts when the number of willing workers changes — never when this market’s own wage changes

The traps, in one list

  • Labour demand is derived: start in the product market, then carry it across
  • Build the MARGINAL column first; a total-output table is a trap
  • Productivity without an output price is not enough to decide anything
  • MRP is what the firm gets; VMP is what buyers get. Hire on MRP
  • Higher marginal product does not mean better buy — divide by the price
  • Cost minimising is not profit maximising; the implication runs one way
  • Never read a monopsony wage off the MFC curve
  • A wage change moves you ALONG a curve, on both sides of the market
  • Two shifts at once can leave one variable indeterminate, and that is an answer
  • Perfect competition in the labour market and in the output market are separate facts
If you can do these seven things, you are ready for Unit 5
  1. Explain why a labour demand curve slopes downward without saying anything about workers’ willingness to work.
  2. Turn a table of workers and total output into an MRP column and pick the hiring quantity.
  3. Take any change described in a stem and say whether it shifts labour demand, shifts labour supply, or moves along one — and in which direction.
  4. Say when MRP and VMP are the same number and when they are not, and give the reason in terms of marginal revenue.
  5. Use the least-cost rule to say which input a firm should buy more of, and explain why the higher marginal product is not the answer.
  6. Draw a labelled monopsony graph and mark the quantity, the wage, the competitive quantity and the competitive wage.
  7. State the hiring rule in the one form that covers both market structures, and say what changes between them.
