1.1 Scarcity, resources and opportunity cost

Every idea in this unit descends from one fact: there is not enough to go around.

The chain the whole course hangs on Scarcity → choice → opportunity cost. Resources are limited and wants are not, so something must be given up. That sacrifice is the cost economists care about — not the price tag.
Scarcity
Wants are unlimited; the means of meeting them are not.In real lifeA hospital has twelve intensive-care beds and, on a bad night, twenty patients who need one. The beds are scarce whatever they cost to build.Less of something is available than people want when it is free. Scarcity is about the gap between availability and desire, not about rarity alone: a thing nobody wants is not scarce however little of it exists. It is the condition that makes economics a subject — if nothing were scarce, nothing would have to be chosen.
Trade-off
Everything you give up when you choose.In real lifeTaking a weekend shift gives up the football match, the lie-in and the family lunch all at once. Those are the trade-offs.The alternatives forgone by taking one course of action. A choice usually has many trade-offs, and they exist only because resources are limited. Opportunity cost is the most valuable single one of them, which is why the two words are not interchangeable.
Opportunity cost
The next best thing given up.In real lifeA student who skips a paid shift to study forgoes about $60 of wages. That, not the $0 the library charges, is what the evening cost.The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made — one alternative, the best of the rejected ones, never the sum of all of them. It counts implicit as well as explicit costs, so it is larger than the price tag whenever a choice uses up something you already owned, such as your own time or a building you could have rented out.
On the exam

A student has one free evening. She could work a shift paying $80, see a film she values at $30, or study, which she values at $50. If she studies, her opportunity cost is

  1. A$130, the value of both rejected alternatives
  2. B$80, the value of the best rejected alternative
  3. C$30, the least valuable alternative
  4. D$160, everything on offer
Answer

B. Opportunity cost is the single next-best alternative given up. The film is also forgone, but it is not added on: only the best of the rejected options counts.

Sunk cost
Already spent, already gone.In real lifeA gym membership paid in January is gone whether you go in March or not, so “I already paid for it” is not a reason to go.Money or time paid out that cannot be recovered whichever option is now chosen. Because it is identical across the alternatives, it is not part of any opportunity cost and a rational agent ignores it. The exam tests this by putting a large, memorable past payment in the stem.
On the exam

A firm paid $2 million last year for a factory site; the payment cannot be recovered. In deciding now whether to build the factory, a rational manager should treat the $2 million as

  1. Apart of the opportunity cost of building
  2. Ba sunk cost that has no bearing on the decision
  3. Ca fixed benefit of building
  4. Dan implicit cost of not building
Answer

B. The $2 million is gone whether or not the factory is built. It is identical across the alternatives, so it cannot be part of the opportunity cost of either.

Marginal
“One more.”In real lifeA bakery deciding whether to bake a 51st loaf asks what that one loaf adds in sales and costs in flour, not what the first fifty did.The change caused by one additional unit, as opposed to the total. Every decision rule in this course is marginal: it compares what one more unit adds with what that one more unit costs, and ignores what the earlier units did.

The line that separates themCount and timing. Trade-offs are plural and future; opportunity cost is the single largest of them; a sunk cost is past, so it is the same under every option and cancels out. The exam sets this by naming a big, memorable amount already spent.

Two ways of slicing the subject

Neither pair is graphed or calculated. Both are asked about in a sentence, and both are free marks.

Economics
The study of how people choose under scarcity.In real lifeChoosing between an hour of sleep and an hour of revision is an economic decision, though no money changes hands.How individuals, firms and governments allocate limited resources among unlimited wants, and what follows from those choices. The subject is defined by the problem it studies, not by money: anything scarce — time, attention, clean air — is economics.
Microeconomics
The decisions of single households, firms and markets.In real lifeWhy one coffee shop raised its latte price by 40 cents, and how many customers it lost, is a micro question.The branch that studies individual decision-makers and one market at a time: what a consumer buys, what a firm produces, how one price is set. This whole course is micro, and this unit supplies the decision rules the rest of it applies.
Macroeconomics
The economy as a single whole.In real lifeWhether a whole country’s unemployment rose last quarter, and what its central bank does about it, is macro.The branch that studies aggregates — national output, the price level, unemployment, growth. Unit 1 is the one unit the two courses share, which is why a Unit 1 question can be phrased in national terms and still be a micro question.
Positive economics
Statements that evidence could settle.In real life“Raising the minimum wage to $15 would cut teenage employment by 2%” can be checked against data, and be right or wrong.Claims about what is, framed so that data could confirm or refute them — “the higher minimum wage reduced teenage employment in this state.” A positive statement can be false; what makes it positive is that being false would show.
On the exam

Which of the following is a positive economic statement?

  1. ARent control should be abolished
  2. BRent control reduces the quantity of rental housing supplied
  3. CLandlords deserve to charge higher rents
  4. DCities ought to build more public housing
Answer

B. A positive statement can be tested against evidence, right or wrong. The other three contain should, deserve and ought: judgments of value that no data can settle.

Normative economics
Statements about what ought to be done.In real life“The minimum wage should be high enough to live on” states a value. No dataset can settle it.Claims resting on a value judgement — “the minimum wage should be raised.” No amount of data settles one, because the disagreement is about the goal rather than the facts. Words like should, fair, too high and better are the giveaway.
The two branches

The line that separates themThe size of the thing being studied, not the size of the topic. Unit 1 belongs to both courses, so a Unit 1 question can be phrased about a whole country and still be micro.

The four factors of production

Factors of production
The four inputs every good is made from.also called resources, economic resources, productive resources or inputsIn real lifeA pizzeria uses an oven and a shopfront (capital), cooks (labor), the tomatoes and the plot the building stands on (land), and an owner who risked her savings to open it (entrepreneurship).Land, labor, capital and entrepreneurship — the resources combined to produce any good or service. Each earns its own payment, and those four payments are where household income comes from. Money is not among them: it buys resources, it does not produce anything. Neither is time.
On the exam

A construction firm lists four things it needs before work can start. Which one is NOT a factor of production?

  1. AA bulldozer owned by a construction firm
  2. BThe $500,000 loan the firm took out to buy the bulldozer
  3. CThe operator’s training
  4. DThe gravel pit the firm quarries
Answer

B. Money buys factors of production; it produces nothing itself. The bulldozer is capital, the training is human capital, and the pit is land.

Landpaid in rent
Whatever nature supplied.also called natural resourcesIn real lifeThe plot under a wind farm, the wind that turns it, and the iron ore the towers were made from are all land.Everything drawn from the earth rather than produced by people — soil, water, timber, oil, minerals, and the site itself.
Laborpaid in wages
Human effort, physical and mental.also called human resourcesIn real lifeA nurse’s shift, a coder’s afternoon, a bricklayer’s morning: the human effort that goes into output.The work people do to produce goods and services, from an assembly line to an operating theatre. It is the effort itself, not the worker’s training — that is human capital.
Capitalpaid in interest
Produced tools used to make other things.also called physical capital or real capitalTextbooks split it into physical and human capital; the exam usually just writes capital.In real lifeA delivery van, a warehouse and the forklift inside it are capital. The money that bought them is not.Goods that were themselves produced and are now used in production rather than consumed — machines, ovens, delivery vans, factory buildings. Being produced is what separates capital from land. In ordinary speech “capital” means money; on this exam it never does.
Physical capital
The equipment — capital you could photograph.In real lifeA dentist’s chair and X-ray machine are physical capital. The degree on the wall is not.The machines, tools, vehicles and buildings a firm produces with. It is what “capital” means on its own in this course, and it sits outside the worker: it can be bought, sold, or worn out independently of whoever operates it.
Human capital
The skill and knowledge inside the worker.In real lifeA welder’s certification raises what an hour of that welder’s labor produces. The course that earned it was investment in human capital.Education, training, experience and health — whatever makes an hour of the same worker’s labor produce more. It is not a fifth factor; it raises the productivity of labor, and investing in it shifts the PPC out for the same reason new machines do. A question naming a machine and a training course in one sentence is testing physical capital against human capital.
Entrepreneurshippaid in profit
The risk-taking that combines the other three.also called entrepreneurial abilityIn real lifeSomeone who notices that a town has no late-night pharmacy, borrows to open one, and eats the loss if it fails.The willingness and ability to organise land, labor and capital into a going concern, bear the uncertainty, and absorb the loss if it fails. Its payment is profit — a residual rather than a contracted rate, which is precisely why it is the payment for bearing risk.
Non-rival resources
Not every resource is scarce.In real lifeOne person streaming a song does not use it up for anyone else. The same file plays for millions at once.A resource is non-rival when one person’s use of it takes nothing away from anyone else. Established knowledge — a proved theorem, a published technique — is the CED’s own example: teaching it to one more person consumes none of it, so it is not scarce. The CED is careful here and you should be too: most factors of production are scarce, but some may not be scarce at all. “Every resource is scarce” is the wrong answer.

The line that separates themTwo questions separate all four. Was it produced? Land no, capital yes. Is the payment agreed in advance? Labor yes, a wage; entrepreneurship no, whatever is left over — which is exactly why profit is the payment for bearing risk.
The two kinds of capital
What it isExample in a stemHow it raises output

The line that separates themWhere it lives. Physical capital is outside the worker and can be sold; human capital is inside the worker and cannot. A stem that names a machine and a training course in one sentence is testing precisely this, and both shift the PPC out.
How the exam tests the four It rarely asks you to list them. It puts money, or time, or a training course beside a machine into the stem and asks which factor is which. Read the four definitions above with those three stems in mind and the question answers itself.
Worked example — what counts in opportunity cost Jalen paid $150 for a concert ticket months ago. Tonight he could resell it for $200. He goes, and spends $50 on food inside.
Opportunity cost = $200 + $50 = $250. The $200 is the resale he gave up; the $50 could have bought other things. The original $150 is sunk — it is spent whether he attends or not, so it never enters the calculation.

1.2 Resource allocation and economic systems

Scarcity forces every society — whatever its politics — to answer the same three questions.

The three economic questionsalso called the fundamental economic questions or the basic economic questions
Whatshould be produced?
Howshould it be produced?
For whomis it produced?
Every society answers all three, because every society faces scarcity
Three questions of equal weight, and none of them optional. What differs between a market, a command and a mixed economy is only the mechanism doing the answering.
Resource allocation
Deciding which uses the scarce resources go to.In real lifeDeciding whether a stretch of riverbank becomes a park, a housing estate or a factory is allocating a resource.The process by which an economy settles what its limited resources will produce and who ends up with the output. Scarcity makes some allocating mechanism unavoidable; the economic system is the choice of which mechanism.
Economic system
The machinery a society uses to answer the three questions.In real lifeWhether a city’s bread comes from thousands of competing bakeries or from a ministry’s production plan is its economic system at work.The institutional arrangements — property rules, prices or plans, who may own what — that coordinate allocation. Command, market and mixed are the three the CED names. Each faces the same three questions and the same scarcity; only the answering mechanism differs.
Market economy
Prices decide, through buyers and sellers acting for themselves.also called free-market economy or free-enterprise economy“Capitalism” is used loosely for the same thing, but it names who owns the capital rather than what allocates the resources.In real lifeNobody plans how many phone cases a city needs. Sellers make more of the ones that sell and drop the ones that do not.Resources are privately owned and allocated by voluntary exchange. The price does two jobs at once: it informs, because a high price says buyers value this good, and it motivates, because that same price is the profit available for supplying it. Resources move toward profit, so they move toward what people value most.
Command economy
Central planners decide.also called centrally planned economy or planned economyIn real lifeIn the Soviet Union a central plan set how many tractors each factory built and where the steel for them was sent.The state owns the productive resources and officials set what is made, in what quantity, and often at what price and wage. Planning changes who decides; it does not abolish scarcity, and the three questions still have to be answered.
Mixed economy
Mostly prices, with the government intervening.In real lifeThe United States lets private firms build cars but runs public schools and sets speed limits. Every real economy mixes the two.Private ownership and market exchange, plus taxation, regulation, public goods and transfers. Essentially every real country is mixed; pure market and pure command are the ends of a scale, useful for comparison rather than as descriptions of anywhere.
Property rights
The enforceable right to own, use and sell a thing.In real lifeA farmer who knows the land is hers plants an orchard that pays off in ten years. A squatter who might be evicted plants a crop that pays off in three months.Clear and enforced property rights are what make voluntary exchange possible and investment worth making, because the owner keeps the gains. Which rights exist, and who holds them, is the sharpest difference between a market system and a command one.
The three economic systems
SystemWho decidesProperty rightsCoordinating mechanism
Market economyBuyers and sellers, acting for themselvesPrivate, enforcedPrices
Command economyCentral planners in governmentState-heldThe plan
Mixed economyMostly prices, with government interveningPrivate, regulatedPrices, plus rules and taxes
The line that separates themWhat coordinates the resources. Not whether the three questions get answered — every system answers all three — and not whether scarcity exists, because it exists under all of them. Only the mechanism differs.
Trap A stem describing a shortage under central planning is not evidence that planning failed — a market economy has shortages too. Nothing in this topic distinguishes the systems by whether scarcity exists. They differ only in who answers the three questions.

1.3 The production possibilities curve

Production possibilities curve (PPC)
Every combination of two goods an economy can just manage.also called production possibilities frontier (PPF), production possibilities boundary or transformation curvePPF is the name most college textbooks use, and it turns up constantly in practice material. Same curve, same rules.In real lifeA workshop with five carpenters can build 20 chairs a day, or 10 tables, or some mix. The curve is every mix it can manage flat out.A model showing the maximum combinations of two goods obtainable from a fixed stock of resources with today’s technology. It puts scarcity, opportunity cost, efficiency and growth into one picture: the curve is the limit, its slope is the cost, points inside it are waste, and the whole curve moving is growth.
Productive efficiency
No output is being wasted — true anywhere on the curve.also called production efficiencyNot the same as allocative efficiency, which asks whether the right combination is being made, not whether output is being wasted.In real lifeA restaurant kitchen where every burner is lit and every cook is busy: no more of one dish can come out without less of another.Producing at the lowest possible cost with every resource in use, so that more of one good can be had only by giving up some of the other. It answers how to produce. Every point on the PPC is productively efficient, which is why “on the curve” alone does not say the economy is making the right things.
Allocative efficiency
Producing the combination people actually want — one point only.In real lifeA stadium that sells out every seat is productively efficient. It is allocatively efficient only if fans wanted the concert it booked rather than the match it turned down.Making the particular mix of goods society values most, so that no reallocation would make anyone better off. It answers what to produce. Only one point on a PPC is allocatively efficient, while every point on it is productively efficient — the gap between the two words is examined directly.
On the exam

In the figure, the economy is producing at Y. The combination people actually want is Z. At Y the economy is

  1. AEfficient in both senses at once
  2. BProductively efficient, but not making the mix people want
  3. CAllocatively efficient, but wasting resources
  4. DEfficient in neither sense
Answer

B. On the curve means no resources are wasted: productively efficient. Allocative efficiency is the one point on the curve people actually want, and this is not it.

Underutilization
Attainable but wasteful — a point inside the curve.also called underutilization of resources or unemployed resourcesIn real lifeA factory running one shift when it was built for three, or a recession in which a tenth of the workforce is idle.Resources sitting idle or badly matched to their use, so the economy produces less of both goods than it could. Unemployment and recession are the usual causes. Putting those resources back to work moves the point out to the curve; it does not move the curve.
Economic growth
The whole curve moves out.In real lifeA country that builds a second port, trains more engineers or adopts faster looms can now make more of everything.A rise in what the economy is capable of producing, caused by more resources, better resources or better technology — not by using existing resources more fully. Growth shifts the PPC outward, and a loss of resources or productivity shifts it inward. A technology that helps only one good pivots the curve, moving that intercept and leaving the other exactly where it was.
On the exam

Which of the following could cause the shift from PPC₁ to PPC₂ shown in the figure?

  1. AIdle factories being brought back into use
  2. BThe discovery of large new oil reserves
  3. CProducing at a combination the economy could already have reached
  4. DSpecialising and trading with another country
Answer

B. New resources move the curve. Falling unemployment and a move onto the curve move the point, not the curve; trade moves consumption beyond the curve, which stays where it is.

Capital goods
Goods made to make other goods.also called investment goods or producer goodsIn real lifeA tractor, a server rack, a cement mixer: bought not to enjoy but to produce something else.Machinery, tools, factories, infrastructure. Choosing them over consumer goods means less consumption now in exchange for greater productive capacity later — and the curve shifts out when that capital is built and working, not at the moment the choice is made.
Consumer goods
Goods bought to be used up.In real lifeA pizza, a phone, a haircut: bought to be used and enjoyed, not to make anything further.Food, clothing, haircuts — output that satisfies a want directly rather than producing something else. On a capital-against-consumer PPC, an economy sitting high on the consumer axis is living well today and growing slowly.
Three points, three verdicts
Where the point isAttainable?Efficient?What is going on
On the curveYesYesEvery resource in use; more of one good costs some of the other
Inside the curveYesNoResources idle or misallocated — unemployment, recession
Outside the curveNon/aBeyond what today’s resources and technology allow
The line that separates themAttainability and efficiency are two separate questions, and the middle row is the one students collapse: a point inside the curve is perfectly attainable, it is just wasteful. Only the outside point is impossible.
The two efficiencies

The line that separates themWaste against wants. Productive efficiency asks whether anything is being wasted; allocative efficiency asks whether the right combination is being made. An economy can be productively efficient and allocatively hopeless — on the curve, building tanks nobody wants.
The two kinds of good on a PPC

The line that separates themWhen the benefit arrives. Two economies on identical curves today are on different curves in twenty years purely because of where on this axis they chose to sit — and the shift comes later, not at the moment of choosing.

The shape carries a claim

The CED names three cost patterns, and the curve looks different under each. Which one a question has given you is read off the schedule, not assumed.

Increasing opportunity cost
Each extra unit costs more than the last — the curve bows outward.also called the law of increasing opportunity cost or the law of increasing costsIn real lifeTurning a wheat farm over to grapes uses the best vineyard slopes first. The next hundred vines go on land that grew wheat well and grows grapes badly.Resources are not equally suited to both goods, so the ones moved first are the ones least useful where they were and the ones moved last are the most useful. The PPC is concave to the origin. This is the normal case, and the one to draw unless a question says otherwise.
On the exam

The production possibilities curve in the figure is bowed outward from the origin. This shape shows that

  1. Aopportunity cost is constant along the curve
  2. Bresources are equally well suited to producing both goods
  3. Copportunity cost increases as more of either good is produced
  4. Dthe economy is experiencing economic growth
Answer

C. The bow means each extra unit of one good costs more of the other than the last, because the resources moved over are progressively less suited to it. A straight line would show constant cost.

Constant opportunity cost
Every extra unit costs the same — the curve is a straight line.In real lifeA print shop that switches a press between flyers and posters in a minute gives up the same number of flyers for every extra poster, first to last.Resources are perfectly adaptable between the two goods, so nothing about them prefers one product. The slope, and therefore the cost, is identical everywhere along the line. Draw this only when a question tells you to assume it, or when a schedule’s differences are constant.
Decreasing opportunity cost
Each extra unit costs less than the last — the curve bows inward.In real lifeRare outside textbooks. The nearest real case is a firm that gets better at a second product the more of it it makes, and the exam does not draw it.The rare third case the CED names alongside the other two: the curve is convex to the origin, so expanding one good gets cheaper as you go. You meet it as a schedule to read rather than a graph to draw, and recognising which of the three shapes a table implies is the actual skill being tested.
The three cost patterns, and the curve each one draws
Shape of the PPCAs you move rightWhen to draw it
Increasing opportunity costBowed outward (concave)Steepens — each unit costs moreBy default, unless told otherwise
Constant opportunity costA straight lineSlope never changesWhen a question says to assume it
Decreasing opportunity costBowed inward (convex)Flattens — each unit costs lessRarely drawn; read it off a schedule
The line that separates themWhat happens to the sacrifice as you keep going. Read it off a table by taking the differences between neighbouring rows: growing means increasing, equal means constant, shrinking means decreasing. The shape follows from the numbers; it is not a stylistic choice.

What moves the curve, and what does not

  • Shifts out: more resources, better resources, better technology. More capital, a larger or better-trained labor force, a new resource discovery.
  • Shifts in: fewer or worse resources. A war, a natural disaster, a falling literacy rate.
  • Pivots: a technology that improves only one good. That good’s intercept moves out; the other intercept does not move at all.
  • Does NOT shift it: a change in demand, a change in unemployment, or moving from inside the curve onto it. Those change the point chosen, not what is possible.
Capital goods today buy the curve of tomorrow Choosing capital over consumption trades output now for a bigger curve later. Producing capital goods instead of consumer goods means less consumption now in exchange for greater productive capacity afterwards. The curve shifts out later, when that capital is built and working — not at the moment the choice is made. Two countries on identical curves today will be on different curves in twenty years for this reason alone.

Capital goods02468

Moving B to D: capital rises 2 → 6, consumer falls 48 → 36. Cost = 12 consumer goods. Only the sacrifice counts — never the sacrifice plus the gain.

Drawing the PPC on the free-response section, step by step

The same figure, built in four moves. Every point on the rubric is a label: the goods on the axes, the numbers at the intercepts, the name on each curve.

  • Label both axes with the actual goods named in the question, not “X” and “Y.”
  • Let the curve touch both axes. A PPC floating above its intercepts claims the economy cannot devote everything to one good, which nobody claims.
  • Label the original curve PPC₁ and any new curve PPC₂.
  • Label any point the question asks you to identify, and label the intercept values when the question gives numbers.
  • Bow the curve unless the question says constant opportunity cost — then draw a straight line.
  • For a one-good improvement, move one intercept and leave the other exactly where it was.
Where points are lost An unlabeled axis earns no point, however correct the drawing. Graders read labels first.

1.4 Comparative advantage, specialization and trade

Absolute advantage
Producing more with the same resources.In real lifeA large hospital with more surgeons does more operations of every kind in a week than a rural clinic. It has the absolute advantage in all of them.The ability to produce a greater quantity of a good than another producer from the same quantity of resources — or the same quantity from fewer. It compares raw productivity, and it does not determine who should specialise. One party can hold it in both goods and both parties still gain from trade.
Comparative advantage
Producing at a lower opportunity cost.In real lifeA lawyer who types faster than her assistant still hands over the typing, because an hour of her typing costs a $300 hour of legal work.The ability to produce a good while giving up less of the other good than the other producer would. Nobody holds it in both goods: a lower cost in one is arithmetically a higher cost in the other, because it is a ratio. This, not absolute advantage, decides who specialises in what.
On the exam
CarsTrucks
First country4080
Second country1040

The table shows the most each country can produce in a day if it makes only that good. Which statement is correct?

  1. AThe first country has the comparative advantage in both goods
  2. BThe second country has the absolute advantage in trucks
  3. CThe first country has the comparative advantage in cars and the second in trucks
  4. DThere are no gains from trade because one country is better at everything
Answer

C. A car costs the first country 2 trucks and the second 4, so the first has the lower cost in cars. A truck costs the first country 0.5 car and the second 0.25, so the second has the lower cost in trucks. Absolute advantage in both does not prevent gains.

Specialization
Each producer makes the good it gives up least to make.In real lifeA dairy region that stops growing its own wheat, puts every field to pasture, and buys flour from the plains.Concentrating production where you hold the comparative advantage and obtaining the rest by exchange. It raises total output from unchanged resources, which is where the gains from trade come from — no new worker, machine or field is involved.
Gains from trade
Both sides consume more than either could produce alone.In real lifeAfter the two regions swap milk for flour, each ends up with more of both than when each tried to make both itself.The extra consumption specialisation and exchange make possible. Both parties gain whenever their opportunity costs differ. If the two ratios are identical there are no gains at any rate, however different the producers’ sizes.
Terms of trade
The rate at which the two goods are swapped.also called the trading ratio or the rate of exchangeIn real lifeThe plains send a tonne of flour for every 600 litres of milk. That rate is the terms of trade, and both sides had to agree to it.Mutually beneficial only when it lies between the two opportunity costs: the exporter must receive more than its own cost of making the good, and the importer must pay less than its own. Outside that band one side would rather produce the good itself.

Consumption possibilities
What you can consume — which trade pushes beyond what you can produce.In real lifeA country that makes only phones but eats rice every day: what it consumes is what it made plus what it traded for.With specialisation and trade a country consumes at a point outside its own PPC. Production still happens on the curve, and the curve itself has not moved. A question that describes trade and then asks about the PPC is asking you to leave the curve exactly where it is.
On the exam

1.5 Cost-benefit analysis

The whole topic is one comparison: what does one more unit add, and what does it cost?

Cost-benefit analysis
Compare what a decision yields with what it takes.also called marginal analysisBoth appear on the exam. Marginal analysis is the wider name for comparing what one more unit adds with what it costs.In real lifeWhether to replace a boiler this year: the fuel it will save, against the price of the new unit and the disruption of fitting it.Total net benefit — total benefit minus total cost — is largest at the optimal choice, and that quantity is what the analysis is looking for. “Benefits exceed costs” is not the answer on its own; plenty of quantities clear that bar and only one maximises the difference.
Rational agent
Someone who weighs costs against benefits and takes the best option available.In real lifeA commuter who takes the bus when the fare is below fuel and parking, and drives when it is not.The behavioural assumption under every model in the course: a consumer maximises utility, a firm maximises profit, and both count opportunity costs, implicit as well as explicit, when working out what a decision truly costs. Rational does not mean selfish or well informed — it means consistent with the objective given.
Explicit cost
Money actually paid out.In real lifeThe rent, the electricity bill and the wages a café pays out each month.An out-of-pocket payment to somebody outside the decision-maker — rent, wages, materials. These are the costs an accountant records, and they are only part of the economic cost.
Implicit cost
The value of what you already owned and used up anyway.In real lifeThe salary the café owner gave up by leaving her job, and the rent she could have charged by letting the shop to someone else.The opportunity cost of resources the decision-maker already holds — the salary given up to run your own shop, the rent not collected on the building you occupy. No money changes hands, which is exactly why these are the ones a question hides. Economic cost is explicit plus implicit.
Total net benefit
Total benefit minus total cost — the thing being maximised.In real lifeA festival brings a town $2 million in spending and costs it $1.4 million in policing and clean-up. Net benefit: $600,000.The surplus a decision leaves behind. It rises while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost and falls once marginal cost exceeds marginal benefit, so it peaks exactly where the two are equal. That is why the marginal rule and the totals never disagree. Note the wording: it is total benefit minus total cost that is largest at the optimum. Total benefit on its own is still climbing there, and does not peak until marginal benefit reaches zero — well past the point worth stopping at.
Total revenue
What a firm takes in — the firm’s version of utility.In real lifeA food truck sells 200 burritos at $9. Total revenue is $1,800, before a single tortilla is paid for.Price times quantity sold. Cost-benefit analysis needs a measure of total benefit, and the two sides of the market measure it differently: for a consumer that measure is utility, and for a firm it is total revenue. The CED pairs them in one sentence, so a question can hand you either and expect the same reasoning.
Decisions that cannot be taken at the margin
Some choices have no “one more” — judge those on totals.In real lifeWhether to build a bridge at all. There is no half-bridge to price at the margin, so the whole benefit is set against the whole cost.Marginal analysis needs an activity that comes in increments: one more mile, one more hour, one more slice. Plenty of decisions do not. Build the bridge or do not build it; take the job or turn it down. There is no “one more bridge” to price, so the comparison has to be total benefit against total cost, and you go ahead when the total benefit is at least as large. Use the marginal rule to find a quantity; use totals for a decision that is yes or no. Picking the wrong tool is what the CED is warning about in CBA-1.B.2, and it is the one place where “decide at the margin” is the wrong instinct.
Fixed benefit
A benefit already locked in by a past choice — the mirror of a sunk cost.In real lifeA commuter has to reach the office either way. The only question is which route or ticket gets her there at the least cost.A payoff you receive whichever way you now choose, because an earlier decision already settled it. Like a sunk cost it is identical across the alternatives, so like a sunk cost it drops out of the comparison: the optimal quantity depends on neither. Sunk costs get all the attention and fixed benefits are the half students forget, which is exactly why CBA-2.B.2 names both.
Marginal benefit (MB)
What one more unit adds.In real lifeThe extra sales one more hour of opening brings a shop, or the extra satisfaction from one more slice of pizza.The change in total benefit from a one-unit increase. It normally falls as quantity rises, because each further unit meets a less pressing want than the one before. Handed a table of totals, subtract down the column to get it.
On the exam

For a firm, the fifth unit of output has a marginal benefit of $50 and a marginal cost of $45; the sixth unit has a marginal benefit of $40 and a marginal cost of $55. The firm should produce

  1. Asix units, since total benefit still exceeds total cost
  2. Bfive units, since the sixth would cost more than it adds
  3. Cas many units as possible
  4. Dfour units, to be safe
Answer

B. Produce while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost and stop where the next unit would reverse that. The fifth unit adds $5 net; the sixth would subtract $15, whatever the totals say.

Marginal cost (MC)
What one more unit costs.In real lifeThe extra wages and lighting that one more hour of opening costs, or the extra flour in one more loaf.The change in total cost from a one-unit increase. It normally rises as quantity rises, because the cheapest and best-suited resources get used first. It is compared with marginal benefit, never with total benefit.
The two halves of economic cost

The line that separates themWhether anyone was paid. Economic cost is explicit plus implicit, and the implicit half is the one a question hides — precisely because nothing was paid, there is no number in the stem to notice. Find what the decision-maker already owned and used up.
Two tools, and picking the wrong one is the error

The line that separates themWhether the activity comes in increments. If there is a “one more” to price, decide at the margin; if there is not — build the bridge or do not — totals are the only tool there is. This is the one place where “always decide at the margin” is wrong advice.
The marginal decision rule Do it while MB > MC. Stop where MB = MC. The rule points three ways, and a question can hand you a quantity that is already too high:
  • MB > MC — increase. The next unit is worth more than it costs.
  • MB < MC — decrease. You are past the optimum; cutting back raises net benefit.
  • MB = MC — maintain. This is the optimal quantity.
Past the optimum each extra unit costs more than it is worth and subtracts from net benefit — even while total benefit still exceeds total cost.

Almost every question in this topic hands you totals. Your first move is always the same: subtract down each column to turn them into marginals, then apply the rule.

But do not carry that over as a rule about every decision. When there is no “one more” to price — build the trail or do not build it — totals are the only tool there is, and the definition above says when each applies.

1.6 Marginal analysis and consumer choice

Utility
Satisfaction, treated as a quantity.also called satisfactionIn real lifeThe pleasure of a hot meal after a cold day, or of a seat on a crowded train.The benefit a consumer gets from consuming a good, counted in invented units called utils so that it can be compared and added. Utility is the consumer’s version of total benefit in cost-benefit analysis; total revenue is the firm’s.
Util
The made-up unit utility is counted in.In real lifeRating that meal a 9 and that seat a 4 is measuring in utils: made-up units that let the two be compared.An arbitrary unit with no meaning between people — your 40 utils and somebody else’s 40 utils are not comparable. Within one consumer’s table they work perfectly, which is all any question needs.
Total utility (TU)
Satisfaction from everything consumed so far.In real lifeThe whole satisfaction from an evening of three slices of pizza, counted together.The running sum of the marginal utilities. It rises while MU is positive, peaks exactly where MU crosses zero, and falls once MU is negative. Consumers are assumed to be maximising this, subject to their budget.
Marginal utility (MU)
The satisfaction added by one more unit.In real lifeHow much the third slice added to the evening, on its own.The change in total utility divided by the change in quantity. It is the height of the step, not the height of the staircase — the examined error is putting total utility’s peak where marginal utility peaks, rather than where marginal utility reaches zero.
Law of diminishing marginal utility
Each extra unit adds less than the one before.also called the principle of diminishing marginal utilityDo not confuse it with the law of diminishing marginal RETURNS, which is about output per extra worker in Unit 3. The curves look alike; the subjects are different.In real lifeThe first glass of water after a run is wonderful. The fourth is a chore.Past some point, further units of the same good in the same sitting yield smaller and smaller increments of satisfaction. Total utility is still rising through all of it — only the additions are shrinking. It is also why a demand curve slopes down: a consumer pays less for a unit that is worth less.
On the exam

In the figure, between the first slice and Qsat the marginal utility of each slice falls but remains positive. Over that range, total utility is

  1. Afalling
  2. Brising, but at a decreasing rate
  3. Cconstant
  4. Dat its maximum
Answer

B. Each slice still adds something, so the total keeps rising. The additions are shrinking, so it rises more slowly. Total utility peaks only where marginal utility reaches zero.

Budget constraint
You cannot spend more than you have.In real life$20 at the cinema buys a ticket and popcorn, or a ticket and two drinks, but not all three.The limit that makes consumer choice a problem at all. With unlimited income there would be nothing to solve: you would take every good to the point where its marginal utility hit zero. The rule below exists because a budget forces a comparison between goods.
Marginal utility per dollar
MU divided by price — the only fair way to compare two goods.In real lifeA $2 coffee that gives 10 utils beats a $5 sandwich that gives 20: five utils per dollar against four.What the last dollar spent on a good buys in satisfaction. Comparing MU per unit is the commonest error in this topic: 25 utils for $5 is worse value than 10 utils for $1. Divide by price before you compare, always.
On the exam

A consumer has spent her whole budget. The last dollar spent on good X yields 8 utils and the last dollar spent on good Y yields 12 utils. To maximise total utility she should

  1. Abuy more X and less Y
  2. Bbuy more Y and less X
  3. Cchange nothing, because the budget is exhausted
  4. Dbuy equal quantities of X and Y
Answer

B. Moving a dollar from X to Y loses 8 utils and gains about 12, so total utility rises. Keep moving until the utils per dollar are equal. Spending the whole budget is necessary but not sufficient.

Not on the exam — the CED says so Indifference curves are outside the course and outside the AP Exam. They are how most college textbooks and a good deal of practice material present consumer choice, so you will meet them; you do not have to learn them. What is in scope is the rule directly above — equating the ratios of marginal utility to price. Time spent on indifference curves and budget lines for this exam is time spent for nothing.
Trap — the half of the rule people drop The rule has two conditions, and a bundle can satisfy the ratios and still be wrong. Equal utils per dollar with money left over is not the optimum: spending that money on either good adds utility, so you were not maximising. The CED says consumers allocate their limited income to the combination that maximises utility — the whole income. Check the arithmetic and check the budget is exhausted.

The card to memorize

Formulas

  • Opportunity cost, output problem: other good’s output ÷ this good’s output
  • Opportunity cost, input problem: this good’s hours ÷ other good’s hours
  • Marginal anything: change in total ÷ change in quantity
  • Optimal quantity: MB = MC
  • Optimal bundle: MUX/PX = MUY/PY, budget spent
  • Terms of trade: strictly between the two opportunity costs

The traps, in one list

  • Money and time are not factors of production
  • Sunk costs never enter opportunity cost
  • Opportunity cost is the sacrifice only, not sacrifice plus gain
  • Comparative, not absolute, advantage drives specialization
  • Trade expands consumption, not production possibilities
  • Falling unemployment moves the point, not the curve
  • Diminishing MU still means total utility is rising
  • Compare utility per dollar, never per unit
