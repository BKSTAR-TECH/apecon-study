2.1 The circular flow and GDP

One number, three ways of arriving at it, and a diagram that shows why all three have to agree. This is the unit’s foundation topic, and its arithmetic reappears in every section that follows.

MEA-1.A.1, and the word that carries it GDP is a measure of final output of the economy. Drop “final” and GDP becomes the sum of every transaction in the economy, which counts the same value again every time it changes hands on the way to becoming something a household actually buys. The value-added approach below exists specifically to rule that out.
Gross domestic product (GDP)also called national output, aggregate output or the size of the economyA news report saying “the economy grew 3% last quarter” is reporting real GDP growth without using either word.
The market value of everything final produced inside a country in a year.In real lifeA government statistics office publishing a single number each quarter that is meant to stand for the whole economy’s output.A measure of final output of the economy: the market value, in a country’s own currency, of every final good and service produced within its borders in a given period. Final is the whole statement. Drop it and GDP becomes the sum of every transaction in the economy, counting the same value repeatedly as it passes from supplier to supplier - which is exactly the double-counting the value-added approach below exists to rule out.
On the exam

A furniture factory buys $200 of lumber and sells the finished tables it makes for $500. Which of the following amounts is counted directly in GDP?

  1. A$700, the lumber and the tables added together
  2. B$500, the value of the finished tables
  3. C$200, the value of the lumber
  4. D$300, the difference between the two prices, counted as the factory’s cost
Answer

B. GDP counts FINAL output. The $500 table already has the $200 of lumber priced into it, so adding the lumber again would count the same wood twice. $300 is the factory’s value added, a true number, but it is not what GDP records for a final good - GDP records the $500 sale price once.

The circular flow diagram
One picture of GDP as a loop of income and spending between households and firms.In real lifeA paycheck a household spends at a shop, which becomes the wage the shop pays its own staff, who spend it somewhere else - the same money, moving in a ring.GDP as a total flow of income and expenditure can be represented by the circular flow diagram: households supply the factors of production to firms and receive wages, rent, interest and profit in return; firms supply goods and services to households and receive consumption spending in return. The two loops carry the same total, measured going opposite directions - which is why the expenditure approach and the income approach below have to agree.
The three ways of measuring GDP
Expenditure, income, and value added - three routes to the same number.In real lifeAuditing a shop three ways at once: adding up every receipt it took in, adding up every wage and profit it paid out, and adding up what it added at each stage of making its product. All three totals should match.There are three ways of measuring GDP: the expenditures approach (add up spending on final goods and services), the income approach (add up the income the process generates - wages, rent, interest, profit, plus depreciation and indirect business taxes), and the value-added approach (add up what every firm in a chain of production contributed, rather than what every firm sold). A page that draws only the first has covered a third of the statement while reading as if it covered the lot.
Three routes to one number

The line that separates themAll three have to land on the same number, because they are the same circular flow measured at three different points. Reporting only the expenditures side and calling it complete leaves the other two-thirds of the CED statement untested by the reader.

What counts as a good, and what a firm actually added

Two ideas the expenditures approach quietly assumes, and the ones the value-added approach makes explicit.

A final good or service
Bought by its last user; nothing is done to it before it is used.In real lifeThe loaf of bread on a kitchen counter, as opposed to the flour, the oven time, and the delivery truck that got it there.A good or service in the form its final buyer consumes it. Its price already contains the value of every ingredient and process that went into it, which is why final goods are counted once and intermediate goods are not counted at all.
An intermediate good
Bought by another firm to make something else, not consumed as is.In real lifeSteel sold to a car plant. Nobody drives away in a sheet of steel; it becomes part of the car, and the car’s price is where that steel’s value is counted.A good used up in producing another good, rather than sold to its final user. An intermediate good’s value never enters GDP on its own - it is already inside the price of whatever final good it became part of. The same physical good can be either: steel sold to a car plant is intermediate, and steel sold to a household for a garden gate is final.
Value added
What a firm sold minus what it bought in from other firms.In real lifeA bakery that buys $2 of flour and sells the loaf for $5 has added $3 - the part that is the bakery’s own contribution, not the miller’s.The difference between a firm’s sales and the cost of the intermediate goods it bought from other firms. Summing every firm’s SALES in a production chain double-counts, because each firm’s inputs were already sold once by the firm before it; summing every firm’s value added does not, and the two totals converge on exactly the final sale price.
A transfer payment
Government hands over money and receives no good or service back.In real lifeA retiree’s monthly pension check, or a payment to someone between jobs. The government is not buying anything with that money.A payment for which no good or service is exchanged in return - a pension, an unemployment benefit, a subsidy check. Transfer payments are government SPENDING but not government PURCHASES, so they never appear inside the G term of the expenditures approach. What the recipient later buys with the money is counted, as ordinary consumption.
Net exports
Exports minus imports - the trade term in GDP, and it can be negative.In real lifeA country that ships $90 billion of goods abroad and buys $130 billion from abroad has net exports of −$40 billion, and that negative number is subtracted from the rest of GDP.Exports (X) minus imports (M), the fourth term of the expenditures approach. Imports are subtracted because C, I and G already include everything households, firms and government bought - including goods made abroad - and none of that foreign output belongs inside a measure of domestic production. When imports exceed exports, C + I + G can add up to MORE than GDP itself, which is arithmetic rather than a paradox.
A final good against an intermediate good
Sold toCounted in GDP directly?
A final good or serviceThe last buyer, who consumes itYes — its price already contains every input that made it
An intermediate goodAnother firm, to make something elseNo — its value is already inside the final good’s price
The line that separates themThe SAME physical good can be either one, depending on who buys it. Steel sold to a car plant is intermediate; steel sold to a household for a garden gate is final. GDP asks who the buyer is, not what the good is.
Worked example — the income approach, checked against the expenditures approach An economy’s households and firms report: wages $400, rent $30, interest $50, profit $90, depreciation $20, indirect business taxes $10.
Income approach total: 400 + 30 + 50 + 90 + 20 + 10 = $600.
The same economy’s expenditures are C = $420, I = $110, G = $80, X = $40, M = $50.
Expenditures approach total: 420 + 110 + 80 + (40 − 50) = 420 + 110 + 80 − 10 = $600.
The check. Both routes land on $600, because they are the same circular flow measured going opposite directions. A discrepancy is a sign of an arithmetic slip, not a sign that the two approaches disagree in principle.

2.2 The limits of GDP as a measure

GDP answers one question - how much final output did the economy produce - and it is worth being precise about everything that question does not ask.

Nonmarket transactions
Production that happens outside any market, so no price is ever recorded.In real lifeA parent who cooks, cleans and cares for children at home. The same work done by a paid nanny and a paid cleaner shows up in GDP; done unpaid, at home, it does not.GDP is a useful indicator of a nation’s economic performance, but it has some limitations, such as failing to account for nonmarket transactions - production and exchange that never passes through a priced market, so it leaves no transaction for the statistics office to record. Unpaid housework, childcare, volunteer labor and subsistence farming are all real output that GDP is structurally unable to see.
On the exam

A household stops paying a gardener and begins mowing its own lawn instead, with no other change in the economy. What happens to measured GDP?

  1. AIt rises, because the lawn is still being mowed
  2. BIt falls, because a market transaction has become a nonmarket one
  3. CIt is unchanged, because the same physical work is happening either way
  4. DIt cannot be determined without knowing the household’s income
Answer

B. GDP only records transactions that pass through a market and get a price attached. The gardener’s fee no longer exists, so GDP falls, even though exactly the same lawn gets mowed - which is the textbook illustration of GDP undercounting nonmarket production.

The underground economy
Real market activity, deliberately kept off the books.In real lifeA cash-only tradesperson who does not report the job, or an unlicensed street vendor. Money changed hands and a service was produced; no receipt exists.Market transactions - both legal (undeclared cash income, unlicensed trade) and illegal (drug sales, smuggling) - that go unreported and so are never captured in official statistics. Unlike nonmarket transactions, this activity DOES have a market price; it is simply hidden from the accountants, which makes it a different limitation from the one MEA-1.B.1 names, even though both understate GDP.
What a GDP figure does not say
A bigger number is not automatically a better standard of living.In real lifeA country that clears forests for quick timber sales posts a burst of GDP growth the same year its air and water quality get measurably worse. The bill for that damage never appears as a negative entry.GDP prices what gets bought and sold; it has nothing to say about leisure time, how evenly income is distributed, or environmental damage done in the process of producing output. Two countries with identical GDP per person can differ enormously in how many hours their workers spend earning it, how unequal their incomes are, and how much pollution their production leaves behind - none of which shows up in the number itself.
Trap — the fastest way to raise measured GDP without raising output Move an activity from unpaid or unreported to paid and reported, and GDP rises even though nothing real changed. A household that starts paying a cleaner it used to do without has, on paper, grown the economy. The reverse is just as real: a recession that pushes work into informal cash arrangements can understate how much the economy actually shrank.
Two reasons GDP misses real production
Does a market transaction happen?Is it reported to the statistics office?Effect on measured GDP
Nonmarket transactionsNo — no price is ever setNothing to reportUnderstated — real output, invisible to the count
The underground economyYes — money changes hands at a real priceNo — deliberately kept off the booksUnderstated — real output, hidden from the count
The line that separates themNonmarket production was never priced at all; underground production WAS priced, and simply hidden. Both leave GDP too low, but a policy that formalizes informal work (bringing cash jobs onto the books) raises GDP without raising output, while a policy that pays for what used to be unpaid (hiring a nanny) does the same thing for a different reason.

What a big GDP number does not tell you

Two economies can report identical GDP per person and differ enormously in how life inside them actually feels.

Three questions GDP cannot answer
How is it shared?the same average income with a very different spread
At what cost?environmental damage never appears as a subtraction
At whose expense?longer hours worked for the same output is not a gain
None of these make GDP the wrong number to track. They make it a number that answers one specific question and is silent on several others a policymaker still has to ask.

2.3 The labor force, unemployment, and participation

Two rates, and the whole topic turns on their two different denominators. Get the denominator right and the arithmetic is one division; get it wrong and every answer that follows is wrong in the same way.

The labor force
Everyone working, plus everyone out of work but actually looking.In real lifeA city’s labor force is its working adults minus its retirees, students and stay-at-home parents who are not job-hunting, plus every one of the unemployed who is.The employed plus the unemployed - adults who are either working or actively looking for work. It excludes retirees, full-time students not seeking work, people caring for family full time, and anyone who has stopped looking. It is the denominator of the unemployment rate and the numerator of the labor force participation rate, and confusing it with the adult population is the single most common arithmetic error in this topic.
Employed
Working for pay, for any number of hours, including one.In real lifeA student working four hours a week at a cafe counts as fully employed in the official statistics, exactly the same as someone working sixty.Anyone who did any paid work at all in the reference period, or who has a job but was temporarily away from it (illness, vacation, a labor dispute). There is no minimum-hours test, which is the reason the unemployment rate can look healthy in an economy full of people working far fewer hours than they want.
Unemployed
Without a job, but available and actively looking for one.In real lifeA laid-off worker sending out applications every week. A laid-off worker who has stopped applying and is not currently looking is a different category entirely.Someone without a job who is currently available for work and has actively searched for a job in the recent past. All three conditions matter: a person with no job who is not looking is not unemployed in the official sense, and neither is someone unavailable to start (a full-time student not job-hunting, for instance).
Not in the labor force
Neither working nor looking - retirees, students, and everyone who has stopped searching.In real lifeA retiree, a full-time student who is not job-hunting, and a parent who has chosen to stay home are all outside the labor force by definition, not by some failure of the count.Adults who are neither employed nor counted as unemployed: they are not working and are not currently seeking work. This category holds every discouraged worker, which is what makes it the place the unemployment rate’s critics point to.
The unemployment rate
The share of the labor force that is out of work.In real lifeA town of 10,000 workers where 400 are actively job-hunting and cannot find one reports an unemployment rate of 4% - out of the labor force, not out of the town.The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is out of work. The denominator is the LABOR FORCE, not the adult population and not the total headcount of unemployed people alone - a rate needs two numbers, and the second one is where the marks are lost.
On the exam

A country has 800,000 employed people, 50,000 unemployed people, and 200,000 adults who are neither employed nor looking for work. What is the unemployment rate?

  1. A5.0%
  2. B5.9%
  3. C6.25%
  4. D9.1%
Answer

B. Labor force = 800,000 + 50,000 = 850,000. Unemployment rate = 50,000 ÷ 850,000 ≈ 5.9%. 5.0% divides by the wrong labor force figure, and 6.25% and 9.1% both mistakenly bring the 200,000 outside the labor force into a denominator.

The labor force participation ratealso called LFPR or the participation rateThe bare phrase “participation rate” in a stem is always this rate; there is no other participation rate on this course.
The share of the adult population that is in the labor force.In real lifeA country where 63 out of every 100 working-age adults are either working or job-hunting has a labor force participation rate of 63%, whatever its unemployment rate happens to be that year.The labor force participation rate is another measure of the labor market activity in an economy. The labor force participation rate is the percentage of the adult population that is in the labor force. Its denominator is the ADULT POPULATION - everyone of working age, whether or not they work or look for work - which is the one difference that keeps this rate from being the unemployment rate’s mirror image.
On the exam

An economy’s adult population is 500,000. Of these, 260,000 are employed and 40,000 are unemployed. What is the labor force participation rate?

  1. A52%
  2. B55%
  3. C60%
  4. D65%
Answer

C. Labor force = 260,000 + 40,000 = 300,000. Participation rate = 300,000 ÷ 500,000 = 60%. The other options divide by the wrong total or add the wrong numbers together.

Why the measured rate understates joblessness
Discouraged workers vanish from the count; part-time workers are counted as fully employed.In real lifeA laid-off machinist who gives up the job search after six months disappears from the unemployment rate entirely - not because a job was found, but because the search stopped. A cashier who wants forty hours and gets twelve counts as employed either way.The measured unemployment rate is often criticized for understating the level of joblessness because it excludes groups such as discouraged workers and part-time workers. A discouraged worker has stopped looking for work and so has left the labor force - the denominator narrows along with the numerator, and the rate can fall even though nobody found a job. A part-time worker who wants full-time hours is counted as fully employed, with no distinction drawn for the hours they could not get.
On the exam

An unemployed worker gives up searching for a job and stops looking altogether, with no change to anyone else in the economy. What happens to the official unemployment rate?

  1. AIt rises, because there is still one more person without a job
  2. BIt falls, because that person leaves the labor force and is no longer counted as unemployed
  3. CIt is unchanged, because the person still has no job
  4. DIt cannot be determined without knowing the person’s age
Answer

B. A discouraged worker leaves the labor force entirely, so the same person disappears from both the numerator (unemployed) and the denominator (labor force). The rate falls even though nobody found work - which is exactly why the official rate is criticized for making a weak labor market look better than it is.

A discouraged worker
Wants a job, has given up looking, and so counts as neither employed nor unemployed.In real lifeA worker who applied for a hundred jobs over a year with no offers, and simply stops applying. Nothing about that decision creates a new job; it only removes the person from the count.Someone who wants work, is available for it, but has stopped actively searching because they believe no job is available to them. They leave the labor force altogether rather than remaining counted as unemployed, which is the mechanism behind the official rate’s best-known blind spot.
An underemployed worker
Has a job, but fewer hours or lower skill use than they want.In real lifeAn engineer working part-time hours at a cafe because no engineering position is open. Officially employed; nowhere near their full economic contribution.A part-time worker seeking full-time hours, or a worker whose job uses far less of their skill than they have. The official statistics count both as simply employed, which is the other half of MEA-1.D.1’s criticism: understating not the number without work, but the shortfall in the work that is happening.

Moving a person from the second row to the third - a discouraged worker giving up the search - takes them OUT of the denominator as well as the numerator, which is the whole of the next comparison.

Worked example — both rates from one set of numbers An economy has an adult population of 500,000. Of these, 260,000 are employed and 40,000 are unemployed.
Labor force = 260,000 + 40,000 = 300,000.
Unemployment rate = 40,000 ÷ 300,000 ≈ 13.3%.
Labor force participation rate = 300,000 ÷ 500,000 = 60%.
The check. The two rates use the SAME labor-force figure of 300,000 in two different roles: numerator for one rate, denominator for the other. If your two answers used two different labor-force numbers, one of them is wrong.

2.3a Types of unemployment and the natural rate

Three types of unemployment, one of which the business cycle actually moves, and a benchmark rate that a student who reads “full employment” as zero unemployment will get wrong for the rest of the course.

Three types of unemployment
Cyclical, frictional, and structural - one for each of three different reasons a person has no job.In real lifeThree unemployed workers in the same city: one laid off when the local factory cut output in a downturn, one between jobs while a background check clears, and one whose factory-floor skills a robot now performs. Three different causes, three different types.Economists primarily focus on three types of unemployment: cyclical, frictional, and structural. Cyclical tracks the business cycle and disappears when the economy recovers; frictional is the ordinary, temporary process of matching workers to jobs; structural is a lasting mismatch between the skills workers have and the skills employers want. Naming the type is naming the fix - a downturn calls for stimulus, a skills mismatch does not.
Frictional unemployment
Short-term, from the normal process of matching workers to jobs.In real lifeA graduate who takes six weeks to find a first job, or an employee who quits on Friday knowing a better offer starts in a fortnight.Unemployment arising from the time it takes to search, apply, and be hired - workers between jobs, new entrants to the labor market, and anyone voluntarily searching for a better match. It is not a sign of anything wrong with the economy; it is a normal, ongoing feature of one, which is why it never falls to zero and is one of the two components of the natural rate.
Structural unemployment
Lasting, from a mismatch between the skills workers have and the jobs available.In real lifeA textile mill town where the mill closes for good and moves overseas. The workers’ skills do not transfer to the software jobs replacing it, so the mismatch can persist for years.Unemployment arising when workers’ skills, location, or industry no longer match what employers need - typically caused by technological change or a shift in which industries an economy specializes in. Unlike frictional unemployment, it does not resolve itself quickly through ordinary search; it usually needs retraining or relocation, which is the other component of the natural rate.
Cyclical unemployment
The part of unemployment caused by the business cycle itself.In real lifeThe wave of layoffs that hits construction and manufacturing hardest in a recession, and reverses as the economy recovers - not because those workers changed skills, but because the whole economy contracted.The deviation of the actual unemployment rate from the natural rate is cyclical unemployment. It rises above zero in a recession and can, in principle, fall below zero in a boom, tracking the business cycle’s ups and downs rather than any lasting feature of the labor market. It is the one type of the three that is NOT included in the natural rate.
On the exam

An economy’s actual unemployment rate is 8% and its natural rate of unemployment is 5%. What is the cyclical unemployment rate?

  1. A3%
  2. B5%
  3. C8%
  4. D13%
Answer

A. Cyclical unemployment is the deviation of the actual rate from the natural rate: 8% − 5% = 3%. It is the ONLY one of the three types that is not part of the natural rate, which is what makes this subtraction meaningful.

The natural rate of unemploymentalso called NAIRU, the full-employment unemployment rate or the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemploymentNAIRU is the same number under a name that describes what happens on the other side of it: push unemployment below the natural rate and inflation accelerates, which is the Unit 5 half of this story.
Frictional plus structural unemployment - the rate at full employment, and it is not zero.In real lifeAn economy praised as being “at full employment” that still reports a 4-5% unemployment rate. Those percentage points are people between jobs or between industries, not a flaw in the count.The natural rate of unemployment is the unemployment rate that would exist when the economy produces full-employment real output. It is equal to the sum of frictional and structural unemployment. It is a positive number by construction, never zero, and it is not fixed forever: the natural rate of unemployment can gradually change over time because of such things as changes in labor force characteristics - a shift in the average age of workers, in how job searches are conducted, or in how industries are organized can move it up or down over a span of decades.
On the exam

In an economy with 4% frictional unemployment and 2% structural unemployment, and no cyclical unemployment, which of the following is closest to full-employment output?

  1. AOutput at 0% unemployment
  2. BOutput at the natural rate of 6% unemployment
  3. COutput at whatever the actual unemployment rate happens to be that year
  4. DOutput cannot be estimated from unemployment data
Answer

B. The natural rate is frictional plus structural: 4% + 2% = 6%. Full-employment output is defined at THAT rate, not at zero - there is always some frictional and structural unemployment even when the economy is producing at potential.

The line that separates themOnly cyclical unemployment is EXCLUDED from the natural rate. That single fact is why the natural rate does not fall to zero in a boom and does not explode in every recession: frictional and structural unemployment are always present, and cyclical is the only one of the three the business cycle actually moves.
MEA-1.E.2 and MEA-2.A.5, read together “Full employment” means the natural rate - a positive number, not zero. Frictional and structural unemployment never disappear, even in an economy running at full capacity. An economy at potential output still has workers between jobs and industries mid-transition; what it does NOT have is cyclical unemployment, because cyclical is defined as zero exactly when the economy is at the natural rate.
Where the actual rate sits relative to the natural rate
Sign of cyclical unemploymentOutput gapBusiness-cycle reading
Actual rate above natural ratePositiveRecessionary gapBelow a peak, in or after a downturn
Actual rate below natural rateNegativeInflationary gapAn overheated expansion
Actual rate equal to natural rateZeroNo gap — at potential outputAt full-employment output
The line that separates themThe natural rate is the benchmark, not the number the economy is always at. “Full employment” means the actual rate EQUALS the natural rate - a positive number - not that unemployment has hit zero, which is the single most reliable misreading of this whole topic.
The natural rate is not a constant of nature MEA-1.E.4 says it can gradually change over time because of such things as changes in labor force characteristics - who is searching for work, how searches are conducted, and how industries are organized. A natural rate estimated in one decade is not automatically the right benchmark for another. Treating it as a fixed number that never moves makes every later comparison across decades meaningless.
Worked example — sorting three workers by type Worker A lost a factory job when a recession cut orders across the whole industry. Cyclical — tied to the business cycle, and it reverses when demand recovers.
Worker B quit a job on Friday with a new one lined up to start in three weeks. Frictional — the ordinary time it takes to move between jobs.
Worker C’s factory-floor role was automated and the skills do not transfer to the software jobs that replaced it. Structural — a lasting mismatch between skills and available work.
Only Worker A’s unemployment falls when the economy recovers. Workers B and C are exactly the reason the natural rate is not zero even in a strong economy.

2.4 The consumer price index and inflation

One index, one basket, and a bias whose direction is worth memorizing cold: high, always high, never the other way.

The consumer price index (CPI)also called the cost-of-living index or the retail price index (used outside the US)Whatever it is called, it is the fixed-basket index - the one that holds quantities still and lets only prices move.
A single number tracking the cost of a typical household’s shopping basket over time.In real lifeA statistics agency pricing the same list of groceries, rent, and gasoline every month and publishing how much more (or less) it costs than it did a year ago.The consumer price index (CPI) measures the change in income a consumer would need in order to maintain the same standard of living over time under a new set of prices as under the original set of prices. It is a LEVEL, expressed as an index number against a base year, and it is the most common measure of the cost of living used in the United States.
The fixed basket
The same quantities of goods, priced in two different years.In real lifePricing the exact same shopping cart - five loaves of bread, three gallons of milk, one tank of gas - at this year’s prices and at the prices from a chosen base year.The CPI measures the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services in a given year relative to the base year. The quantities in that basket are fixed at the base year’s levels and used again, unchanged, when pricing every later year - only the PRICES are allowed to move. That single design choice is what makes the base year read exactly 100 and is also the source of the bias examined below.
The inflation rate
The percentage change in a price index from one period to the next.In real lifeA CPI moving from 200 to 210 over a year is 5% inflation - the CHANGE in the number, never the number itself.The inflation rate is determined by calculating the percentage change in a price index, such as CPI or the GDP deflator. An index is a level; the inflation rate is that level’s rate of change. A CPI reading of 125 is not “125% inflation” - it says the basket costs 25% more than in the base year, which is a 25% CUMULATIVE rise, not an annual rate unless the base year was exactly one year earlier.
On the exam

The consumer price index rises from 150 to 165 over one year. What is the inflation rate for that year?

  1. A1.5%
  2. B10%
  3. C15%
  4. D150%
Answer

B. (165 − 150) ÷ 150 = 10%. The index level, 165, is not the inflation rate; the inflation rate is the percentage CHANGE between the two levels.

A real variable
A nominal amount with the effect of price changes stripped out.In real lifeA worker whose paycheck rose 3% this year while prices rose 5%. In dollars, the raise looks like a gain; in real terms, that worker can buy less than before.Real variables, such as real wages, are the nominal variables deflated by the price level. Dividing a nominal figure by a price index (and rescaling) converts a dollar amount at one point in time into a quantity comparable across years - what the money would actually buy, rather than how many dollars it is.
Substitution bias
The CPI cannot substitute away from a good that got dearer, so it overstates inflation.In real lifeBeef doubles in price and a household quietly shifts its shopping toward chicken instead. The fixed CPI basket still buys the same amount of beef it always did, and reports a bigger increase in the cost of living than the household actually experienced.The CPI as a measure of inflation has some shortcomings, such as substitution bias, causing it to overstate the true inflation rate. Because the basket’s quantities are locked at the base year, the CPI cannot capture households buying less of whatever got relatively more expensive - it prices out a shopping trip nobody actually takes anymore, and that trip is always at least as costly as the one the household really made.
On the exam

Which of the following best explains why the CPI tends to overstate the true inflation rate?

  1. AIt only measures the prices of goods produced domestically
  2. BIts fixed basket cannot reflect consumers substituting toward goods whose prices rose less
  3. CIt excludes the prices of imported goods entirely
  4. DIt is calculated using current-year quantities rather than base-year quantities
Answer

B. The CPI as a measure of inflation has some shortcomings, such as substitution bias, causing it to overstate the true inflation rate: real households shift spending away from goods that got relatively dearer, but the fixed basket keeps pricing the base year’s exact quantities regardless. The direction of the error - HIGH - is the examinable half of the statement.

MEA-1.F.2, exclusion Not on the exam: calculating the producer price index (PPI) is beyond the scope of the course.
The producer price index (PPI), and why this course stops at its name
A price index for what firms pay each other - named, but never calculated here.In real lifeA steelmaker’s costs rising before the sticker price of a car does, months ahead - the PPI is meant to catch inflation earlier in the pipeline than the CPI, which prices what shoppers finally pay.A price index tracking the prices firms receive for the goods and services they produce, rather than what households pay retail. Calculating the producer price index (PPI) is beyond the scope of the course and AP Exam - the course teaches that it exists and roughly what it tracks, and stops there.

The line that separates themA CPI of 125 is not “125% inflation.” It says the basket costs 25% more than in the base year. Worked example — basket, index, and inflation rate from scratch A two-good basket: 5 kg beef at $8 in year 1 and $12 in year 2; 10 kg chicken at $4 in both years.
Cost of the basket, year 1: (5 × $8) + (10 × $4) = 40 + 40 = $80.
Cost of the basket, year 2: (5 × $12) + (10 × $4) = 60 + 40 = $100.
CPI, year 2 = ($100 ÷ $80) × 100 = 125.
Inflation rate = (125 − 100) ÷ 100 = 25%.
The check. The quantities (5 kg, 10 kg) are IDENTICAL in both cost lines. If you priced year 2’s basket with year 2’s quantities, you have computed something else - not the CPI.

2.5 The costs of inflation

Inflation that surprises nobody costs very little. Inflation that surprises everybody moves real wealth between people who signed contracts before the surprise - and the direction of that move is the one detail students reverse most often.

Unexpected inflation
Redistributes wealth arbitrarily - it does not destroy it.In real lifeA retiree who lent a neighbor $10,000 five years ago at a fixed rate is repaid in dollars that buy noticeably less than they did when the loan was made. The neighbor who borrowed comes out ahead by exactly what the retiree lost.Unexpected inflation arbitrarily redistributes wealth from one group of individuals to another group, such as lenders to borrowers. Nothing is destroyed - the economy’s total wealth is the same - but it moves between people who signed contracts in fixed nominal terms before the price level surprised them. The direction named by the CED is the one most often reversed by students: it runs FROM lenders TO borrowers, because a fixed loan repayment buys less than it used to.
On the exam

A worker takes out a fixed-rate loan just before inflation turns out to be much higher than anyone expected. Who benefits from the unexpected inflation, and who is hurt?

  1. AThe borrower benefits; the lender is hurt
  2. BThe lender benefits; the borrower is hurt
  3. CBoth are hurt equally, because prices rose for both of them
  4. DNeither is affected, because the interest rate was fixed in advance
Answer

A. Unexpected inflation arbitrarily redistributes wealth from one group of individuals to another group, such as lenders to borrowers. The borrower repays the loan in dollars worth less than when the money was borrowed, which is a real gain for the borrower and a real loss for the lender - the direction students most often reverse.

Anticipated inflation
Inflation everyone saw coming and priced into their contracts in advance.In real lifeA landlord who writes a 4% annual rent increase into a lease specifically because both sides expect about 4% inflation that year. Nobody is surprised, so nobody is arbitrarily redistributed from.Inflation that matches what borrowers, lenders, and wage-setters expected when they wrote their contracts. Because it was built into interest rates, wages and prices ahead of time, it redistributes far less than unexpected inflation does - the redistribution problem is specifically about the GAP between what was expected and what actually happened.
A fixed-income household under inflation
A household whose income in dollars does not adjust loses purchasing power every year prices rise.In real lifeA retiree living on a pension that pays the same dollar amount every month. If that amount is not indexed to inflation, each year’s grocery run buys a little less than the year before.Anyone receiving a fixed number of dollars each period - an unindexed pension, a long-term fixed-rate bond’s coupon - loses real purchasing power whenever prices rise, whether or not the rise was expected. This is the household-level version of the same redistribution MEA-1.H.1 describes between lenders and borrowers.
MEA-1.H.1, and the direction that gets reversed Unexpected inflation moves wealth from lenders to borrowers. A fixed loan is repaid in dollars worth less than when it was made. The lender receives fewer real dollars than expected; the borrower hands over fewer real dollars than expected. Read that twice if the direction feels backwards - it is the single most reversed fact in this topic.
Anticipated inflation against unexpected inflation
Priced into contracts in advance?Redistributes wealth?
Anticipated inflationYes — interest rates and wages were set expecting itLittle to none — both sides already adjusted
Unexpected inflationNo — it surprised both sides of every fixed contractYes, arbitrarily — from lenders to borrowers, and from anyone on a fixed income
The line that separates themIt is the SURPRISE that redistributes, not the inflation itself. An economy that always inflates at a steady, well-anticipated 3% redistributes almost nothing between lenders and borrowers, because both priced 3% into the deal from the start.
Who unexpected inflation moves wealth between
Effect of unexpected inflationWhy
Borrowers (debtors)GainThey repay a fixed loan in dollars worth less than when they borrowed
Lenders (creditors)LoseThey are repaid in dollars worth less than they lent

The line that separates themEvery row in this table is the SAME mechanism seen from a different seat: a fixed number of dollars is worth less once prices rise. Whoever is promised or owed that fixed number loses; whoever owes it gains.
Trap — “inflation destroys wealth” It does not, in this mechanism. MEA-1.H.1 says inflation redistributes wealth, not that it erases it. What one party loses in real terms, the other gains - the economy’s total wealth is not smaller, it has simply moved from one group’s ledger to another’s. Confusing redistribution with destruction is a different, much larger claim the CED does not make here.
Worked example — a real interest rate turned negative A saver locks in a bond paying 4% interest for one year. Inflation over that year turns out to be 7%, higher than anyone expected when the rate was set.
Real return ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate = 4% − 7% = −3%.
The saver is repaid MORE dollars than they lent, and can still buy LESS with them than when they lent it. The borrower who issued that bond is the one who gained the 3 percentage points the saver lost.

2.6 Real versus nominal GDP

Two GDP figures from the same year can tell two different stories, and the whole point of this topic is knowing which one to trust for which question.

Nominal GDPalso called GDP in current dollars or money GDPWhichever phrase is used, it means priced at THIS year’s own prices - the one GDP figure that has not had inflation’s effect removed.
How much was spent on output, priced at the year’s own prices.In real lifeA country’s GDP figure that rises partly because it made more, and partly because everything simply cost more that year - nominal GDP does not separate the two.Nominal GDP is a measure of how much is spent on output. It uses that year’s own (current) prices, so it can rise even if actual production is flat or falling, purely because prices went up.
Real GDPalso called GDP in constant dollars, inflation-adjusted GDP or chained GDP“Chained” describes the more sophisticated method statistical agencies actually use instead of the fixed base-year weighting this course teaches by hand - see MEA-1.J.1.
How much was produced, with the effect of price changes stripped out.In real lifeTwo years of a bakery’s output compared in the SAME year’s prices, so the comparison is about how many more loaves came out of the oven, not about the price of flour.Real GDP is a measure of how much is produced. Nominal GDP measures aggregate output using current prices. Real GDP measures aggregate output using constant prices, thus removing the effect of changes in the overall price level - so a change in real GDP reflects a change in actual production, and a change in nominal GDP can reflect production, prices, or both at once.
On the exam

Between year 1 and year 2, an economy’s nominal GDP rose by 12% and its price level rose by 8%. Which of the following is closest to the change in real GDP?

  1. A4% increase
  2. B8% increase
  3. C12% increase
  4. D20% increase
Answer

A. Real GDP measures aggregate output using constant prices, removing the effect of price-level changes: roughly 12% nominal growth minus 8% price growth leaves about 4% of GENUINE output growth. 20% adds the two effects instead of separating them, which is the reverse of what real GDP is for.

Calculating real GDP: the base-year method
Weight this year’s output by base-year prices - taught, and disowned in the same breath.In real lifePricing this year’s entire harvest at last decade’s grain prices, to see how much MORE was actually grown rather than how much more it would sell for today.One way of measuring real GDP is to weigh final goods and services by their prices in a base year. Because this can lead to overstatement of real GDP growth, statistical agencies actually use different methods. Both halves are the statement: the base-year method is the one the exam expects you to compute by hand, and the caveat that agencies do not actually use it is there so the method is not mistaken for an error in the national accounts rather than a deliberate teaching model.
The GDP deflatoralso called the implicit price deflator or the GDP price index“Implicit” because its basket is never written down anywhere - it is whatever the economy happened to produce that year, backed out of the ratio of nominal to real GDP.
The price index built INTO GDP itself - nominal over real, times 100.In real lifeDividing this year’s dollar-value GDP by the version of GDP that removes price changes, to read off how much of the dollar rise was just prices.Nominal GDP can be converted to real GDP by using the GDP deflator: real GDP = (nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator) × 100. Unlike the CPI, the deflator’s implicit basket is whatever the economy actually produced that year - it changes every year rather than being fixed - and it covers everything domestically produced rather than only what households buy.
Real GDP per capita
Real GDP divided by population - output PER PERSON, not output overall.In real lifeA country whose total real GDP grows while its population grows even faster: the total pie is bigger, but each slice, per person, is smaller.Real GDP divided by the population, used as a rough proxy for the average standard of living. Total real GDP and real GDP per capita are separate claims scored separately: an economy’s total output can rise while its population grows faster still, leaving each person with less than before even as the aggregate number looks like growth.
Nominal GDP against real GDP
Prices usedA rise in it can mean
Nominal GDPCurrent (that year’s own) pricesMore was produced, prices rose, or both at once — nominal GDP cannot tell you which
Real GDPConstant (base-year) pricesMore was actually produced — the price effect has been removed
The line that separates themReal GDP is what is left of nominal GDP’s growth after the price level’s own growth is subtracted out. An economy can report double-digit nominal GDP growth in a year of high inflation while real GDP - actual output - barely moves.
Trap — the base-year method is taught, then disowned in the same sentence MEA-1.J.1 teaches weighting output by base-year prices, and immediately adds that statistical agencies use different methods because this one overstates real GDP growth. Both halves matter: the exam expects you to compute it this way BY HAND, and the caveat exists so the method is not mistaken for an error in the real national accounts rather than a deliberate teaching simplification.
The base year against the current (comparison) year
Prices used for real GDPIndex reading
Base yearIts own prices, by definitionAlways exactly 100
Current (comparison) yearThe BASE year’s prices, applied to this year’s quantitiesAbove 100 if prices rose since the base year, below if they fell
The line that separates themReal GDP for the current year is not priced at the current year’s own prices - that would just be nominal GDP again. It borrows the base year’s prices and applies them to the current year’s quantities, which is the one substitution that keeps price changes out of the number.
Worked example — real GDP, the GDP deflator, and per-capita GDP Year 1 (base year): 100 units at $2 each. Year 2: 120 units at $3 each. Population: 30 in year 1, 40 in year 2.
Nominal GDP, year 2 = 120 × $3 = $360.
Real GDP, year 2 (base-year prices on year-2 quantity) = 120 × $2 = $240.
GDP deflator, year 2 = ($360 ÷ $240) × 100 = 150.
Nominal GDP, year 1 = 100 × $2 = $200, which is also real GDP in the base year.
Real GDP per capita: year 1 = $200 ÷ 30 ≈ $6.67; year 2 = $240 ÷ 40 = $6.00 — per-capita output fell even though real GDP rose from $200 to $240.
The check. Real GDP used $2 (the base-year price) in BOTH years’ calculations. The moment a $3 appears in a real-GDP line, the arithmetic has drifted into nominal GDP.

2.7 Business cycles

Everything in this unit has been leading here: the labor market and the price level both move as the economy swings through this cycle, and this topic names the swing itself.

The business cyclealso called the trade cycle or the economic cycleOlder texts favor “trade cycle”; the CED and the exam both say business cycle.
The economy’s recurring swings above and below its long-run trend.In real lifeA national economy that grows for years, then shrinks for a shorter stretch, then grows again - not a straight climb, but a wave around the climb.Business cycles are fluctuations in aggregate output and employment because of changes in aggregate supply and/or aggregate demand. The horizontal axis of the graph that shows this is TIME - the one graph in the whole course drawn that way, and reading it as if it were a supply-and-demand diagram gets everything after it wrong.
Recession and expansion
The two PHASES of the cycle - stretches of time, not single moments.In real lifeA multi-year run of rising output and hiring is an expansion; a shorter stretch of falling output and layoffs that follows it is a recession. Both take time to unfold.The phases of a business cycle are recession and expansion. A phase is a stretch of time during which output is generally moving in one direction - contracting during a recession, growing during an expansion - and it is a different kind of thing from a turning point, which is a single instant.
Peak and trough
The two TURNING POINTS of the cycle - single moments, not stretches of time.In real lifeThe single month in which hiring, output and confidence all crest before the layoffs begin is the peak; the single month at the bottom of the following slump, right before hiring picks back up, is the trough.The turning points of a business cycle are peak and trough. A peak is the single highest point output reaches before a recession begins; a trough is the single lowest point before an expansion begins. Students routinely answer “peak” when a question asks for a phase - the fix is remembering that a phase is a stretch of time and a turning point is one moment inside it.
On the exam

Which of the following correctly pairs a phase of the business cycle with a turning point?

  1. APeak is a phase; recession is a turning point
  2. BExpansion is a turning point; trough is a phase
  3. CRecession is a phase; trough is the turning point at its end
  4. DPeak and trough are both phases, and recession and expansion are both turning points
Answer

C. The phases of a business cycle are recession and expansion, and the turning points are peak and trough. A recession runs from a peak down to a trough, so the trough is the single moment that ends it - a phase and its ending turning point, correctly paired.

The output gap
The difference between actual output and potential output.In real lifeAn economy producing $50 billion less than its factories, workers and technology could sustainably support has a $50 billion output gap - a recessionary one, in this case.The difference between actual output and potential output is the output gap. A gap can run in either direction: actual output below potential is a recessionary gap, and actual output above potential (which is possible only temporarily) is an inflationary gap.
Potential outputalso called full-employment output, potential GDP or Y sub FWhichever name a stem uses, it is GDP at the natural rate of unemployment - not GDP at zero unemployment.
Full-employment output - the level of GDP at the NATURAL rate of unemployment, not at zero.In real lifeAn economy running at its long-run productive capacity, with unemployment sitting at whatever rate frictional and structural unemployment alone would produce - not an unemployment rate of zero.Potential output is also called full-employment output. It is the level of GDP where unemployment is equal to the natural rate of unemployment. This is the reconciliation that keeps the phrase “full employment” from meaning zero unemployment: the natural rate is a positive number by definition, so an economy at potential output still has some workers between jobs and some industries mid-transition.
A recessionary gap
Actual output below potential output.In real lifeAn economy running below its own long-run capacity, with idle factories and workers who want jobs and cannot find them - the same picture as a point inside the production possibilities curve.An output gap in which actual output falls short of potential output. It coincides with an actual unemployment rate above the natural rate - cyclical unemployment is positive - and with a point INSIDE the economy’s production possibilities curve, the picture Unit 1 already drew for exactly this state.
An inflationary gap
Actual output above potential output - possible, but only for a while.In real lifeAn overheated economy running its factories past their sustainable capacity and hiring workers faster than the labor market can supply them without bidding wages up sharply.An output gap in which actual output exceeds potential output. It coincides with an actual unemployment rate below the natural rate. Because potential output is the economy’s sustainable capacity, an inflationary gap cannot persist indefinitely without rising prices pulling output back toward potential.
Phases against turning points of the business cycle

The line that separates themA question that asks “what phase is the economy in” wants recession or expansion; a question that asks “what point on the cycle” wants peak or trough. Answering “peak” to the first question or “recession” to the second is the single most common mix-up in this topic.
Trap — naming a turning point when the question wants a phase “Peak” answers a question about a turning point; it does not answer a question about a phase. A recession is the whole stretch from one peak down to the following trough, not the moment at either end of it. Reading the question carefully for “phase” against “turning point” settles more of this topic than any other single habit.
A recessionary gap against an inflationary gap
Actual against potential outputActual against the natural rateCan it last indefinitely?
A recessionary gapActual below potentialActual unemployment above naturalYes, until demand recovers

The line that separates themPotential output is a ceiling on what the economy can sustainably produce, not on what it can produce for a while. An economy can sit BELOW potential for years; sitting ABOVE it is possible only briefly, because it means running factories and hiring workers past what the economy’s underlying capacity supports.
Where this unit’s three ideas meet on one figure Peak = inflationary gap = actual unemployment BELOW natural. Trough = recessionary gap = actual unemployment ABOVE natural. The business cycle, the output gap, and the gap between actual and natural unemployment are three names for the same state of the economy, read off three different figures. A stem can hand you any one of the three and expect you to supply either of the other two.
