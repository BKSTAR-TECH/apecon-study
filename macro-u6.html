6.1 The balance of payments accounts

Before any currency moves, there is a ledger. The balance of payments is an accounting system, not a policy and not a verdict - and almost every mark in this topic is lost to a word whose everyday meaning points the opposite way from its meaning here.

The balance of payments (BOP)
The record of every transaction a country makes with the rest of the world in a period.also called the balance of payments accounts, the international accounts or the external accountsA stem that says “a country’s balance of payments accounts” in the plural is naming the same two-account system, not a longer list.In real lifeA national statistics office publishing a quarterly table of everything the country sold abroad, bought from abroad, invested abroad and was invested in.An accounting system that records a country’s international transactions for a particular time period. It consists of two accounts and nothing else: the current account and the capital and financial account. It is a record rather than a policy, and it does not measure whether a country is doing well - it measures where its money crossed the border and in which direction.
The current account (CA)
Three components, not one: net exports, net income from abroad, and net unilateral transfers.In real lifeA country’s quarterly release listing the value of its exports and imports, the profits its firms earned overseas, and the money migrant workers sent home - all in the same account.The account that records net exports, net income from abroad, and net unilateral transfers. The first is goods and services bought and sold, the second is wages, interest and profit earned by residents abroad minus the same paid to foreigners here, and the third is money that moves with nothing coming back - aid, gifts, remittances. Naming only the first is the commonest way to answer this wrong while sounding right, because trade is the biggest of the three and the only one anybody reports.
On the exam

A country records exports of goods and services of $520 billion, imports of $610 billion, net income from abroad of positive $25 billion, and net unilateral transfers of negative $15 billion. What is the balance on its current account?

  1. AA deficit of $90 billion, because that is exports minus imports
  2. BA deficit of $80 billion
  3. CA surplus of $80 billion, because income and transfers outweigh the trade gap
  4. DIt cannot be worked out without the capital and financial account
Answer

B. All three components go in: (520 − 610) + 25 − 15 = −80. Option A is the balance of trade on its own, which is part of the current account rather than the whole of it, and it is the answer a reader gets by stopping after the first component.

The balance of trade
Exports minus imports of goods and services - one component of the current account, not another name for it.also called the balance of trade, the trade balance or X − MAll four name the same number. None of them names the current account, which has two further components inside it.In real lifeA newspaper headline reporting that the country “ran a trade gap of $90 billion last quarter” is reporting this number and not the current account.A nation’s balance of trade (i.e., net exports) is part of the current account and may also show a surplus or a deficit. It is exports of goods and services minus imports of them, and it is the largest of the current account’s three components - but income earned abroad and transfers sent home sit in the current account and in no trade figure at all, so the two numbers are different numbers and a question can hand you either.
Net income from abroad
What residents earn on what they own overseas, minus what foreigners earn on what they own here.In real lifeThe dividends a national pension fund collects on the foreign shares it holds, set against the profits a foreign-owned factory sends back to its parent company.Wages, interest, rent and profit flowing to residents from abroad, net of the same flowing out to foreigners. It sits in the current account even though nothing was shipped, because the income is a return on work done or capital already placed rather than a purchase of an asset. That distinction is the one the exam tests: buying the foreign share is a capital and financial account entry; collecting the dividend on it afterwards is a current account entry.
Net unilateral transfers
Money that crosses a border with nothing coming back the other way.In real lifeA nurse working abroad who wires part of every paycheck to her parents at home: money leaves her country of work, and no good, service or asset moves against it.Gifts, foreign aid, pensions paid overseas and remittances sent home, netted against the same arriving. They belong to the current account, which surprises readers who expect an account of trade to hold only trade. The test is what came back: nothing did, which is exactly what makes the transfer unilateral, and it is why these entries never balance against a matching item.
The capital and financial account (CFA)
The account for assets: shares, bonds, property, factories, and financial capital transfers.also called the financial account, the capital account or the capital and financial accountsOlder papers split this into two accounts and newer ones do not. A stem naming either half on its own is asking about assets crossing a border, which is the whole of what this account holds.In real lifeA sovereign wealth fund buying an office block in another country, or a household here putting savings into a foreign share index.The account that records financial capital transfers and purchases and sales of assets between countries. The question that sorts a transaction into it is not how big it is or who made it - it is whether what changed hands was an asset. A car crossing a border is a current account entry; the factory that built the car changing owner is a capital and financial account entry.
On the exam

A pension fund based in Japan buys government bonds issued by Country X. In Country X’s balance of payments accounts, how is this transaction recorded?

  1. AIn the current account, as a credit, because money enters Country X
  2. BIn the current account, as a debit, because a Japanese buyer is involved
  3. CIn the capital and financial account, as a credit
  4. DIn the capital and financial account, as a debit
Answer

C. A bond is an asset, so the transaction belongs in the capital and financial account, and the money is coming into Country X, so it is a credit. Options A and B sort the transaction on what it looks like rather than on what changed hands.

The balance of payments identity
CA + CFA = 0. The two accounts are forced onto opposite sides of zero.In real lifeA country that spends more on imports than it earns on exports finds that the money it sent abroad comes back to buy its bonds and its buildings, because there is nowhere else for it to go.The sum of all credit entries should match the sum of all debit entries, which is the same claim as CA + CFA = 0. Nothing about it is a coincidence: the money a country sends abroad to pay for imports is held by foreigners afterwards, and what they eventually do with it is buy assets here. So a current account deficit is not merely accompanied by a capital and financial account surplus - it requires one, of exactly the same size, and the two accounts therefore move in opposite directions whatever the year turns out to be.
On the exam

A country reports a current account deficit for the year. Which of the following must also be true of the same year?

  1. AIts capital and financial account is also in deficit, because both measure money leaving
  2. BIts capital and financial account is in surplus by the same amount
  3. CIts balance of trade must be in surplus to compensate
  4. DIts balance of payments accounts do not sum to zero that year
Answer

B. The two accounts sum to zero, so a deficit on one is a surplus of equal size on the other. Option A is the everyday reading of surplus and deficit as good and bad news moving together, and it is exactly what the identity forbids. Option C puts the trade balance inside the current account and then asks it to offset itself.

MEA-4.A.5 — the whole system, in two accounts and nothing else
The current accountgoods, services, income earned, and gifts
The capital and financial accountassets: shares, bonds, property, factories
Every international transaction lands in exactly one of the two, or in neither
There is no third account and no leftover. A transaction that appears to fit nowhere has usually failed the first question rather than the second: nothing crossed a border, so no entry is made at all.
The current account against the capital and financial account
What crossed the borderWhat it recordsA transaction that lands here

The line that separates themOne question sorts every transaction: was what changed hands an asset? If it was, the entry is in the capital and financial account; if it was a good, a service, income or a gift, it is in the current account. The size of the transaction and who made it decide nothing.
The balance of trade against the current account
What is inside itSize in the worked figuresWhat a question means by it
The balance of tradeExports minus imports of goods and services−$90bn“the trade balance”, “the trade gap”, “net exports”
The current account (CA)That, plus net income from abroad and net unilateral transfers−$80bn“the current account balance”
The line that separates themThey are different numbers, and the CED says so in its own gloss: the balance of trade is part of the current account. Income earned abroad and money sent home sit in the current account and in no trade figure at all, so a page that treats the two as synonyms is a page that will answer a two-number question with one number.
Trap — treating the current account as a longer name for the trade balance It is the commonest way to be wrong about this topic while sounding entirely right, because trade is the largest of the three components and the only one that gets reported. A country’s balance of trade is part of the current account. The other two parts - what its residents earned abroad, and what left as gifts and remittances - sit in the current account and in no trade figure, so a question can hand you either number and they will not agree.
A credit
Any transaction that brings money into the country.In real lifeA foreign visitor paying for a week in a hotel here: the room never leaves the country, and the money arrives.Any transaction that causes money to flow into a country is a credit to its balance of payments account. The entry follows the money, not the goods: an export sends goods out and is a credit, because the payment comes in. Somebody who tracks the crates rather than the payments gets every line of a balance of payments table backwards, and a household bank statement, where a credit is money the account holder puts in, gives no help at all.
A debit
Any transaction that sends money out of the country.In real lifeBuying a foreign-built phone: the phone arrives, and the payment leaves.Any transaction that causes money to flow out is a debit. Imports, income paid to foreigners, transfers sent abroad and purchases of foreign assets are all debits, whichever account they land in - so the account and the side are two separate questions and a transaction has to answer both. The exam asks them together and marks them separately.
A current account deficit
More money leaving on the current account than arriving on it.In real lifeA country whose households buy a great deal from abroad and whose firms sell less overseas than they used to.The current account is not always balanced; it may show a surplus or a deficit. A deficit means the three components together are negative - most often because imports exceed exports, but income and transfers can move it either way. It is not by itself evidence of anything going wrong: the identity guarantees a matching capital and financial account surplus, which is foreign money buying assets here, and whether that is welcome depends on what those assets are.
A capital and financial account surplus
A financial capital inflow: foreigners bought more assets here than residents bought abroad.In real lifeA year in which overseas pension funds bought heavily into this country’s government bonds while local savers left their money at home.The CFA is not always balanced; it may show a surplus, which is a financial capital inflow. The word points the opposite way from the one a reader has just learned: a trade surplus means goods going out, and a capital account surplus means money coming in. Hold on to the direction rather than the label, because the exam prints the label and scores the direction.
On the exam

Over one year, foreign investors purchase $300 billion of a country’s assets while residents of that country purchase $220 billion of foreign assets. Which of the following describes the country’s capital and financial account, and what has happened to financial capital?

  1. AA deficit of $80 billion, and financial capital flowed out
  2. BA surplus of $80 billion, and financial capital flowed in
  3. CA surplus of $520 billion, because both purchases add to it
  4. DA balance of zero, because purchases in both directions cancel
Answer

B. Assets sold to foreigners minus assets bought abroad is 300 − 220 = a surplus of 80, and a surplus on this account is money arriving. Option A keeps the arithmetic and reverses the direction, which is the error the word “surplus” invites.

A capital and financial account deficit
A financial capital outflow: residents bought more foreign assets than foreigners bought here.In real lifeA year in which local investors move savings into overseas markets faster than foreign money arrives.The other half of MEA-4.A.4: a CFA deficit is a financial capital outflow. By the identity it has to sit against a current account surplus of the same size, which is why a country that sells the world more than it buys is also, in the same breath, lending the world the money to pay for it.
A credit against a debit
Which way the money wentWhich way the thing wentExamples
A creditInto the countryOut, if anything moved at allAn export sold, a tourist’s hotel bill, a foreigner buying bonds here
A debitOut of the countryIn, if anything moved at allAn import bought, wages sent home by a worker here, a resident buying foreign shares
The line that separates themThe entry follows the money, not the goods. An export sends crates out and is a credit because the payment comes in. Anyone who tracks the crates gets every row backwards - and a household bank statement, where a credit is money you paid in, is no help either.
What a surplus means on each of the two accounts
The wordWhat is actually happeningWhat the other account must be doing
A current account deficitDeficitMore money leaving on trade, income and transfers than arrivingThe capital and financial account is in surplus by exactly the same amount
A capital and financial account surplusSurplusFinancial capital flowing IN - foreigners bought more assets here than residents bought abroadThe current account is in deficit by exactly the same amount
The line that separates themThe identity forces them onto opposite sides of zero, so the two accounts can never both be in surplus or both in deficit. Everyday usage - surplus good, deficit bad - would have them move together, and that is the single most reversible pair of words in the unit.
Worked example — both balances from one list A country reports, for one year in billions: exports $520, imports $610, net income from abroad +$25, net unilateral transfers −$15, foreign purchases of its assets $300, its own purchases of foreign assets $220.
Balance of trade = $520 − $610 = −$90bn.
Current account = −$90 + $25 − $15 = −$80bn, a deficit.
Capital and financial account = $300 − $220 = +$80bn, a surplus - which means financial capital flowed in.
Check. −$80bn + $80bn = $0. The two accounts came out on opposite sides of zero and the same size, which they always must. If yours came out both negative, one of the six numbers went in on the wrong side.
MEA-4.A.6 — why the identity is not an accident CA + CFA = 0 The money a country sends abroad to pay for imports does not disappear when it crosses the border. Foreigners hold it afterwards, and what they eventually do with it is buy something here - and if they are not buying goods and services, they are buying assets. So a current account deficit does not merely happen to sit beside a capital and financial account surplus. It requires one, of exactly the same size, in the same year.
Trap — reading the two accounts as two report cards Everyday English has surplus meaning good news and deficit meaning bad, so a reader expects a country doing well to run a surplus on both. The identity makes that impossible. A country running a current account deficit is by definition attracting an equal amount of foreign money into its assets, and a country running a current account surplus is by definition sending an equal amount out. Whether either is welcome is a separate question the balance of payments does not answer.

6.2 Exchange rates, and the two ways of writing one

An exchange rate is a price, and like every price it needs units. Getting the units the wrong way up is the single most common arithmetic error in this unit, and it survives every plausibility check except one.

The foreign exchange market
The market where one currency is exchanged for another.also called the currency market, the FX market or the forex marketA question set in “the currency market for the peso” is set on exactly the graph this unit draws.In real lifeThe bureau at an airport, and the far larger wholesale market behind it where banks trade currencies all day.In the foreign exchange market, one currency is exchanged for another. It is a market like any other - a demand curve, a supply curve, a price that clears it - with one difference that changes how every question reads: the good being priced is money, so the same event appears twice, once in each currency’s own market, and the two pictures have to agree.
The exchange rate
The price of one currency in terms of the other.also called the international value of the currency, the value of the currency or the price of one currency in another“The international value of the dollar” is the phrase released items use most, and it means the exchange rate. Value up is appreciation.In real lifeA board at a currency desk showing 1.25 dollars per euro on one line and 0.80 euros per dollar on the next.The price of one currency in terms of the other. “In terms of the other” is what makes it a pair of numbers rather than one: quoted as dollars per euro it answers what an American pays for a euro, and quoted as euros per dollar it answers what a European pays for a dollar. The two are reciprocals and multiply to 1, so knowing either is knowing both - but only one of them answers any given question, and reaching for the wrong half of the pair is the cheapest mark to lose in this unit.
On the exam

The exchange rate is 0.80 euros per United States dollar. A machine made in the United States is priced by its manufacturer at 10,000 dollars. What does that machine cost a buyer in Europe?

  1. A8,000 euros
  2. B10,000 euros
  3. C12,500 euros
  4. D800 euros
Answer

A. 10,000 dollars × 0.80 euros per dollar = 8,000 euros; the dollars cancel and euros are left, which is the check that catches the wrong quote. Option C is what comes out of multiplying by 1.25 dollars per euro instead - the other half of the pair, which is a distractor on every item of this kind.

The reciprocal quote
The same rate written the other way up: one divided by the quote you were given.In real lifeA traveller told the pound costs 1.25 dollars works out immediately that a dollar costs 0.80 pounds, because 1 ÷ 1.25 = 0.80.Every exchange rate has two quotes, and each is one divided by the other. They multiply to exactly 1, which is a check worth doing when a stem hands over a number without saying which way round it is. The reliable habit is to write the units beside the number: euros per dollar, multiplied by dollars, leaves euros. A quote used the wrong way up survives every plausibility check except that one.
Appreciation
A currency becomes more valuable in terms of the other - it buys more of it than before.In real lifeA traveller who got 0.80 euros for a dollar last year and gets 1.00 euros for it this year is holding a currency that appreciated.If one currency becomes more valuable in terms of the other, it is said to appreciate. The test is what one unit buys: if a dollar buys more euros than it did, the dollar appreciated. And because the rate is a pair, one currency appreciating is the same event as the other depreciating - never two events, and never both currencies moving the same way.
Depreciation
A currency becomes less valuable in terms of the other - it buys less of it than before.In real lifeA euro that used to fetch 1.25 dollars and now fetches 1.00 has depreciated, and everything priced in dollars has become dearer for anybody spending euros.If one currency becomes less valuable in terms of the other, it is said to depreciate. The two percentage moves in one event are not equal and opposite, because a percentage needs a base and the two quotes have different bases: a euro falling from 1.25 dollars to 1.00 fell 20%, while the dollar rising from 0.80 euros to 1.00 rose 25%. Both describe the same afternoon, so a stem asking “by how much” is asking about one named currency.
A flexible exchange rate
A rate set by demand and supply in the market rather than fixed by a government.also called a floating exchange rate, a freely floating rate or a market-determined rateA stem that opens “assume an open economy with a flexible exchange rate” is telling you the rate is free to move, which is what makes the shifts you draw change it.In real lifeThe rate between the dollar and the yen, which moves every minute of every trading day and which nobody announces.The regime every graph in this unit assumes, and the reason released stems say so explicitly - “an open economy with a flexible exchange rate” is the sentence that tells you the currency is free to move and that the curves you draw will actually change the rate. Under a fixed rate the same shifts would be met by the central bank buying or selling its own currency to hold the price where it was.
MKT-5.A.1 — why one rate is two numbers The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of the other. “In terms of the other” is not decoration. It means an exchange rate has to name two currencies and say which one is being priced, exactly as “$3” means nothing until you know what it buys. So every rate comes as a pair of reciprocal quotes, they multiply to 1, and the stem decides which one you need.
The two halves of one exchange rate
QuoteWhat it answersMultiply it byTo get
Dollars per euro ($1.25)What an American pays for one euroA price in eurosThat price in dollars
Euros per dollar (0.80)What a European pays for one dollarA price in dollarsThat price in euros
The line that separates themWrite the units beside the number. Euros per dollar, multiplied by dollars, leaves euros; the wrong quote leaves euros per dollar squared, which is nothing. That single habit removes the distractor that appears on every conversion item in this unit.
Worked example — the units check, in one line A stem gives the rate as 0.80 euros per dollar and asks what a $10,000 American machine costs a European buyer.
Write the units next to the numbers: 10,000 dollars × 0.80 euros/dollar. The word “dollars” appears on the top and on the bottom, so it cancels, and what is left is euros.
Answer = 8,000 euros.
What the wrong quote does. Reaching for $1.25 per euro instead gives 10,000 × 1.25 = 12,500 - and the units say dollars × dollars/euro, which leaves dollars squared per euro. That is not a price of anything, and it is the only warning you get.
Appreciation against depreciation
What one unit buys nowThe worked exampleWhat the other currency did
AppreciationMore of the other currency than beforeA dollar went from buying 0.80 euros to buying 1.00It depreciated, in the same event
DepreciationLess of the other currency than beforeA euro went from buying $1.25 to buying $1.00It appreciated, in the same event
The line that separates themThey are one event described from two sides, never two events. So the test is always the same: take one unit of the currency the question names and ask whether it buys more of the other than it did. And the two percentage moves are not equal - the euro fell 20% while the dollar rose 25% - because a percentage needs a base and the two bases are different.
Trap — expecting the two percentage moves to match When the euro falls from $1.25 to $1.00, the euro has fallen 20% and the dollar has risen 25%. Neither figure is wrong and neither is the “real” one: a percentage is measured against a base, and the two quotes have different bases. So a stem asking “by how much did it change” is asking about one named currency, and the answer depends on which one it named.

6.3 The foreign exchange market: where the two curves come from

The foreign exchange market has two curves that slope the usual way for the usual reason - and one difference that changes how every question in this unit reads. The good being priced is money, so the same trade appears twice, once on each side.

The demand for a currency
Foreigners who want this country’s goods, services or assets have to buy its currency first.In real lifeA German firm ordering an American machine must obtain dollars before it can pay for anything at all.The demand for a currency in a foreign exchange market arises from the demand for the country’s goods, services, and financial assets and shows the inverse relationship between the exchange rate and the quantity demanded of a currency. The curve slopes down for the usual reason, one step removed: a cheaper dollar makes everything priced in dollars cheaper for a foreigner, so they buy more of it and need more dollars to do so.
The supply of a currency
Residents here who want to pay for something priced abroad have to sell their own currency.In real lifeAn American ordering an Italian bicycle has to turn dollars into euros before the seller sees a cent.The supply of a currency in a foreign exchange market arises from making payments in other currencies and shows the positive relationship between the exchange rate and the quantity supplied of a currency. That single sentence decides most shift questions: an event that changes what residents here buy abroad lands on this curve, which is why a tariff on imports - a policy about what this country buys - moves supply and not demand.
Currency demand is derived
Nobody wants a currency for itself; they want what it buys.In real lifeNobody collects Japanese yen for the sake of the notes - they want a Japanese car, a hotel room in Osaka, or a Japanese government bond.Every determinant of an exchange rate is really a determinant of something else - of demand for a country’s exports, of the return on its bonds, of how attractive its assets look this month. This is why the shift questions in this unit are never about the currency itself, and why an event has to be traced back to the goods, services or assets behind it before it can be put on a curve.
The equilibrium exchange rate
The rate at which the quantity of the currency demanded equals the quantity supplied.In real lifeThe single number a trading screen settles on at any moment, at which everybody wanting to buy that currency finds somebody wanting to sell it.In the foreign exchange market, equilibrium is achieved when the exchange rate is such that the quantities demanded and supplied of the currency are equal. It is read off the vertical axis, and the vertical axis is a price of one currency in another - so an equilibrium here is a pair of numbers as much as any other quote, and the same crossing appears in the other currency’s panel the other way up.
MKT-5.B — two sentences, and everything else follows from them
Demand comes fromforeigners who want this country’s goods, services or financial assets
Supply comes fromresidents here who need another currency to pay for something
So every determinant of an exchange rate is really a determinant of something else
This is why the shift questions in topic 6.4 are never about the currency itself. An event has to be traced back to the goods, services or assets behind it before it can be put on a curve at all - and the tracing is the part that is marked.
The demand for a currency against the supply of it
Who is on this sideWhere it comes fromSlope, and why
The demand for a currencyForeignersThey want this country’s goods, services or financial assets and must buy its currency firstDownward: a cheaper currency makes everything priced in it cheaper for them
The supply of a currencyResidents of this countryThey want to pay for something priced in another currency and must sell theirsUpward: a currency that buys more abroad makes imports look cheap, so more of it is offered
The line that separates themThe whole of the shift half of this unit is one question: did the event change what foreigners want from here, or what residents here buy abroad? The first moves demand and the second moves supply, and getting that wrong reverses the answer rather than blurring it.
Worked example — one trade, both panels An American orders a bicycle from a workshop in Italy, priced at 400 euros.
In the market for dollars: the American has to sell dollars to get euros, so this trade adds to the supply of dollars.
In the market for euros: the same trade is somebody wanting euros, so it adds to the demand for euros.
The check. One transaction, two panels, and it landed on a different curve in each. That is normal and it is not two events: supply of one currency and demand for the other are the same people doing the same thing, seen from opposite sides of the counter.
Trap — reading a currency graph as if it were a goods market The slopes are the same and the reasoning behind them is the same, but two things are not. The vertical axis is a price of one currency in another, so it has to be read as a pair - and the buyers on the demand side are foreigners, while the sellers on the supply side are residents here. Half of the shift questions in this unit are answered by knowing which of those two groups the stem just described.

6.3a A rate that is not clearing, and how it fixes itself

MKT-5.D.1 is one sentence and it is the only place in this unit where nothing shifts. A rate away from equilibrium leaves a gap, the gap is what moves the rate, and no one has to decide anything.

A surplus of a currency
At a rate above equilibrium, more of the currency is offered than anybody wants to buy.In real lifeA currency desk that finds itself holding pounds nobody will take at the price it is quoting, and starts quoting a lower one.Disequilibrium exchange rates create surpluses and shortages in the foreign exchange market. Above the equilibrium rate the quantity supplied of the currency exceeds the quantity demanded, and the gap is measured horizontally, between two quantities at one rate. What closes it is the rate falling: nobody imposed the price and nothing shifted, which is what separates this figure from a price ceiling.
A shortage of a currency
At a rate below equilibrium, more of the currency is wanted than is being offered.In real lifeBuyers queueing for a currency at a rate that looks like a bargain, and finding there is not enough of it to go round at that rate.Below the equilibrium rate the quantity demanded of the currency exceeds the quantity supplied. The unmet buyers bid the rate up. Both gaps in this pair are horizontal distances at a single rate, and both are closed by the rate moving rather than by either curve shifting.
Adjustment back to equilibrium
Market forces drive exchange rates toward equilibrium - nobody has to decide anything.In real lifeA trading day that starts with a currency quoted too high and ends with the quote drifting down until buyers and sellers meet.Market forces drive exchange rates toward equilibrium. A surplus pushes the rate down and a shortage pushes it up, and in both cases the movement is along the curves rather than a shift of either one. That distinction is what a question is testing when it describes a rate that is not clearing: nothing has changed about what anybody wants, only the price they are being asked to trade at.
A surplus of a currency against a shortage of it
Where the rate isWhich quantity is biggerWhich way the rate is pushed
A surplus of a currencyAbove equilibriumQuantity supplied exceeds quantity demandedDown, until the two meet
A shortage of a currencyBelow equilibriumQuantity demanded exceeds quantity suppliedUp, until the two meet
The line that separates themBoth gaps are horizontal - two quantities read at one rate - and in both cases nothing shifted. What closes the gap is a movement along the two curves, which is what separates this figure from any question about a curve moving.
MKT-5.D.1 — the two halves, in one sentence each
Above equilibriuma surplus of the currency, and the rate is pushed down
Below equilibriuma shortage of it, and the rate is pushed up
Either waythe movement is ALONG both curves, not a shift of either
Market forces drive exchange rates toward equilibrium
Both gaps are horizontal distances measured at one rate. If you find yourself measuring a vertical gap, you have measured the distance from the quoted rate to the equilibrium rate - which is real, but it is not the surplus and not the shortage.
Trap — confusing this with a curve that moved A question describing a rate that is too high has told you where the price is, not that anybody’s wants have changed. Nothing about the demand for this country’s goods or assets is different, and nothing about what its residents buy abroad is different - so neither curve moves and the answer is a movement along them. A question that says a curve shifted will say what changed about somebody’s behaviour, and topic 6.4 is entirely about those.

6.4 What moves the equilibrium exchange rate

This is the largest topic in the unit by exam volume, and it is one question repeated: an event, four pairs of directions, and one right answer. Every one of them is settled by deciding which curve moved before deciding anything else.

A shift in currency demand
Something changed what foreigners want from this country: its goods, its services or its assets.In real lifeA run of strong results at this country’s listed firms, which has overseas funds buying its shares - and buying its currency to do it.Factors that shift the demand for a currency, such as the demand for that country’s goods, services, or assets, change the equilibrium exchange rate. Demand right means the currency appreciates; demand left means it depreciates. The sorting question is always the same one: did the event change what foreigners want from here?
A shift in currency supply
Something changed what residents here buy abroad, and therefore how much of their currency they offer.In real lifeA year of rising incomes at home, in which households buy noticeably more imported goods than they did.Supply of the currency right means it depreciates, and supply left means it appreciates - which is the reverse of the intuition a goods market builds, because here more of the thing on offer means a lower price for the thing itself. Two events that look as though they belong on the demand side sit here: a rise in home incomes, and a tariff.
On the exam

Residents of a country begin buying substantially more imported goods than before, with nothing else changing. In that country’s currency market, which curve moves, in which direction, and what happens to the currency?

  1. ADemand for the currency shifts right, and it appreciates
  2. BDemand for the currency shifts left, and it depreciates
  3. CSupply of the currency shifts right, and it depreciates
  4. DSupply of the currency shifts left, and it appreciates
Answer

C. Paying for imports means selling this currency to obtain somebody else’s, so more importing puts more of it on the market: supply shifts right and the currency depreciates. Options A and B put a domestic decision on the demand curve, which is made of what foreigners want.

A tariff
A tax on imports - and in this unit, a shift of the SUPPLY of the country’s own currency.In real lifeA duty added at the border to every imported washing machine, which shows up as a higher shelf price and fewer of them sold.The CED’s own example of a supply-side shifter: tariffs or quotas on the other country’s goods and services shift the supply of a currency. A tariff means residents here buy fewer foreign goods, so fewer units of this currency are offered in exchange for them: supply left, and the currency appreciates. Nothing about a tariff changes a foreigner’s appetite for this country’s exports or bonds, which is what the demand curve is made of.
On the exam

Country X, an open economy with a flexible exchange rate, imposes a tariff on imported goods. In the foreign exchange market for Country X’s currency, what happens to the supply of that currency and to its value?

  1. ASupply increases, and the currency depreciates
  2. BSupply decreases, and the currency appreciates
  3. CDemand increases, and the currency appreciates
  4. DDemand decreases, and the currency depreciates
Answer

B. A tariff means residents of Country X buy fewer imports, and paying for imports is the only reason they offer their currency abroad - so supply falls and the currency appreciates. Options C and D put a decision made inside Country X on the demand curve, which is made entirely of what foreigners want from it.

A quota
A limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported - same curve as a tariff, same direction.In real lifeA cap of so many tonnes of imported sugar a year, after which no more may enter whatever anybody is willing to pay.A quantity restriction rather than a tax, and in the currency market it works the same way: fewer imports bought means fewer units of this currency offered abroad, so the supply curve shifts left and the currency appreciates. The CED names tariffs or quotas in one breath for exactly this reason - the instrument differs, the effect on the currency does not.
An inflation differential
When prices here rise faster than prices abroad, this currency tends to depreciate.In real lifeA country where the cost of everything locally made has doubled in a decade while its trading partner’s prices barely moved, and whose currency now buys much less abroad.Higher inflation here makes home-made goods dear relative to imports, so residents buy more imports and offer more of this currency: supply right, and the currency depreciates. Foreigners also find this country’s exports dear and buy fewer, which pulls demand left - the two channels push the same way, which is why the exam treats a relative inflation rise as an unambiguous depreciation.
Speculation about a currency
An expectation that a currency will be worth more later is a reason to buy it now.In real lifeTraders who expect a central bank to raise rates next month buying that currency this week, and moving it before the announcement.A belief about a currency’s future value moves its demand today: expecting it to appreciate is a reason to hold it, and holding it means buying it. This is the one shifter in the unit whose cause is entirely about a future that has not happened, and it is why an exchange rate can move on an announcement about next year.
The direction rule, all four cases at once
Demand rightappreciates

Supply leftappreciates
The supply pair runs the opposite way from a goods market, because the thing being priced IS the thing being supplied
Learn these four as a block rather than deriving them under time pressure. Then the only work an exam item leaves is putting the event on the right curve, which is the part that is actually being tested.

6.4a Fiscal and monetary policy in an open economy

Two policy levers, and only one of them has a route the CED spells out. The difference is a single word, and it is the reason two policies both called expansionary can move a currency in opposite directions.

Fiscal policy and the exchange rate
Fiscal policy reaches the exchange rate by two routes that pull opposite ways.In real lifeA government spending package that raises incomes and prices at home while it also sends the government to the bond market to borrow the money.Fiscal policy can influence aggregate demand, real output, the price level, and exchange rates. The two routes are the reason a released stem always says which one it means. Through the price level: prices here rise, exports get dear, the currency weakens. Through the real interest rate: the government borrows, the rate rises, financial capital comes in, the currency strengthens. A stem saying “based solely on the change in real income” is choosing the first; one saying “as a result of the change in the interest rate” is choosing the second.
Monetary policy and the exchange rate
Monetary policy reaches the exchange rate through interest rates, and the CED says so outright.In real lifeA central bank cutting its policy rate, after which overseas savers move money out of that country’s bonds and its currency slips.Monetary policy can influence aggregate demand, real output, the price level, and interest rates, and thereby affect exchange rates. The word “thereby” is the whole difference from the fiscal statement: the route runs through the interest rate, so an expansionary cut lowers the return on this country’s assets, lowers demand for its currency, and depreciates it - which then raises net exports and adds to aggregate demand a second time.
On the exam

A central bank in an open economy with a flexible exchange rate conducts contractionary monetary policy. Based on the resulting change in interest rates, what happens to the international value of the currency and to the country’s exports?

  1. AThe currency appreciates and exports decrease
  2. BThe currency appreciates and exports increase
  3. CThe currency depreciates and exports decrease
  4. DThe currency depreciates and exports increase
Answer

A. Contractionary policy raises interest rates, which draws financial capital in; those buyers must acquire the currency, so demand for it rises and it appreciates. A stronger currency makes this country’s goods dearer to foreign buyers, so exports fall. Option B stops after the first step, which is the examined error in this unit.

The interest rate channel
A policy that moves the interest rate moves the currency the same way.In real lifeTwo announcements on the same morning - a rate rise here, no change abroad - and a currency that is stronger by lunchtime.Rates up here means this country’s assets pay relatively more, foreign savers buy them, and buying them means buying this currency first: demand right, currency up. Rates down runs the identical chain backwards. Both expansionary monetary policy and contractionary fiscal policy lower the rate, which is why two policies with opposite names can move a currency the same way.
The price level channel
A policy that raises the price level here makes this country’s goods dear and weakens its currency.In real lifeA country where a spending boom has pushed shop prices up, and whose neighbours have quietly stopped buying its exports.Prices here up means exports look expensive abroad and imports look cheap at home: fewer foreigners want this currency, more residents offer it, and it depreciates. This is the route a stem picks out when it says “based solely on the change in the price level” or “in real income”, and it is the route that runs the opposite way from the interest rate channel after an expansionary fiscal policy.
MKT-5.E.2 against MKT-5.E.3 — the word is “thereby”
Fiscal policyinfluences aggregate demand, real output, the price level, and exchange rates
Monetary policyinfluences all of those AND interest rates, and thereby exchange rates
Monetary policy has a named route; fiscal policy has two, and they disagree
Read them one after the other and the extra clause is easy to skim past. It is the whole content: an expansionary monetary policy has one mechanism - a lower interest rate - so its effect on the currency is unambiguous. An expansionary fiscal policy raises the price level, which weakens the currency, and raises the real interest rate through borrowing, which strengthens it.

Worked example — the same expansion, two stems A government increases spending without raising taxes, in an open economy with a flexible exchange rate.
Stem A: “based solely on the change in the price level”. Prices here rise, this country’s goods look dear to foreigners, demand for its currency falls, and it depreciates.
Stem B: “as a result of the change in the interest rate”. The government borrows, the real interest rate rises, financial capital flows in, those buyers need this currency, and it appreciates.
The check. The two answers are opposite and both are right, for the routes their stems named. If a stem names neither route, it is almost always naming the interest rate one by describing borrowing - and if it genuinely names neither, the currency direction is indeterminate and that is a real answer.
Trap — “expansionary policy weakens the currency” True of expansionary monetary policy, whose entire mechanism is a lower interest rate, and false of expansionary fiscal policy read through borrowing, which pushes the real interest rate up and pulls financial capital in. Two policies with the same adjective, two opposite currency movements. The adjective is not what decides it; what the policy does to the interest rate is.

6.5 What a currency movement does to net exports

This is the topic the whole unit is examined on, and it is one habit: after saying what the currency did, say what that did to exports and to imports. Both clauses, in the same answer, before drawing any conclusion about net exports.

Net exports (X − M)
Exports minus imports - one component of aggregate demand, and the one the exchange rate moves.also called the balance of trade, the trade balance or X − MAll four name the same number. None of them names the current account, which has two further components inside it.In real lifeA quarter in which a country sold $520 billion abroad and bought $610 billion in: net exports of −$90 billion.The variable at the end of every chain in this unit. It is a component of aggregate demand, so a change in it shifts AD and therefore changes output, employment and the price level - which is why an exchange rate question that begins in a currency market can finish in the AD-AS diagram. It is also the balance of trade, sitting inside the current account.
What an appreciation does to trade
A stronger currency makes exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home, so net exports fall.In real lifeAn American machinery firm losing European orders in a year when the dollar strengthened, without ever having raised its own price.Factors that cause a currency to appreciate cause that country’s exports to decrease and its imports to increase. As a result, net exports will decrease. Both halves move, in opposite directions, and the conclusion needs both: an answer that says only “a stronger currency hurts exporters” has given half the statement and will not survive a question about imports. Notice that no producer changed a price - the exchange rate did all of it, which is why an appreciation hurts exporters who did nothing.
On the exam

The dollar appreciates against the euro. A machine built in the United States is priced by its maker at 10,000 dollars in both years, and a bicycle built in Europe is priced by its maker at 400 euros in both years. Which of the following describes what happens to the two prices buyers actually face?

  1. AThe machine costs Europeans more, and the bicycle costs Americans less
  2. BThe machine costs Europeans less, and the bicycle costs Americans more
  3. CBoth goods cost their foreign buyers more, because the dollar is stronger
  4. DNeither price changes, because neither producer changed a price
Answer

A. A stronger dollar means a European needs more euros to buy the same dollar price, so the American export costs them more; and an American needs fewer dollars to buy the same euro price, so the European import costs less. Option D is the trap: the sticker prices really are unchanged, and the prices foreign buyers face still moved.

What a depreciation does to trade
A weaker currency makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, so net exports rise.In real lifeA country whose currency has slipped finding its tourism booming, because a week there now costs foreign visitors much less.Factors that cause a currency to depreciate cause that country’s exports to increase and its imports to decrease. As a result, net exports will increase. Since net exports are a component of aggregate demand, this is also the route by which a currency movement changes output, employment and the price level - and it is why a central bank cutting rates gets a second push on aggregate demand it did not have to legislate.
On the exam

The currency of the United Kingdom falls in value against other currencies. Which of the following sequences describes what most likely follows in the United Kingdom?

  1. AExports increase, imports decrease, net exports increase, and aggregate demand increases
  2. BExports decrease, imports increase, net exports decrease, and aggregate demand decreases
  3. CExports increase, imports increase, net exports are unchanged, and aggregate demand is unchanged
  4. DExports decrease, imports decrease, net exports increase, and aggregate demand increases
Answer

A. A weaker currency makes British goods cheaper to foreign buyers and foreign goods dearer to British buyers, so exports rise while imports fall and net exports rise. Net exports are a component of aggregate demand, so aggregate demand rises with them. Option B is the appreciation case, and the two are reliably confused when only the currency direction is read.

The price an export faces abroad
The home price converted at today’s rate - which moves even when the home price does not.In real lifeA 10,000 dollar machine that cost a European 8,000 euros last year and 10,000 euros this year, from the same American factory at the same price.Multiply the price in the producer’s own currency by the quote that converts it into the buyer’s. The arithmetic is one multiplication, and the reason it matters is that neither producer changed anything. This is the calculation that makes MKT-5.F.1 concrete rather than a slogan, and it is worth doing once with real numbers so that “exports become more expensive” is a result rather than a phrase.
The price an import faces at home
The foreign price converted at today’s rate - the other half of the same event.In real lifeA 400 euro bicycle that cost an American 500 dollars last year and 400 dollars this year, from the same European workshop at the same price.The mirror of the export calculation, and the half that gets left out. When a currency appreciates the two conversions move in opposite directions - the export dearer abroad, the import cheaper at home - which is exactly why net exports fall by more than either effect alone would suggest.
MKT-5.F.1 and MKT-5.F.2 — both halves move, in opposite directions
An appreciationexports decrease, imports increase, so net exports decrease
A depreciationexports increase, imports decrease, so net exports increase
And because net exports are part of aggregate demand, output and the price level follow
Neither statement is complete with one clause. An answer that says a stronger currency hurts exporters has given half of it, and half will not survive a question about imports - which is exactly how these items are usually written, because that is where the second mark is.

Worked example — two goods, no price change at all The rate moves from $1.25 per euro to $1.00 per euro, so the dollar appreciates from 0.80 to 1.00 euros.
A US-built machine priced by its maker at $10,000, in both years.
Year 1 cost to a European: $10,000 × 0.80 = 8,000 euros. Year 2: $10,000 × 1.00 = 10,000 euros. It got dearer, so fewer are exported.
A European bicycle priced by its maker at 400 euros, in both years.

The point is in the sentence after it, and that sentence has two clauses - exports become more expensive abroad and imports become cheaper at home - which together support the conclusion about net exports. Write all three parts every time, even when the question looks as if it only wants one.

6.6 Real interest rates and international capital flows

The last topic of the course, and it joins three markets that have been kept apart until now. It is also where this unit’s examined error is at its most expensive, because the chain is long and the mark is at the far end of it.

The real interest rate
The nominal rate minus inflation - what a lender actually earns, and the rate capital chases.In real lifeA savings account paying 9% in a country where prices are rising 7%: the saver ends the year with 2% more shopping, not 9%.In an open economy, differences in real interest rates across countries change the relative values of domestic and foreign assets. The word real is doing work: two countries can have the same nominal rate and completely different real returns, and a country with the higher nominal rate can have the lower real one - in which case financial capital flows the opposite way from the direction the headline rates suggest.
The interest rate differential
The GAP between two countries’ real rates, not the level of either one.In real lifeA fund manager comparing two government bond markets and moving money to whichever pays more after inflation - and moving it back if the other catches up.Financial capital will flow toward the country with the relatively higher interest rate. “Relatively” is the qualifier a question tests by moving both rates: a country whose real rate rose can still lose financial capital if its trading partner’s rose further, because what capital responds to is the gap. A stem that changes only one rate is the easy version; a stem that changes both is asking about this word.
On the exam

Country A pays a nominal interest rate of 9% with inflation of 7%. Country B pays a nominal interest rate of 6% with inflation of 1%. Toward which country will financial capital flow, and why?

  1. ACountry A, because its nominal interest rate is higher
  2. BCountry A, because its inflation rate is higher
  3. CCountry B, because its real interest rate of 5% exceeds Country A’s 2%
  4. DNeither, because the two countries’ nominal and real rankings disagree
Answer

C. Subtract inflation from each nominal rate: Country A offers a real 2% and Country B a real 5%. Financial capital follows the real rate, so it flows to B - the opposite direction from the nominal ranking, which is exactly why the statement says real.

A financial capital flow
Money crossing a border to buy assets, and it buys the destination’s currency on the way.In real lifeAn overseas pension fund switching a billion out of one country’s bonds into another’s after a rate decision.The link between the loanable funds market and the foreign exchange market, and it is a link rather than a graph: capital arriving has to be converted into the destination’s currency before it can buy anything there, so an inflow is a rise in the demand for that currency and an outflow is a rise in its supply. That conversion step is the whole reason a change in interest rates moves an exchange rate at all.
Net capital inflow
Foreign purchases of assets here minus purchases of foreign assets by residents.also called net financial capital inflow, net foreign investment or financial capital flowsWhatever it is called, it is money crossing a border to buy ASSETS - and it has to buy the destination’s currency on the way, which is why it moves an exchange rate.In real lifeA country whose bonds are being bought heavily from overseas while its own savers keep their money at home.The same quantity the capital and financial account balance reports, seen from the loanable funds side: a net inflow adds to the funds available for investment here, which is why an open economy can invest more than it saves. Central banks can influence the domestic interest rate in the short run, which in turn will affect net capital inflows - so this is the variable a monetary policy decision reaches first on its way to the exchange rate.
Short-run control of the interest rate
A central bank can move the domestic rate in the short run, and capital flows respond.In real lifeA rate announcement at two in the afternoon, and a currency measurably different by three.Central banks can influence the domestic interest rate in the short run, which in turn will affect net capital inflows. The time qualifier matters: this is a short-run lever, and the chain it starts - rate, capital flow, exchange rate, net exports - is a short-run chain. It is also why monetary policy in an open economy is more powerful than the closed-economy story suggests, because the currency route adds to the investment route rather than working against it.
The twin deficit chain
A budget deficit raises the real rate, pulls capital in, strengthens the currency and lowers net exports.In real lifeA government that borrows heavily for a spending program, and finds two years later that its exporters are complaining about a strong currency.Six steps, and the exam asks for the last three at least as often as the first three: the government borrows, the demand for loanable funds rises, the real interest rate rises, financial capital flows in, the currency appreciates, and net exports fall. Both accounts move in the same answer and in opposite directions, which is the balance of payments identity arriving from the other end - the capital coming in is the money the country then spends on imports.
On the exam

A government in an open economy with a flexible exchange rate increases its spending without raising taxes. Which of the following describes the effect on the real interest rate and on net exports?

  1. AThe real interest rate rises and net exports rise
  2. BThe real interest rate rises and net exports fall
  3. CThe real interest rate falls and net exports rise
  4. DThe real interest rate falls and net exports fall
Answer

B. Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds and with it the real interest rate. The higher rate draws financial capital in, those buyers must acquire this currency, so it appreciates - and a stronger currency makes exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home, so net exports fall. Option A is the answer that stops after the capital inflow and reads it as good news for trade.

Worked example — two countries, and then a change Country A pays a nominal 9% with inflation of 7%. Country B pays a nominal 6% with inflation of 1%.
Real rates: A is 9 − 7 = 2%; B is 6 − 1 = 5%.

Net exportsthe balance of trade, the trade balance or X − MAll four name the same number. None of them names the current account, which has two further components inside it.
The foreign exchange marketthe currency market, the FX market or the forex marketA question set in “the currency market for the peso” is set on exactly the graph this unit draws.
The exchange ratethe international value of the currency, the value of the currency or the price of one currency in another“The international value of the dollar” is the phrase released items use most, and it means the exchange rate. Value up is appreciation.
A flexible exchange ratea floating exchange rate, a freely floating rate or a market-determined rateA stem that opens “assume an open economy with a flexible exchange rate” is telling you the rate is free to move, which is what makes the shifts you draw change it.
Net capital inflownet financial capital inflow, net foreign investment or financial capital flowsWhatever it is called, it is money crossing a border to buy ASSETS - and it has to buy the destination’s currency on the way, which is why it moves an exchange rate.

Where this unit joins the rest of the course

Nothing in this unit stands on its own. It takes the real interest rate from Unit 4, hands net exports to Unit 3’s aggregate demand, and settles an accounting identity that constrains both - which is why the last topic of the course is also the one that reaches furthest back into it.

The four connections, stated once
From Unit 4the real interest rate, which is what capital chases across borders
To Unit 3net exports, a component of aggregate demand
Into Unit 4net capital inflow, added to the supply of loanable funds
Across Unit 5a policy mix whose currency effects are only visible here
Which is why a Unit 6 free-response question usually asks for two graphs from two different units
A released item in this unit rarely stays inside it. It starts in the loanable funds market or with a central bank decision, passes through the foreign exchange market, and finishes in the aggregate demand diagram - so the skill being tested is as much knowing which graph comes next as knowing how to draw any one of them.
The one habit to carry out of this unit Say the second step. Every chain in Unit 6 has a first link almost everybody writes - the account is in deficit, the currency appreciated, capital flowed in - and a second link that is where the mark is. What did that do to exports, and what did it do to imports? Answer both, in the same sentence, and then say what net exports did. That single habit is worth more on this unit than any graph on the page.
