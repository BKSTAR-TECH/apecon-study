2.1 Demand

Two words that sound identical and are not: demand, and quantity demanded. Nearly every mark lost in this topic is lost between them.

The distinction the whole unit rests on
Own pricemoves you ALONG the curve
Anything elseMOVES the curve
One curve either way — the question is whether it stayed put
A change in the good’s own price can never shift its own curve. Read that twice: it is the single most reliable fact in Unit 2, and it decides the answer to a large share of the multiple choice.

The exam does not always use those two words, and the substitutes are where the marks go.

A movement along the curvealso called a change in quantity demanded, a change in quantity supplied or sliding along the curveOnly a change in the good’s OWN price does this. Nothing else can.

A shift of the curvealso called a change in demand, a change in supply or the curve movingThe exam’s phrasing is the trap. “A change in DEMAND” means the curve shifted; “a change in QUANTITY demanded” means it did not. One word apart, opposite answers.

What buyers are responding to

Before the curve, three things the model quietly assumes. All three are examinable in a sentence, and all three are free marks.

Property rights
Knowing what is yours, and that it will stay yours.In real lifeA farmer who holds title to her land plants a crop that takes three years to mature. Without the title she plants something she can harvest before somebody else claims the field.A well-defined system of property rights is necessary for the market system to function well. If ownership is unclear or unenforceable there is nothing definite to trade, no reason to invest in something you may not keep, and no basis for a price. This sits at the front of Unit 2 because everything after it - buying, selling, contracting - quietly assumes it.
Incentives
Anything that makes an action more or less worth taking.In real lifeA deposit on a glass bottle. Nobody is ordered to return it; a small sum makes returning it worth the walk.Economic agents respond to incentives - the whole model rests on it. An incentive need not be money, and it need not be intended: a subsidy, a fine, a queue and a shortage are all incentives, and agents respond to the one they actually face rather than the one a policy meant to create.
Constraints
The limits you choose within - not just what you can afford.In real lifeSomeone offered a cheap midnight flight turns it down. The fare was affordable; the sleep before a 7 a.m. shift was not.Individuals often respond to incentives, such as those presented by prices, but also face constraints such as income, time, and legal and regulatory frameworks. The CED lists three kinds deliberately, and the two that are not money are the ones the exam uses: a stem where the cheapest option is rejected is usually a stem about a time or a legal constraint, not an irrational buyer.

The law of demand

Demand
The whole schedule: how much buyers would take at every price.In real lifeA coffee shop that knows it would sell 300 cups at $3, 240 at $4 and 190 at $5 knows the demand for its coffee. Not one number - the list.The entire relationship between price and the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy, over every price, holding everything else constant. Demand is a curve, never a number. The market demand curve comes from the summation of individual demand curves: at each price, add up what every buyer would take. This is why "demand rose" and "quantity demanded rose" are different claims - the first says the whole schedule moved, the second says buyers slid to a different point on the schedule they already had.
Quantity demanded
One number: what buyers take at one particular price.In real lifeAt $4 the coffee shop sells 240 cups. 240 is the quantity demanded at $4, and it says nothing about what happens at $3.The amount buyers are willing and able to buy at a specific price. It is a single point on the demand curve, so it can only change if the price changes - and when it does, the point moves along a curve that has not itself moved. Every AP answer that begins "quantity demanded" is describing a movement along the curve.
The law of demand
Price up, quantity demanded down. Always the opposite way.also called the inverse relationship between price and quantity demandedSome textbooks state it as a property of the curve rather than as a law. Same claim: own price up, quantity demanded down.In real lifeWhen a city raised its bus fare from $2 to $2.75, daily rides fell by about a tenth. Nobody had to be persuaded; the price did it.A change in a good’s own price causes a change in quantity demanded in the opposite direction, and a movement along the demand (marginal benefit) curve. Two things in that sentence are examinable on their own. Own price: the law is about the good’s own price and nothing else, so a change in any other price is a different event entirely. And the demand curve is also a marginal benefit curve - the height at each quantity is what the buyer of that unit is willing to pay for it, which is what makes the area under it a measure of benefit in topic 2.6.
Income effect
A lower price makes your money go further, so you buy more.In real lifePetrol drops from $6 to $4 a gallon. Your wage has not changed, but your weekly commute now leaves $20 in your pocket that it used to take - and you spend some of it.One of the two reasons the demand curve slopes down. When a price falls, the purchasing power of a fixed income rises, so the buyer can afford more of everything - including more of the good that got cheaper. The effect works on real income, not nominal: nobody paid you more, the money you already had simply buys more.
Substitution effect
A lower price makes the alternatives look worse, so you switch.In real lifeChicken drops to half the price of beef and the same shopper, with the same budget, comes home with chicken. Nothing about their income changed; the swap did.The second reason the demand curve slopes down. When a good gets cheaper relative to its alternatives, buyers move toward it and away from them. It is about relative prices, so it operates even if income is held perfectly constant - which is exactly what separates it from the income effect sitting beside it.
Diminishing marginal utility
The second slice is worth less to you than the first.In real lifeThe first cold drink after a run is worth almost anything. The fourth, you would rather not pay for.The third explanation the CED offers for the downward slope, and the one that connects Unit 2 back to Unit 1. Each extra unit adds less satisfaction than the one before, so a buyer will only take an extra unit at a lower price - which is the demand curve, read from the other direction. The CED gives the income and substitution effects and/or diminishing marginal utility: any of the three is a legitimate answer to "why does demand slope down", and a page that gave one would have left two marks on the table.
Why the curve slopes down — and why there are three answers The CED offers the income effect and substitution effect and/or diminishing marginal utility. That “and/or” is a gift: any of the three earns the point. What loses the point is naming one and describing another — the income effect is about what your money can buy, the substitution effect is about what else you could buy. If your explanation mentions a rival good, it is the substitution effect.

The line that separates themCount the goods in the explanation. One good and a wallet is the income effect. Two goods is the substitution effect. One good and a series of units is diminishing marginal utility. The CED accepts any of the three, so the only way to lose the mark is to name one and describe another.

What moves the whole curve

Determinants of demand
The things that move the whole curve, none of which is the good’s own price.In real lifeA news report linking a breakfast cereal to heart health moves every supermarket’s cereal sales at every price at once. The price on the shelf did not change first.Changes in the determinants of consumer demand cause the demand curve to shift. The exam’s five are tastes, income, the prices of related goods, expectations about future prices or income, and the number of buyers. What unites them is what is missing: the good’s own price is not on the list and can never be, because a change in own price is the one thing that moves you along the curve instead of moving the curve.
Normal good
You buy more of it when your income rises.In real lifeA pay rise, and the weekly takeaway becomes a restaurant meal. Restaurant meals are normal goods.A good whose demand increases when consumer income rises, and falls when income falls. Most goods are normal, which is why the case is easy to forget until a stem turns it around. Note the direction is about demand, the whole curve - a normal good’s curve shifts right on a pay rise at every price, not just at the price it was selling for.
Inferior good
You buy LESS of it when your income rises.In real lifeInstant noodles and long-distance coach tickets. Students buy them; the same students, five years into a career, buy fewer - not because the price rose, but because they can now afford otherwise.A good whose demand decreases when income rises, and increases when income falls. "Inferior" is a statement about how buyers respond to income, not about quality - an inferior good can be perfectly good. This is the single most reliable trick in the topic: a stem describing a recession and asking what happens to demand for a budget brand is testing whether you know an inferior good’s demand goes up when incomes go down.
Substitutes
Two goods that do the same job, so you swap one for the other.In real lifeButter and margarine. When butter’s price jumps, margarine sales rise - and margarine’s own price never moved.Two goods where a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other. Read the sentence carefully, because it moves between the two vocabularies on purpose: the price of butter changes quantity demanded of butter (a movement along butter’s curve) and demand for margarine (a shift of margarine’s curve). Which curve you are asked about decides which of the two things happened.
Complements
Two goods used together, so they rise and fall together.In real lifePrinters and ink cartridges. A collapse in printer prices sells more cartridges, at every cartridge price.Two goods where a rise in the price of one decreases demand for the other, because they are consumed as a pair. The same two-vocabulary care applies as with substitutes, and the sign is what separates them: for substitutes the two prices push the other good’s demand the same way, for complements the opposite way.

The line that separates themWhich way the two move together. Substitutes move in opposite directions — one gets dearer, you buy more of the other. Complements move in the same direction — one gets dearer, you buy less of both. Say the pair out loud with “instead of” and “along with”: whichever fits names the relationship.
The stem that catches everyone “The price of coffee rises. What happens in the market for coffee?” — quantity demanded falls, a movement.
“The price of coffee rises. What happens in the market for tea?” — demand for tea rises, a shift.
Same sentence, same price change, and the answers are different kinds of thing. The word that decides it is the one naming which market you are standing in: a price is the own price of exactly one good.

2.2 Supply

Everything you just learned about demand applies to supply, with one sign reversed. That is the whole topic, and it is worth being precise about which one.

Supply
The whole schedule: how much sellers would offer at every price.In real lifeA grower who would cut 200 stems at $1, 320 at $1.50 and 500 at $2 has described the supply of her flowers. Again - the list, not one number.The entire relationship between price and the quantity sellers are willing and able to produce, over every price, holding everything else constant. Like demand, the market supply curve is the summation of individual supply curves, and like demand it is upward-sloping: higher prices cover the cost of drawing in production that was not worth doing before.
Quantity supplied
One number: what sellers offer at one particular price.In real lifeAt $1.50 the grower cuts 320 stems. 320 is the quantity supplied at $1.50 and nothing else.The amount sellers are willing and able to sell at a specific price - a single point on the supply curve. It changes only when the good’s own price changes, and when it does the point moves along a curve that has not moved. The vocabulary is deliberately parallel to the demand side, because the exam tests the same distinction twice.
The law of supply
Price up, quantity supplied up. The same way, not the opposite way.also called the direct relationship between price and quantity supplied or the positive relationship“Direct” and “positive” both mean the same way, and both turn up in stems as the opposite of demand’s “inverse”.In real lifeWhen wheat prices doubled, land that had been left to pasture was ploughed and planted. The land was always there; at the old price it was not worth using.A change in own price causes a change in quantity supplied in the same direction, and a movement along the supply curve. This is the one place where the supply side is not a mirror of the demand side, and it is the sign that has to be memorised: demand moves opposite, supply moves the same way. Everything else about the two - point against curve, own price against determinants - is identical.
The one difference Demand moves opposite. Supply moves the same way. Everything else is identical: own price moves you along the curve, a determinant moves the curve, quantity supplied is a point and supply is the whole schedule. Only the direction differs, because a higher price is a worse deal for a buyer and a better one for a seller.
The two laws, against each other
Own price risesOwn price fallsSlope
The law of demandQuantity demanded fallsQuantity demanded risesDownward
The law of supplyQuantity supplied risesQuantity supplied fallsUpward
The line that separates themOnly the sign. A higher price is a worse deal for whoever is paying it and a better one for whoever is collecting it, so buyers retreat and sellers advance. Everything else about the two — point against curve, own price against determinants — is word for word the same, which is why the exam can test the same distinction twice in one paper.

What moves the supply curve

Determinants of supply
The things that move the whole supply curve, none of which is own price.In real lifeA drought does not wait for the price of wheat to change. It reduces what every farm can offer, at every price, at once.Changes in the determinants of supply cause the supply curve to shift. The exam’s set is input prices, technology, the prices of other goods a seller could produce, expectations, and the number of sellers - plus taxes and subsidies, which topic 2.8 treats in their own right. As on the demand side, the good’s own price is not among them and cannot be.
Input prices
What it costs the seller to make the thing.In real lifeThe price of aluminium rises and every drinks canner’s costs rise with it - so at any given can price, fewer cans get made.The prices of the resources used in production. They move supply in the opposite direction to themselves: dearer inputs shift supply left, cheaper inputs shift it right. This is the determinant the exam uses most, usually disguised as a wage rise, an energy price or a new tariff on a component.
Technology
A better way of making it, so more gets made at any price.In real lifeDrip irrigation lets the same field of tomatoes be grown with a third of the water. Nothing about the tomato price changed; the farm can simply offer more of them.Any improvement in the method of production. It lowers the cost of each unit, so it shifts supply right - and it is the one determinant that essentially never shifts supply left, because knowledge is not usually lost. A stem describing a new process, a better machine or a smarter layout is describing a rightward supply shift, whatever words it uses.
What moves the supply curve, and which way
DeterminantA rise in it shifts supplyWhyStem language
Input pricesLeftEvery unit costs more to makeWages, energy, materials, a component tariff
TechnologyRightEvery unit costs less to makeA new process, a better machine, automation
The line that separates themBoth act on the cost of making one more unit, and supply shifts away from cost. Dearer inputs push it left, better technology pushes it right — and technology essentially never pushes left, because a way of doing something is not usually forgotten.
Trap — the direction of an input price An input price and supply move opposite ways. Wages up means supply left. Students who have just learned that supply slopes upward often shift it right on any price rise, because a price went up. The price that slopes the curve upward is the price of the output; the price in this determinant is the price of an input, and it is a cost.
Worked example — sorting four events A bakery. Which of these moves the bread supply curve, and which slides along it?
Flour becomes dearer. Input price - supply shifts left.
Bread’s price rises. Own price - a movement along, up the curve. Quantity supplied rises; supply does not.
A new oven halves baking time. Technology - supply shifts right.
Two more bakeries open in the street. Number of sellers - market supply shifts right.
Only the second is a movement, and it is the only one where the thing that changed was the price of bread itself.

2.3 Price elasticity of demand

Everything so far has said which way quantity moves. Elasticity says how far — and that number decides most of the rest of the unit, from who bears a tax to what a price cut does to a firm’s takings.

Elasticity
A responsiveness number: how hard one thing reacts when another moves.In real lifeTwo gyms each raise the monthly fee by a tenth. One loses 4% of its members; the other, with a rival across the road, loses a quarter. Same price rise, two very different reactions - and elasticity is the number that says so.A measure of how much one quantity responds to a change in another, written as one percentage divided by another. Economists measure it for a change in the good’s own price, in income, and in the prices of related goods. Because both halves are percentages the answer carries no units, so it is the same number whether the good is priced in dollars or in pesos and sold in kilos or in tons - which is exactly what a slope cannot do, and the reason elasticity exists.
Price elasticity of demand
How much quantity demanded moves when the good’s own price moves.also called own-price elasticity of demand, PED or EdTextbooks differ over the minus sign: some print the negative value, some print the absolute value and say so, and some quietly drop it. All three describe the same demand. Compare the magnitude against 1 and you are safe whichever convention a question uses.In real lifeA transit authority raises a fare from $1.25 to $1.50 and rides fall by about a twentieth. A large price rise, a small change in behaviour - and the authority ends the month with more money, not less.The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price: the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good’s own price. Two habits keep it straight. Take the magnitude - the law of demand makes the raw number negative, so the exam compares its absolute value against 1, and the minus sign is the law of demand showing up rather than a mistake. And hold on to this sentence, because it is where the marks are: elasticity varies along a straight demand curve although its slope never changes, so slope is not elasticity. High on the curve the price is large and the quantity small, so a given price cut is a small percentage against a large quantity response - elastic. Low down the arithmetic reverses.
On the exam

A firm sells on the straight-line demand curve in the figure and is currently selling a quantity well short of the one marked QR. If it lowers its price a little, what happens to the magnitude of the price elasticity of demand and to total revenue?

  1. AThe magnitude is greater than 1, and total revenue rises.
  2. BThe magnitude is greater than 1, and total revenue falls.
  3. CThe magnitude is less than 1, and total revenue rises.
  4. DThe magnitude is less than 1, and total revenue falls.
Answer

A. Above the midpoint of a linear demand curve demand is elastic, so the magnitude exceeds 1. On the elastic stretch quantity moves proportionally more than price, so cutting the price raises total revenue. The slope of the line is the same everywhere on it; the elasticity is not, which is the point of the figure.

Elastic demand
Quantity moves proportionally MORE than price. The magnitude is above 1.In real lifeOne airline puts its fare up a tenth on a route four carriers fly. Bookings drop by a third, because every traveller has three other ways to make the same journey.Demand is elastic with respect to that price, over the range of the given change, when the magnitude of the elasticity is greater than 1: a one per cent price change moves quantity demanded by more than one per cent. Both qualifiers matter and the exam leans on them - elastic is not a property a good has for ever, it is a verdict about one stretch of one curve at one price.
Inelastic demand
Quantity moves proportionally LESS than price. The magnitude is below 1.In real lifeInsulin. A five per cent price rise does not persuade anyone to use five per cent less of it, because nothing else does the job and the dose is not a matter of taste.Demand is inelastic when the magnitude of the elasticity is less than 1: quantity demanded does respond, but by proportionally less than the price moved. Inelastic never means no response at all - that is the perfectly inelastic case, and it is a different answer on a multiple-choice list. A stem describing a necessity with no close substitute, bought out of a small share of income, is describing inelastic demand.
Unit elastic demand
Price and quantity move by exactly the same percentage. The magnitude is 1.In real lifeA market stall drops its price by a tenth and sells a tenth more. It takes exactly the same money home as it did the day before.The separating benchmark. Where the magnitude equals 1 the change in the price and the change in the quantity demanded are proportional, and demand is unit elastic with respect to that price over that range. It is a dividing line rather than a third kind of good, and it is also the exact point at which total revenue stops rising and starts falling - which is why the exam more often asks for the quantity that maximises revenue than for the elasticity itself.
Perfectly elastic demand
A horizontal demand curve: one price is the only price.In real lifeOne wheat farm among thousands. It sells every bushel it grows at the going price and not one bushel a cent above it, because the buyer’s next call gets identical wheat.The limiting case where the elasticity is infinite. Buyers take any quantity at the ruling price and nothing at all above it, so the curve is a horizontal line. This is the demand curve a single firm faces in perfect competition, which is why Unit 3 opens with it - and it is still a demand curve, even though it looks nothing like the downward slope of the market it belongs to.
Perfectly inelastic demand
A vertical demand curve: the same quantity at every price.In real lifeAn emergency dose of antivenom. The quantity needed is set by the bite, not by the price on the box.The other limiting case: the elasticity is zero and the curve is vertical, so quantity demanded does not respond to price at all. It matters far beyond its realism, because it is one end of the incidence argument in 2.8 - put a per-unit tax on a good with perfectly inelastic demand and buyers bear every cent of it while the quantity traded does not move.
The midpoint method
Divide each change by the AVERAGE of the two values, so the answer is the same in both directions.In real lifeA price going from $4 to $5 has risen a quarter; coming back from $5 to $4 it has fallen a fifth. Same journey, two numbers - which is the untidiness this method removes.Each percentage change is taken as the change divided by the average of the starting and ending values, rather than by the starting value alone. The result is one elasticity for the range instead of one number going up and a different one coming down. Most exam items set up the simple starting-value calculation and accept it; the midpoint answer is the one to reach for when a stem hands you two price-quantity pairs and does not say which came first.
Total revenue
Price times quantity sold. What comes in, before any cost goes out.In real lifeA cafe selling 240 flat whites at $4 takes $960 that morning. Whether $5 would have been better depends entirely on how many it would then have sold.The money a seller collects: price multiplied by the quantity actually sold. Seen from the other side of the counter the same number is total expenditure, which is why the CED names both. Because a price change moves the two factors in opposite directions, whether total revenue rises or falls is never readable from the price change alone - it turns on which factor moves proportionally more, and that is precisely what elasticity measures.
The total revenue test
Watch what a price change does to revenue and you have read the elasticity backwards.also called the total expenditure test or the revenue testThe CED itself names revenue and expenditure together, because they are the same number seen from the two sides of the counter. A stem about consumer spending is a stem about total revenue.In real lifeA convenience store raises every price at once. Money taken on coffee goes up; money taken on soft drinks goes down. Without computing anything the owner now knows which of the two has elastic demand.The impact of a given price change on total revenue depends on whether demand is elastic, inelastic or unit elastic - and because the relationship runs both ways, it can be used as a test. Price and revenue moving in opposite directions means demand is elastic. Moving in the same direction means demand is inelastic. Revenue not moving at all means unit elastic. The exam uses it from both ends: sometimes it gives the elasticity and asks about revenue, sometimes it reports what happened to revenue and asks you to name the elasticity.
On the exam

A bakery raises the price of its loaves and finds that the total revenue it earns from loaves has fallen. Which of the following must be true of the demand for its loaves over that price range?

  1. AIt is perfectly inelastic.
  2. BIt is inelastic.
  3. CIt is unit elastic.
  4. DIt is elastic.
Answer

D. Price rose and revenue fell, so the two moved in opposite directions - the elastic case, in which quantity fell by proportionally more than the price rose. Perfectly inelastic demand would have left quantity unchanged and revenue higher; unit elastic demand would have left revenue exactly where it was.

The sentence that earns the marks Elasticity varies along a straight demand curve. Its slope does not. A linear demand curve has one slope from end to end and runs through every elasticity there is: elastic near the top, unit elastic once in the middle, inelastic near the bottom. So “the flatter curve is the more elastic one” is only true when you compare the two at the same point, which is exactly how the figure draws it. Elasticity is a property of a point.

Two habits keep it clean: compare the magnitude, because the raw number is negative for every ordinary demand curve, and remember the verdict belongs to a stretch of one curve, not to the good — the same curve is elastic at the top and inelastic at the bottom.

Why the number is negative, and why nobody says so

The law of demand guarantees that price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so the raw calculation always comes out negative. The exam compares the magnitude — the absolute value — against 1, and an answer choice reading “−2.5” and one reading “2.5” are describing the same demand. Getting a negative number is the law of demand confirming itself, not an error to hunt for.

Worked example — the arithmetic, twice A store raises a price from $200 to $220 and quantity demanded falls by 20 per cent.
The percentage price change: $20 on $200 is 10 per cent.
The elasticity: 20 divided by 10 is 2. Above 1, so demand is elastic over that range.
And the revenue check: price rose, demand is elastic, so total revenue fell. Quantity gave up a fifth to buy a tenth on price, which is a bad trade.

What decides whether demand is elastic

Availability of substitutes
The more other things will do the job, the more elastic demand is.In real lifePut the price of one brand of bottled water up and shoppers reach for the bottle beside it. Put the price of all bottled water up and far fewer of them go without.The price elasticity of demand depends on certain factors, and the first of them is the availability of substitutes. Where a buyer can switch, a price rise loses the sale; where nothing else will do, the buyer pays. Every other determinant in this block is a variation on it - a necessity is a good with no substitute worth having, and a narrowly defined market is one with a substitute on the next shelf.
Share of income
The bigger the hole it makes in a budget, the more elastic demand is.In real lifeTable salt doubles in price and nobody changes their shopping. A car doubles in price and almost everybody does, though both are things people want.A good taking a large share of a buyer’s income is one whose price is worth thinking about, so buyers shop around, delay, and go without - which makes demand more elastic. A good costing a rounding error is not worth the thought, so its demand is inelastic almost regardless of substitutes. This is why so many of the exam’s inelastic examples are cheap things.
Necessity or luxury
Necessities are inelastic; things that can simply be skipped are elastic.In real lifeNobody postpones a prescription because the pharmacy raised its dispensing fee. Plenty of people postpone a holiday because the airline raised its fare.A necessity is bought whatever the price, so quantity barely responds; a purchase that can be deferred or dropped responds sharply. Take care with the labels, because they describe behaviour rather than morals: the same good can be a necessity to one buyer and a luxury to another, and the exam expects the reading the stem sets up.
Time to adjust
Demand is more elastic the longer buyers have to react.In real lifePetrol doubles on Monday and the same drivers make the same trips that week. Two years later, some of them are in smaller cars, on a bus, or living closer to work.In the short run a buyer is stuck with the car, the boiler and the commute they already have, so quantity demanded moves very little. Given time, substitutes can be found or built and demand becomes more elastic. Any stem that sets a short-run answer against a long-run one is testing this and nothing else.
How the market is defined
The narrower the market, the more elastic the demand for it.In real lifeDemand for one supermarket’s own-brand cereal is very elastic. Demand for breakfast cereal in general is much less so. Demand for food is barely elastic at all.Elasticity depends on how widely the good is drawn, because a narrow definition leaves the substitutes outside it. This is the availability of substitutes seen from the other end, and the exam uses it as a trap: a question about one firm’s demand and a question about the whole market’s demand can have opposite answers about the same product.

2.4 Price elasticity of supply

The same idea, the same arithmetic, and the same benchmark of 1 — on the other side of the market. The one thing that changes is the sign.

Price elasticity of supply
How much quantity supplied moves when the price moves.In real lifeA price rise from $4 to $5 a ton takes peanut growers from 40 tons a week to 60. Quantity rose by half against a price rise of a quarter, so supply responded twice as hard as the price moved.The percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price - the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. The arithmetic is the demand side’s, with one difference worth saying out loud: the law of supply makes the number positive, so there is no magnitude to take. Compare it against 1 exactly as you would for demand.
On the exam

The price elasticity of supply of a good is 2. If the price of the good rises by 10 percent, the quantity supplied will change by which of the following?

  1. AIt will decrease by 20 percent.
  2. BIt will increase by 5 percent.
  3. CIt will increase by 12 percent.
  4. DIt will increase by 20 percent.
Answer

D. Elasticity is the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price, so the quantity change is the elasticity times the price change: 2 multiplied by 10 percent is 20 percent. Supply obeys the law of supply, so a price rise raises quantity supplied - an increase, not a decrease.

Elastic supply
Quantity supplied moves proportionally more than price. Above 1.In real lifeA T-shirt printer with idle machines and shelves of blank stock. A better price this week and output doubles by Friday, because nothing had to be built first.Supply is elastic with respect to that price, in the range of the given change, when the elasticity is greater than 1. The conditions are practical rather than theoretical - spare capacity, inputs that can be bought quickly, output that stores, and enough time. Where they hold, sellers can chase a price rise; where they do not, they simply cannot.
Inelastic supply
Quantity supplied moves proportionally less than price. Below 1.In real lifeBeachfront land. Prices treble and not one extra metre of coastline appears; where new supply is possible at all it takes years to arrive.The elasticity is less than 1: sellers would like to supply more and cannot, at least not yet. Growing seasons, fixed factories, land, trained specialists and anything perishable all produce inelastic supply. When a stem puts a supply elasticity and a demand elasticity side by side, the inelastic one is nearly always the point of the question.
Price of alternative inputs
A seller who can swap one input for another can respond to a price more easily.In real lifeA frozen-meal maker can use shrimp or chicken. When shrimp gets dear it uses more chicken and keeps making meals - so its output does not have to fall the way it would if only shrimp would do.The price elasticity of supply depends on certain factors such as the price of alternative inputs. A producer who can substitute between inputs, or switch a line between outputs, expands and contracts cheaply and so responds more to a price change. A producer locked to one input at one price cannot, however much it would like to sell more, and its supply is inelastic as a result.
Spare capacity
Idle machines and unused hours make supply elastic; a full plant makes it inelastic.In real lifeA stadium already selling every seat cannot supply one more at any price. The same stadium half empty can supply hundreds without spending a cent.How close a producer sits to its limit decides how easily it can answer a higher price. With room to spare, extra output costs little more per unit and supply is elastic; at the limit, extra output means new plant, and short-run supply is close to vertical. This is also the bridge into Unit 3 - a supply curve steepens near capacity because marginal cost rises there.
Time, on the supply side
Supply is more elastic the longer producers have to respond.In real lifeCoffee prices jump and this year’s harvest is what it is. New trees bear in three or four years - by which time supply has answered the price after all.Time is the determinant that matters most on the supply side, because building capacity takes it. In the very short run supply can be nearly vertical; over a long enough horizon almost every supply curve becomes elastic. A stem that separates a short-run answer from a long-run one in a supply question is separating exactly this.
Every difference between the two elasticities, in one line Demand’s number is negative. Supply’s is positive. Nothing else differs. Numerator, denominator, the benchmark of 1, the three verdicts and the two limiting cases are word for word the same on both sides. That is why an item asking for a supply elasticity looks exactly like one asking for a demand elasticity, and why the only thing you have to remember separately is that there is no magnitude to take.

2.5 Other elasticities

Elasticity is a shape of question, not a single formula. Divide a percentage change in a quantity by a percentage change in anything and you have measured a responsiveness.

The general form Elasticity = % change in a quantity ÷ % change in whatever moved it. The CED puts it plainly: elasticity can be measured for any determinant of demand or supply, not just the price. The two the exam names are income and the price of a related good, and both are calculated exactly the way price elasticity is. Only the denominator changes.
Income elasticity of demand
How demand responds to a change in income - and the sign is the answer.In real lifeIncomes in a city rise over a year. Restaurant meals are up and long-distance coach tickets are down. One of those numbers is positive and the other is negative, and that is the whole classification.The percentage change in the quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in consumers’ income. Economists use it to decide whether a good is normal or inferior, and the sign does that on its own: positive is normal, because more income buys more of it, and negative is inferior, because more income buys less. The magnitude adds a second reading the exam sometimes wants - a normal good whose income elasticity is above 1 is one people buy disproportionately more of as they get richer.
On the exam

The income elasticity of demand for a good is negative. Consumers’ incomes then rise. Other things equal, which of the following happens in the market for that good?

  1. ADemand increases, raising both equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.
  2. BDemand decreases, lowering both equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.
  3. CQuantity demanded increases along an unchanged demand curve.
  4. DSupply decreases, raising price and lowering quantity.
Answer

B. A negative income elasticity identifies an inferior good, so a rise in income shifts its demand curve to the left. A leftward demand shift along an upward-sloping supply curve lowers price and quantity together. Income is not the good’s own price, so this is a shift of demand and never a movement along it.

Cross-price elasticity of demand
How demand for one good responds to the price of another - and again the sign is the answer.In real lifeThe price of mushrooms rises, and more asparagus is bought. The two are substitutes, and the number that says so comes out positive.The percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good divided by the percentage change in the price of another good. Economists use it to decide whether goods are substitutes, complements, or not related. Positive means substitutes - the other good got dearer and buyers came here instead. Negative means complements - the other good got dearer and buyers took less of both. And a value of zero means the two are not related, which is a third answer rather than a missing one, and it is the option students read past.
Unrelated goods
A cross-price elasticity of zero. One price says nothing about the other good.In real lifeThe price of printer ink and the demand for garden hoses. Nothing connects them, and a shopper who buys both connects them only in the trolley.Two goods are unrelated when a change in the price of one leaves demand for the other where it was, so their cross-price elasticity is zero. It is worth having a name for, because multiple-choice lists routinely offer it and a student who learned only that positive means substitutes and negative means complements reads zero as a broken calculation rather than as the answer.

2.6 Market equilibrium, consumer surplus and producer surplus

Put the two curves on one pair of axes and they answer a question neither can answer alone: what is the price? And once there is a price, there is a way to measure what the market is worth to the people in it.

The supply and demand model
Two curves that between them explain what a price is and why it moved.In real lifeWhy the same hotel room costs $90 in February and $400 during a festival. Nothing about the building changed; one of the two curves did.A tool for understanding what influences prices and quantities, and why both differ between markets and change over time. Its power is that it separates the two sides of every story, so a question about a price becomes a question about which curve moved and in which direction. Everything else in Unit 2 - elasticity, surplus, price controls, taxes, trade - is drawn on top of this one picture.
Market equilibrium
The price at which buyers want exactly the quantity sellers want to supply.In real lifeA market stall that goes home with nothing unsold and turned nobody away. It found the price the two sides agreed on.In a perfectly competitive market, equilibrium is reached when the price of the good brings quantity supplied and quantity demanded into balance - buyers wish to buy exactly what sellers wish to provide, so markets clear and there is neither a shortage nor a surplus. It is a point on a graph and a sentence in words, and the exam asks for both. Note what it does not claim: equilibrium says nothing about whether a price is fair, affordable or high, only that at that price nobody is left wanting a trade they cannot get.
Equilibrium price
The market-clearing price - and a signal telling everyone where to put their resources.In real lifeWhen lithium prices climbed, nobody had to instruct mining companies to go looking for lithium. The price said it, and it said it to everybody at once.The price at which quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. Its second job is the one the CED singles out: equilibrium price provides information to economic decision-makers and so guides resource allocation. A high price says more of this is wanted here; a low one says put your effort somewhere else. That is the answer to Unit 1’s question of who decides what gets produced in a market system - nobody does, and the price does.
Equilibrium quantity
The quantity traded once the price has settled.In real lifeThe 700 apartments actually rented in a town this month, at the rent where nobody is queueing and nothing stands empty.The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price, read off the horizontal axis under the intersection. Away from equilibrium the quantity actually traded is the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied - the short side of the market rules, because no trade happens unless both sides agree to it. That single sentence answers a large share of the price-control questions in 2.8.
What equilibrium does and does not claim
Clearsno shortage, no surplus
Signalsthe price tells resources where to go

And none of the three says the price is fair, low, or affordable
The third pillar carries a condition that is easy to skim past and is examined directly: it holds in the absence of market failures. Where there is an externality, market power or missing information, the market still clears and still signals, and it no longer maximises surplus. That is what Unit 6 is about.

Measuring what the market is worth

Willingness to pay
The most a buyer would have paid rather than go without.In real lifeSomebody who would have paid $25 for a remote control finds it on the shelf at $18. The $25 was never printed anywhere; it is simply what the remote was worth to them.The maximum price a particular buyer would accept for a particular unit, which is that unit’s marginal benefit to them. The demand curve is that list of numbers ranked from highest to lowest, which is both why it slopes down and why the height of the demand curve at any quantity is the willingness to pay for that unit.
Consumer surplus
What buyers would have paid, minus what they did pay.In real lifeThe buyer willing to pay $25 who pays $18 walks away $7 better off and never sees an invoice for it. Add that up across every buyer in the market and you have consumer surplus.The difference between what buyers are willing to pay for the units they buy and what they actually pay. On the graph it is the area below the demand curve and above the price, out to the quantity traded. It exists because everybody pays the same market price while the units are worth different amounts to different buyers, so all but the very last buyer gets back more than they gave up.
Willingness to accept
The least a seller would take rather than not sell.In real lifeA carpenter who would rather keep a chair than sell it below $60. At $75 he sells, and the $15 is his.The minimum price a seller would accept for a particular unit, which is that unit’s marginal cost of production. The supply curve is those numbers ranked from lowest to highest, which is why it slopes up: the cheap units get made first, and every further unit needs a higher price to be worth making at all.
Producer surplus
What sellers received, minus the least they would have accepted.In real lifeThe carpenter’s $15 on that chair, and the same arithmetic on every other chair he sold that month.The difference between the price sellers receive and the lowest price they would have accepted, added up over the units traded. On the graph it is the area above the supply curve and below the price. Economists use consumer surplus and producer surplus together to measure the benefit a market creates for each side and to judge how well the market is working - which is what makes them the measuring stick for every intervention in 2.8 and 2.9.
Total economic surplus
Consumer surplus plus producer surplus: the whole gain the market creates.In real lifeEvery trade in a market leaves both sides better off than not trading at all. Total surplus is that pile of small gains, counted once.The sum of consumer and producer surplus - the total gain from trade in a market. Market equilibrium maximises it in the absence of market failures, which is what "perfectly competitive markets are efficient" means. That qualifier is not decoration, and the exam knows it: the claim holds for a market with no externality, no market power and no missing information, and Unit 6 is entirely about the markets where it does not.
On the exam

The figure shows a perfectly competitive market with no externalities, in equilibrium at P* and Q*. Which of the following is true of total economic surplus there?

  1. AIt is smaller than at any quantity above Q*, because more trade always creates more surplus.
  2. BIt is as large as it can be, because every unit worth more to a buyer than it costs a seller is traded and no other unit is.
  3. CIt equals consumer surplus alone, because producer surplus is a cost borne by buyers.
  4. DIt cannot be determined without knowing the price elasticities of demand and supply.
Answer

B. Every unit up to Q* has a marginal benefit above its marginal cost, so trading it adds surplus; every unit past Q* costs more than it is worth, so trading it would subtract surplus. Producing beyond Q* therefore lowers total surplus rather than raising it. Producer surplus is part of total surplus, not a deduction from it, and the elasticities change the size of the triangles without changing where the maximum is.

Allocative efficiency
Producing exactly the quantity at which the last unit is worth what it cost.In real lifeA bakery that stops baking at the loaf whose flour and labour cost about what the next customer would pay for it. One loaf further and the effort is worth less than it took.The condition in which the marginal benefit of the last unit equals its marginal cost, so resources are producing the things people value most. In a competitive market that happens at equilibrium, because the demand curve is the marginal benefit curve and the supply curve is the marginal cost curve, so where they cross the two are equal. It is the same MB = MC rule Unit 1 applied to a single decision, applied here to a whole market.

2.7 Disequilibrium, and one curve moving

Two questions live in this topic, and they are usually asked one after the other: what happens when the price is wrong, and what happens when something moves a curve.

Disequilibrium
Any price at which the two sides of the market disagree - and it does not last.In real lifeA concert that sells out in four minutes was priced below equilibrium, and the resale listings that appear within the hour are the market moving the price to where it was always going.A state in which quantity demanded and quantity supplied are not equal, so the market carries a shortage or a surplus. The examinable half is what happens next: market forces drive price and quantity toward equilibrium. A surplus puts unsold stock in front of sellers and they cut the price; a shortage puts buyers in a queue and sellers discover they can charge more. Nothing has to be organised for any of it to happen, which is the point.
Shortage
Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. The price is below equilibrium.In real lifeFlashlights the day after a storm. At the old price everyone wants one, and the shelves are bare within hours.The gap when a price sits below equilibrium: buyers want more than sellers will offer, so the quantity actually traded is the quantity supplied. Left alone, the price rises and the gap closes. Held down by law it cannot, which is exactly what a binding price ceiling does - so a shortage is not by itself evidence of a policy, but a lasting one usually is.
Surplus
Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. The price is above equilibrium.In real lifeRacks of unsold winter coats in March, with the sale signs already going up. The price was above what anybody would now pay.The gap when a price sits above equilibrium: sellers offer more than buyers will take, so the quantity actually traded is the quantity demanded and the rest goes unsold. The price falls until the gap disappears. Do not confuse this surplus with consumer or producer surplus - the exam uses one word for an unsold quantity and for a welfare gain, and only the sentence around it says which is meant.
A double shift
Both curves move at once, and one of price or quantity becomes unpredictable.In real lifeSmartphones in a year when incomes rise and manufacturing gets cheaper. More get sold for certain - but whether the price ends up higher or lower depends on which change was the bigger.Factors that shift market demand and market supply change price, quantity, consumer surplus, producer surplus and total economic surplus within that market. When both curves move, one variable has a determinate direction and the other does not, and which is which follows from the two directions alone. How large any of these effects is depends on the price elasticities of demand and supply: the same shift against an inelastic curve moves price a long way and quantity hardly at all, and against an elastic curve does the reverse.
Indeterminate
A real AP answer: the direction cannot be known without the sizes.In real lifeOne force pushing a price up and another pushing it down, and nobody has said which push is harder. The honest answer is that you cannot tell.When two shifts push one variable in opposite directions, the outcome depends on their relative sizes, which a typical stem does not give. "Cannot be determined" is then correct rather than a cop-out, and it is offered precisely because students talk themselves out of it. The trap is a graph drawn with two equal shifts, in which the variable happens to land where it started: equal shifts are the drawer’s choice and not a result, so reading that picture as "unchanged" is wrong.
On the exam

In the market shown in the figure, demand increases and supply decreases at the same time. Which of the following describes the effect on equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity?

  1. APrice rises; quantity rises.
  2. BPrice rises; quantity cannot be determined.
  3. CPrice cannot be determined; quantity rises.
  4. DPrice falls; quantity cannot be determined.
Answer

B. Both changes push price upward, so price rises for certain. They push quantity in opposite directions - higher demand raises it, lower supply lowers it - so the quantity outcome turns on which shift is larger, which the question does not say. The two shifts are drawn the same size, but that is a choice of drawing rather than a result, which is why the quantity is marked with a range instead of a value.

2.8 Government intervention: price and quantity controls

A control is a price the government insists on. Whether it does anything at all depends on one comparison: is it on the far side of equilibrium from the market, or the same side?

Price ceiling
A legal maximum price. It bites only if it is set BELOW equilibrium.In real lifeRent control. A city caps what a landlord may charge, and the flats that do come up are gone the day they are listed.A government-imposed maximum price. Set below the equilibrium price it is binding: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, so there is a lasting shortage, the quantity traded falls to the quantity supplied, and non-price rationing - queues, waiting lists, who you know - does the job the price used to do. Set above equilibrium it changes nothing at all, because the market was already clearing at a legal price. Both cases are examined, and it is the harmless one students answer wrongly.
On the exam

A government imposes on this competitive market the price ceiling drawn in the figure. Which of the following will occur?

  1. AA shortage develops and the quantity traded falls.
  2. BA surplus develops and the quantity traded falls.
  3. CNothing changes: the market goes on clearing at its equilibrium price and quantity.
  4. DThe price rises to the ceiling and the quantity traded rises.
Answer

C. The ceiling is drawn above the equilibrium price, so it is not binding: the equilibrium price is already legal and nothing in the market has any reason to move. A ceiling causes a shortage only when it sits below equilibrium, and a ceiling can never push a price up, because it is a maximum rather than a minimum.

Price floor
A legal minimum price. It bites only if it is set ABOVE equilibrium.In real lifeA minimum wage. In a labour market the floor sits under the wage, and where it binds, more people want work at that wage than employers want to hire.A government-imposed minimum price. Set above the equilibrium price it is binding: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, so there is a lasting surplus and the quantity traded falls to the quantity demanded. Set below equilibrium it does nothing. In a labour market that surplus has a name - unemployment - and the axes become the wage and the quantity of labour, but the graph and the reasoning are the same ones.
Binding
A control binds when it forbids the price the market would otherwise reach.In real lifeA speed limit of 200 km/h on a road nobody can drive faster than 120 on. It is a real law and it changes nothing.A price control binds only when it stands between the market and its equilibrium: a ceiling below equilibrium, a floor above it. A non-binding control leaves price, quantity, consumer surplus, producer surplus and deadweight loss all exactly where they were. The habit that earns the mark is to find equilibrium first and compare the control against it, rather than reading a shortage off the word "ceiling".
Quantity control
A cap on how much may be traded, rather than on what it may cost.In real lifeA city that issues a fixed number of taxi licences. The fare is free to move; the number of cabs is not.Governments regulate quantities as well as prices - licences, permits, catch limits, import quotas. The effect is the mirror image of a price control: the quantity is held below the equilibrium quantity and the price then rises to whatever buyers will pay for the restricted amount. Like a price control in an efficient market it destroys surplus, because trades worth more to buyers than they cost sellers are prevented from happening.

The three-step habit that answers every control question
  1. Find equilibrium first. Not the control - the equilibrium. Everything is measured against it.
  2. Ask whether the control binds. A ceiling binds below equilibrium; a floor binds above. If it does not bind, the answer is “no change” and you are finished.
  3. Read the SHORT side. The quantity traded is the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied, and it is always less than the equilibrium quantity when the control binds.
Trap — a helpful-sounding policy that reduces the quantity A price ceiling is meant to make something affordable, and it leaves less of it being bought than before. Worked example — a rent ceiling, step by step A town rents 700 apartments at $900 a month in equilibrium. A ceiling is set at $700.
Does it bind? $700 is below the $900 equilibrium, so yes.
What happens to each side? At $700 more tenants want a flat, so quantity demanded rises above 700. Fewer landlords are willing to let, so quantity supplied falls below 700.
How many are rented? The short side: the quantity supplied, which is fewer than 700. More people want a flat than before and fewer of them get one.
And who decides which tenants? Not the price, because the price is no longer allowed to do it - so queues, waiting lists and personal connections do it instead.

Taxes, subsidies and who really pays

A tax and a subsidy are the same object with opposite signs, and both drive a wedge between the price a buyer pays and the price a seller keeps. Where that wedge comes from decides everything else.

Per-unit tax
A fixed amount charged on each unit sold. It drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep.also called an excise tax, a specific tax or a per-unit sales taxThe exam uses “excise tax” and “per-unit tax” interchangeably in the same paper. Both mean a fixed amount per unit, which is what makes the supply shift vertical and measurable - as opposed to a percentage tax, which is not on this course.In real lifeA 20-cent tax on a gallon of gasoline. The pump price goes up, usually by less than 20 cents, and the rest comes out of the seller.A tax of a fixed amount on every unit traded. On a supply-and-demand graph it moves supply vertically upward by exactly the tax, because at every quantity sellers now need the old price plus the tax before they will supply. Everything else follows from that one move: the quantity traded falls, buyers pay a higher price, sellers keep a lower one, and the vertical gap between those two prices is the tax. Drawing it as a vague leftward shift is the examined error, because the wedge can then no longer be measured.
On the exam

The figure shows a per-unit tax imposed on a competitive market. Which of the following correctly describes the two new prices marked on the vertical axis, the higher one and the lower one?

  1. AThe upper one is what sellers keep and the lower one is what buyers pay; the gap between them is the government’s total revenue.
  2. BThe upper one is what buyers pay and the lower one is what sellers keep; the gap between them is the tax per unit.
  3. CBoth are prices buyers pay, before and after the tax; the gap between them is the deadweight loss.
  4. DBoth are prices sellers keep, before and after the tax; the gap between them is the fall in quantity.
Answer

B. The tax opens a vertical wedge at the new quantity: buyers pay the higher price, read off the demand curve, and sellers keep the lower price, read off the original supply curve, so the distance between the two is exactly the per-unit tax. Government revenue is that wedge multiplied by the quantity still traded, so it is an area rather than a distance, and the deadweight loss is the triangle to the right of it.

Subsidy
A per-unit payment from the government. The same wedge, the other way up.In real lifeA payment to installers for every rooftop solar panel fitted. The household pays less than the job costs, the installer receives more than the household paid, and the state pays the difference on every panel.A per-unit payment that moves supply vertically downward by exactly the subsidy. The quantity traded rises, buyers pay a lower price, sellers receive a higher one, and the gap between those prices is what the government pays on each unit. Governments use taxes and subsidies to change incentives and so to influence what consumers and producers do, shifting the supply and demand curves accordingly - and in a market that was already efficient a subsidy destroys surplus exactly as a tax does, by pushing output past the point where the last unit is worth what it costs.
Tax revenue
The per-unit amount times the quantity STILL traded. An area, not a line.In real lifeA tax that raises a price far enough to drive half the buyers away collects on the half that remain, not on the market as it used to be.Taxes and subsidies affect government revenues or costs, and the arithmetic is one rectangle either way: the per-unit amount multiplied by the quantity traded after the policy. The examined mistake is to multiply by the original quantity - the tax is collected only on units actually sold, and the tax itself is what reduced that number. On the graph the rectangle sits between the two prices out to the new quantity, with the deadweight-loss triangle immediately to its right.
Tax incidence
Who actually bears a tax - which has nothing to do with who hands over the money.also called the burden of the tax, the tax burden or who bears the taxA stem asking “on whom does the burden fall” is asking for incidence, and the answer is always the less elastic side - never the side named as paying it over.In real lifeA tax collected from petrol stations still turns up at the pump. Whether it turns up in full is decided by how easily drivers can do without, not by whose name is on the tax form.The division of a tax between buyers and sellers. The rule is short and it is examined directly: the incidence of taxes and subsidies falls more heavily on whichever side is less elastic, because the side that cannot easily walk away has no way to push the charge onto the other. Two things follow. Which side legally remits the tax makes no difference to the answer - the graph looks different, the split does not. And at the limit, with perfectly inelastic demand, buyers bear all of it and the quantity traded does not move at all.
On the exam

A per-unit tax is imposed on a competitive market in which demand is relatively inelastic and supply relatively elastic, as in the figure. Which of the following is true of the burden of the tax?

  1. ASellers bear more of it, because the tax is collected from sellers.
  2. BBuyers bear more of it, because they respond less to the price change.
  3. CBuyers and sellers bear half each, whatever the elasticities.
  4. DSellers bear all of it, because the supply curve is the curve that moved.
Answer

B. A tax falls more heavily on the less elastic side. Inelastic buyers keep buying at a higher price rather than going without, so sellers can pass most of the tax along, while elastic sellers would cut output sharply rather than absorb it. Who remits the tax, and which curve is drawn as moving, make no difference to the split.

Deadweight loss
Surplus that nobody gets: the trades an intervention prevented.also called excess burden, welfare loss or efficiency loss“Excess burden” is the phrase used about taxes in particular: the loss over and above the money actually collected. Same triangle.In real lifeA sale that would have suited the buyer and the seller alike, and simply does not happen. Nobody pockets the difference, because the difference was never made.The losses to buyers and sellers when government intervention in an efficient market stops trades that were worth more to buyers than they cost sellers. It is surplus destroyed rather than transferred, which is what separates it from the tax revenue sitting next to it on the same graph: revenue moves surplus from the market to the government, deadweight loss moves it nowhere. On the graph it is the triangle between the demand and supply curves over the quantity range the policy removed.
Draw it as a vertical move, always A per-unit tax shifts supply UP by exactly the tax. A per-unit subsidy shifts it DOWN by exactly the subsidy. Sellers need the old price plus the tax before they will supply any given quantity, so the move is vertical and its size is known. Draw it as a vague leftward shift and the wedge stops being measurable, which loses the incidence, the revenue and the deadweight loss in one stroke — three answers thrown away by one careless line.

Why intervention costs efficiency, and when it does not

The qualifier at the front of the rule Taxes, subsidies, price controls and quantity controls can only reduce allocative efficiency in a market that was already at the efficient quantity. That condition is the whole statement, and it is at the front of the sentence for a reason: it says the market had nothing wrong with it before the policy arrived. Read without it, this rule becomes “government intervention is always harmful”, which is the reading Unit 6 spends a topic undoing — in a market with an externality, the very same tax raises total surplus.
Worked example — a $2 tax, read off the numbers Before the tax a market clears at $3 and 30 units. A $2 per-unit tax is imposed, and the market now clears at 20 units with buyers paying $4.
What sellers keep: $4 minus the $2 tax = $2.
Incidence: buyers pay $1 more than before, sellers keep $1 less. An even split, so the two sides are about equally elastic here.
Government revenue: $2 times the 20 units still traded = $40. Not 30 units - those ten trades no longer happen, so nothing is collected on them.
Deadweight loss: the triangle over those ten lost units, one half times 10 times the $2 wedge = $10.

2.9 International trade and public policy

One new object, and it is a horizontal line. Everything in this topic follows from which side of the domestic equilibrium that line falls on.

Autarky
No trade with anybody. The domestic market on its own.also called the no-trade equilibrium, a closed economy or the domestic equilibrium before tradeDo not confuse it with autarchy, which is a form of government. In a trade question “before trade” and “under autarky” mean the same thing, and the exam uses both.In real lifeA country that grew all its own rice before its ports opened. The price it paid was whatever its own farmers and its own shoppers settled on between them.An economy closed to international trade, so its price and quantity are set purely by domestic supply and domestic demand. It is the baseline every trade question is measured against: opening an economy to trade may leave the price higher or lower than it was under autarky, depending on where the world price sits, and the gap between what domestic buyers want and what domestic sellers offer at the new price is filled by trade. Consumer surplus, producer surplus and total economic surplus all change, and total surplus rises.
World price
The price a small country can buy or sell any quantity at. A horizontal line.In real lifeA small country’s buyers can get all the wheat they want at the going world price, and nothing they do moves that price.The price prevailing on the world market, faced by a country too small to affect it. Drawn as a horizontal line across the domestic supply-and-demand graph, and the whole question is which side of the domestic equilibrium it falls on. Below the domestic equilibrium price, domestic buyers want more than domestic sellers will supply and the gap is imported. Above it, domestic sellers want to supply more than domestic buyers will take and the gap is exported. A tariff raises that line; it does not move the domestic supply curve.
Imports
The gap between what buyers here want and what sellers here supply, when the world price is lower.In real lifeAt a world price of $3 a country’s shoppers want 12 million pounds of rice and its own farms grow 4 million. The other 8 million arrives by ship.What is bought from abroad when the world price sits below the domestic equilibrium price. Read it off the graph as the horizontal distance between the two domestic curves at the world price. Opening to imports lowers the domestic price, so domestic consumer surplus rises, domestic producer surplus falls, domestic production falls, and total surplus rises - the gain to buyers is larger than the loss to sellers, which is the whole argument for trade and also why the losers are the ones who lobby.
Exports
The same gap the other way, when the world price is higher.In real lifeAt a world price above what local buyers had been paying, a country’s growers supply 80 units while its own shoppers take 50. The 30 in between are sold abroad.What is sold abroad when the world price sits above the domestic equilibrium price. The domestic price rises to the world price, so domestic producer surplus rises, domestic consumer surplus falls, domestic consumption falls, and total surplus rises again. Every sign is the mirror of the import case, which is why the exam can ask either one and why reading the graph beats memorising a list.
Tariff
A tax on imports. It raises the price at which foreign goods are available.In real lifeA duty on imported cheese. The imported cheese on the shelf costs more, the local dairy sells more at the higher price, and the shopper goes home with less cheese than before.A tax governments sometimes use to influence international trade, charged on imported goods. It lifts the horizontal world-price line by the amount of the tariff, and the rest follows: the domestic price rises, domestic quantity supplied rises, domestic quantity demanded falls, imports shrink to the smaller gap between the two, and the government collects government revenue equal to the tariff multiplied by the quantity still imported. Consumer surplus falls by more than producers and the government together gain, and the difference is deadweight loss - two triangles, one on each side of the revenue rectangle. A tariff does not shift the domestic supply curve, and drawing it that way is the error the rubric catches.
Quota
A legal cap on the quantity that may be imported.In real lifeA country that will admit no more than a set tonnage of sugar a year, however many buyers want it.A limit on the quantity imported rather than a charge on it. Quotas can be used to alter the quantities produced, and through that they affect price, consumer surplus and total economic surplus: less comes in, the domestic price rises, domestic production rises and domestic consumption falls, exactly as under a tariff. The one difference the exam tests is where the money goes. A tariff raises revenue for the government; a quota raises none, and the same gap between the world price and the higher domestic price is earned instead by whoever holds the right to import.

Worked example — reading trade off a graph Domestic supply and demand cross at $9. The world price is $7, and at $7 the domestic supply curve gives 2 units while the domestic demand curve gives 6.

The card to memorize

Formulas

  • Any elasticity: % change in a quantity ÷ % change in what moved it
  • Price elasticity of demand: %∆Qd ÷ %∆P, compared as a magnitude
  • Price elasticity of supply: %∆Qs ÷ %∆P, already positive
  • Income elasticity: %∆Qd ÷ %∆income — sign gives normal or inferior
  • Cross-price elasticity: %∆Qd of one ÷ %∆P of another — sign gives the relationship
  • Midpoint: divide each change by the average of the two values
  • Total revenue: P × Q
  • Consumer surplus: ½ × base × height, under demand and above the price
  • Tax or tariff revenue: per-unit amount × the quantity STILL traded
  • Deadweight loss: ½ × the wedge × the quantity lost

The traps, in one list

  • Own price moves you along; everything else moves the curve
  • Slope is not elasticity — one straight line holds all three verdicts
  • A negative price elasticity is the law of demand, not an error
  • On income and cross-price elasticity the sign IS the answer
  • A cross-price elasticity of zero means unrelated, not miscalculated
  • The quantity traded is the SHORT side of the market
  • A non-binding control does nothing at all
  • Both binding controls REDUCE the quantity traded
  • Incidence follows elasticity, never who remits the tax
  • Revenue uses the new quantity, never the old one
  • Two curves moving means one reading cannot be determined
  • A tariff raises the world-price line; it does not shift domestic supply
